My 10 focus habits

Fewer interruptions and a calmer head. For each habit: the exact setting, the number from the research and why it works.

Most entrepreneurs I talk to get interrupted all day: notifications, chats, email and loose tasks in their head. By the end of the day, the most important work is still waiting. I know it from myself: I used to drink more than eight cups of coffee a day and react to everything. That's why I build systems that do the work for you, so you need less willpower. This page starts with what almost nobody knows, then my own stack, then the 10 habits.

What almost nobody knows

Five findings you rarely hear, from research and from my own data, with what to do about them.

2.8 sec

A 2.8-second interruption doubled the number of mistakes.

People did a task in a fixed order of steps. After an interruption of 2.8 seconds on average, they lost track of which step came next twice as often. After 4.4 seconds, it was three times as often. So during focused work, only let through people who are truly urgent (habit 3).

Experiment from 2014

1 alert

Just getting a notification cost about as much attention as calling or texting.

A notification is a signal from your phone: a sound, a buzz or a red dot. People didn't touch their phone, yet they did worse on an attention task when notifications came in. Keep notifications only from people who need you right away (habit 1).

Experiment from 2015

57%

Fewer app openings after 6 weeks with the one sec app.

one sec makes you wait a moment before an app like Instagram opens, and gives you a button to back out. People closed the app straight away 36% of the time. One of the researchers created one sec.

Study of 280 people

4

Your mind holds only about 4 things at once.

Your working memory is your mind's notepad: where you hold something for a moment while you think. The well-known estimate was 7 things. Newer research puts it at about 4. So every task you try to remember takes up a big share of that space. Put it in one list (habit 7).

Review of the research

86 → 152 ms

My HRV after I went from about 800 to 30 to 60 mg of caffeine a day.

HRV (heart rate variability) is the small variation in time between your heartbeats, in milliseconds (ms). A higher HRV usually means your body has recovered well; it drops after too little sleep, alcohol or stress. I changed more than just my caffeine in that time, so I don't know which part did what. Changing something yourself? Track your HRV for a few weeks before and after.

Part 1

My focus stack

What I use every day. You can set up most of it today.

On my phone and at my desk

The one sec app

Before an app like Instagram opens, one sec makes me wait a few seconds and take a breath. Often I close it again (habit 6).

Messages at 8.00, 12.00 and 18.00

In between, my chats and email stay closed. In the morning I clear my chats first, so my team can get on (habit 4).

Two numbers on one phone

With an eSIM, a digital SIM card, you can have two numbers on one phone. Only my family and close friends have my personal number. A call on that number is always important (habit 3).

One list for every task

Every task and idea goes into Todoist within a few seconds, with a shortcut (habit 7). Then I plan them into my calendar with Akiflow.

Noise-cancelling headphones

Headphones that filter out the sound around you. I often use binaural beats: two slightly different tones, one in each ear. Other options are silence or brown noise, a low and even rumble. The idea that binaural beats tune your brainwaves is shaky: of 14 studies, 5 found that effect and 8 didn't.

A walking pad under my desk

With a 10 kg weighted vest I get 30,000 to 40,000 steps a day. My resting heart rate (how often your heart beats per minute when you sit still) went from 53 to 37 in 12 months. I changed more than one thing.

Light therapy glasses

Glasses that shine bright light into your eyes, for dark mornings (habit 9).

A wearable

A ring or watch that tracks your sleep and heart rate. Every morning I first write down how I think I slept, then I check my HRV.

From eight cups of coffee to almost nothing

I used to drink more than eight cups of coffee a day, about 800 mg of caffeine. Now I take 30 to 60 mg a day, from capsules. For me, 150 mg is already too much.

  1. I switched to decaf. Decaf isn't completely free of caffeine: in one study, a large cup (about half a litre) had up to 14 mg and a shot of espresso up to 16 mg. Drink a lot of it? Then count it.
  2. I take my caffeine as a capsule, so I know exactly how much it is.
  3. For comparison: EFSA, the European food safety authority, sees no safety concern for healthy adults up to 200 mg at once and 400 mg a day. Pregnant or breastfeeding? Then the limit is 200 mg a day.
  4. Cutting down? Do it step by step. When people stopped all at once, half of them got a headache. The symptoms started after 12 to 24 hours and lasted 2 to 9 days, sometimes even in people used to just 100 mg a day.

You can see what it did to my HRV at the top. When to take your last caffeine is in habit 8.

My supplements for focus

This is what I take myself, with my dose. Next to it: the official limit and what the research says. What you need shows up in your blood and your data.

  1. L-theanine, an amino acid from tea, together with my caffeine: 60 to 120 mg of L-theanine with 30 to 60 mg of caffeine. An amino acid is a building block of protein. There's no official upper limit. In 2011 EFSA approved no health claim for L-theanine and thinking or relaxation. In a small study by Unilever, a tea maker, with 27 people, 50 mg of caffeine with 100 mg of L-theanine gave faster and more accurate performance on a task where you switch between things, and less distraction.
  2. CDP-choline (citicoline), a substance your body breaks down into, among other things, choline: a building block for your brain cells. I take 500 mg a day. That's also the most a supplement may give per day in the EU. It isn't meant for children and can interact with some medicines. In 2024, EFSA found no proven effect on memory. Only one study in healthy people found an effect, at 500 mg a day for 12 weeks. A study with 1 gram a day found none.
  3. A B complex, one pill with the B vitamins, such as B6, B12 and folic acid. I take 1 capsule a day of a 'methylated' one: some of the B vitamins in it are already in the form your body uses. Check how much B6 is in yours. EFSA set the safe upper limit for adults at 12 mg of B6 a day. Too much B6 for a long time can damage your nerves: tingling, numbness or pain in your hands and feet. Lareb, the Dutch centre for side effects, also had reports from people taking 12 mg or less. And in 16 studies with 6,276 people, B12 on its own or in a B complex didn't help thinking or mood in people without a clear deficiency. Whether you're low shows up in your blood.
  4. Bromantane. I use it for more mental drive during hard thinking work. In Russia it's a medicine for persistent fatigue. In the EU it isn't an approved medicine or supplement, and it's on the doping list. The research comes almost entirely from Russia. In one large study all 728 participants with persistent fatigue got the drug, and most felt better after 4 weeks. There was no group without the drug, so you can't tell how much of that came from the drug. In healthy volunteers, a single dose helped with mental fatigue. In rats it increases the production of dopamine, a chemical messenger in your brain.

Never start without data. The wrong supplement can make symptoms worse. Measure what you need first. Taking any medication, or pregnant? Talk to your GP first. Compete in sport? Bromantane is on the doping list.

Part 2

Your phone

Fewer triggers that pull your attention away.

1. Turn off non-essential notifications

Just getting a notification costs attention (see the top). Turning everything off didn't work best either. In a study of 237 people, those with no notifications at all became more anxious and more afraid of missing out. Those who got them in 3 batches a day felt more attentive and less stressed.

  1. Keep notifications only from people and apps that need you right away.
  2. Bundle the rest. iPhone: Settings > Notifications > Scheduled Summary, at 8.00, 12.00 and 18.00.
  3. Turn off the red dots: Notifications > pick an app > Badges.

Dopamine is the chemical messenger in your brain behind the 'I want' feeling. In monkeys it built up most when a reward had a 50% chance, as uncertain as it gets. A notification is that kind of uncertain reward, though this hasn't been measured in people with their phones.

2. Keep your chats tidy with the archive

An endless chat list is full of open loops: things that aren't finished and keep running in the back of your mind. This is how to turn it into an inbox:

  1. WhatsApp: archive all your chats at once (Settings > Chats, on Android under Chat history).
  2. Turn 'Keep chats archived' OFF there. Then a new message brings the chat back into your list by itself. With it on, that message stays in the archive, usually without a notification.
  3. Done with a chat? Archive it.
  4. Email: set a filter that archives anything containing 'unsubscribe' automatically.
  5. Slack: open Unreads (⌘ Shift A on a Mac, Ctrl Shift A on Windows). Esc marks a conversation as read, so you work through them one by one. Don't see Unreads? Switch it on in your sidebar settings.

A 2026 meta-analysis (a study that pools many studies) of thousands of workers found that those with more unfinished work thought about work more often in their free time. That's a link. Whether an empty chat list improves your focus hasn't been studied.

3. Decide who can reach you

A focus mode is a setting on your phone that holds back calls and notifications, except from the people you choose. An interruption of 2.8 seconds already doubled the number of mistakes (see the top).

  1. iPhone: Settings > Focus > pick a focus > People. Android: 'Do not disturb' > People.
  2. Pick only your family and your most important contacts. On iPhone, turn on 'Repeated Calls': anyone who calls again within 3 minutes gets through.
  3. Let the focus turn on by itself at set times, for example during your deep work (habit 5).
  4. Two numbers on one phone (see my stack)? Give your personal number only to family and close friends. Then a call on that number is always worth answering.
  5. Say in your voicemail when you call back, for example between 13.00 and 18.00.
  6. Use WhatsApp Business? Set an away message with the same times: Tools > Away message > Schedule.

Part 3

Your workday

Set times, one task at a time and a clear head.

4. Check messages and email at set times

Every check is a task switch. Your mind has to let go of one task and pick up the other, and part of your attention often stays stuck on what you were just doing. That's called attention residue.

  1. Pick 3 set times. Mine are 8.00, 12.00 and 18.00.
  2. Then go through WhatsApp, Slack and email until nothing is left open, with a fixed end time. Close everything after.
  3. Reply to everyone in that round, even if it's just: 'Got it, it's in my system.' Then put the task on your list (habit 7). People feel heard, and you don't have to remember it.
  4. Agree with your team: calling is for urgent things, messages are for the rest. What I notice: once people know you reply at set times, their messages get clearer.

In 124 adults, a week of checking email 3 times a day (in practice about 5 times) gave less daily stress than a week of checking whenever they liked (about 13 times). In two experiments, finishing a task under some time pressure left less attention behind on it; that's what the end time is for. After a task switch you're slower and make more mistakes. Preparing makes that smaller.

5. Start your day with your most important task

Deep work is work that needs all of your attention, like a strategy or a difficult proposal. For most entrepreneurs I recommend 9 to 12 for it.

  1. Put that task in your calendar the evening before, as a fixed block.
  2. Fewer than 3 people on your team? Then start your deep work straight away. A bigger team? Do a short inbox round first, like I do, so your team can get on. Then close everything.
  3. Block your chat apps on your laptop until 12.00, on a fixed schedule, with an app blocker such as 1Focus (Mac) or Freedom (Windows). Keep email open, so your login codes still come in. Apple's own app limit doesn't help much here: you tap 'Ignore Limit' and you're in.
  4. Split your list into deep work and busy work: small, routine tasks. Deep work goes in the morning. Small tasks, like sending a document, and meetings go in the afternoon.
  5. A true evening person? Put your block later. Your chronotype, whether you're naturally a morning or an evening person, helps decide when you're sharpest.
  6. After hard thinking work, take a break of more than 10 minutes.

In 22 studies, breaks of up to 10 minutes gave a little more energy. They only improved performance on lighter tasks, and the longer the break, the bigger that effect. After demanding work, the researchers say you probably need more than 10 minutes.

6. Make your phone boring

What helps is friction: a small hurdle that gives you the moment to choose.

  1. Put one sec on the apps you open most often for no reason. Pick a wait of a few seconds and set the hours it's on, for example only in the mornings. In the study, the button to back out worked best, and the wait helped too. The message that makes you think did nothing.
  2. Switch your screen to greyscale (black and white): Settings > Accessibility > Display & Text Size > Colour Filters > Greyscale. Don't add a shortcut to switch it off again. The more effort it takes to get colour back, the longer you leave it on.
  3. Hide the apps that pull you in most behind Face ID: hold the app, tap Require Face ID, then Hide and Require Face ID. They move to a locked folder at the bottom of your App Library.
  4. Take your other apps off your home screen (Remove from Home Screen, not Delete App) and search for them when you need them.
  5. Do the same on your laptop: an empty dock and desktop, and open apps by searching, with Spotlight (⌘ Space) or an app like Raycast.

In 112 people, greyscale cut screen time straight away, though their wellbeing didn't change. Keeping your phone out of sight helps less than people think. A well-known 2017 study found that a phone on the desk lowered working memory (what your mind is holding at that moment). A replication with its plan fixed in advance found no difference between desk, bag or another room. A meta-analysis of 22 studies did find a negative effect, with big differences between studies. Putting it away costs nothing, so do it anyway.

7. Get it out of your head

Your working memory, your mind's notepad, holds only about 4 things at once. The famous estimate of 7 turned out to be too generous. So put open tasks somewhere else:

  1. One place for everything, for example an app like Todoist. Prefer talking? An app like SuperWhisper turns your voice into text.
  2. Make adding a task take less than 3 seconds. Any slower, and your mind keeps holding on to it. Also make sure you don't see your old tasks while you add one, or they pull you in.
  3. iPhone: in the Shortcuts app, make a shortcut with Todoist's action to add a task, set so the app doesn't open. Put it on your lock screen or Action Button. Mac: give it a key combination, for example ⌥ A in Raycast.
  4. For each task, write down what, when and where. Only then does your mind let it go.
  5. Once a day, plan the tasks from your list into your calendar. I use Akiflow for that.
  6. Before bed, spend 5 minutes writing your to-do list for the next few days, as specific as you can.

In experiments, unfinished goals kept running through people's heads until they made a specific plan for them. In a sleep lab, 57 young adults fell asleep faster after 5 minutes of writing a to-do list than after writing down what they had already done. The more specific the list, the faster.

Part 4

Your body

Sleep, light and movement: the base under your focus.

8. Stop caffeine 8 to 9 hours before bed

Adenosine is a substance that builds up in your brain during the day and makes you sleepy. Caffeine blocks the spot where adenosine attaches, and if you take it too late, it's still doing that at night.

  1. A cup of coffee (about 100 mg): at least 8.8 hours before bed. Asleep at 23.00? Then 14.00 is your last cup.
  2. A standard serving of pre-workout, a training powder (about 220 mg): at least 13 hours before bed.
  3. Count all of it: tea, cola and energy drinks contain caffeine too, and decaf a little.
  4. Stay under 100 mg a day. I stop 10 hours before bed.

In 24 studies, caffeine cost 45 minutes of sleep on average. In a trial of 23 men, 100 mg had no measurable effect, even 4 hours before bed. 400 mg still disturbed sleep when taken 12 hours before bed, and at 8 and 12 hours the men didn't notice it themselves.

9. Get daylight in the morning

Your internal clock, a rhythm of roughly 24 hours in your body, decides when you're alert and when you get sleepy. Light in your eyes sets that clock. Yet in a large American survey, people spent 87% of their time inside buildings and another 6% in vehicles.

  1. Go outside as early in the morning as you can.
  2. On dark mornings I wear light therapy glasses.

Office workers who got a lot of morning light fell asleep faster at night, especially in winter. In a Dutch study, people whose first light came later in the day woke up more often at night. Whether light makes you more alert directly is less certain: in 68 studies, people often felt more alert in brighter light, but reaction tests rarely showed it.

10. Walk 10 minutes after eating

After a meal your blood sugar, the amount of sugar in your blood, goes up. Working muscles take up some of that sugar.

  1. Walk for 10 minutes after lunch and after dinner.
  2. Afternoon dip? Walk briskly for 10 minutes before you reach for something sweet.
  3. Sitting a lot? Break it up with short bits of walking. That's why I have a walking pad under my desk.

In 41 people with type 2 diabetes, walking 10 minutes after each meal gave 12% lower blood sugar than one 30-minute walk a day, and 22% after dinner. Walking breaks worked better than just standing up. In 18 people, a brisk 10-minute walk gave more energy for 2 hours, while a sweet snack gave more tiredness an hour later. The effect of a single bout of exercise on your thinking is small (79 studies).

Seeing what works: your sleep and HRV

How you feel is a poor guide. In a sleep lab, people who spent 6 hours a night in bed for 2 weeks did as badly on attention tests as after up to 2 nights without sleep. They hardly noticed it themselves. A wearable, a ring or watch you wear at night, shows how long you sleep and what your HRV does.

  1. In the morning, first write down how you think you slept, and only then look at your numbers. I do this every morning.
  2. Look at your trend over weeks rather than at a single night.
  3. Put your weaker focus days next to your sleep and HRV. That shows what makes the difference for you, like late caffeine.

In a sleep lab, six well-known wearables were right 86 to 89% of the time about whether someone was asleep or awake. For sleep stages, such as deep sleep and dream sleep, that was 50 to 65%. So focus mostly on how long you sleep. In 20 studies, lower HRV went together with worse performance on thinking tasks, though the number of studies is small.
