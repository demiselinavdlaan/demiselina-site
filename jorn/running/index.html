Run without feeling wrecked: my zone 2 plan

Find your zone 2 in two minutes, build up over 8 weeks, and see why your heart rate shoots up on an easy run. With the research behind every step.

If you feel wrecked after a run, your heart rate was too high, and the run was mostly extra stress for your body. The classic mistake when you start is running too fast. You want your heart rate so steady that it feels like you could go on endlessly. If you finish and think 'I could just keep going', you've got it right. Of everything I've tried, running lowered my stress the most. These days I train at least 240 minutes a week in zone 2, an easy pace at which you can still talk, and I'm training for a 50 km ultra in Dubai. Zone 2 still feels stupid: it's not pushing at all, and even for me it takes getting used to. This page starts with what almost nobody knows. Then you find your zone 2, get an 8-week plan and see why your heart rate does what it does. Under every step is the research, including where it's weak.

What almost nobody knows

Five findings about easy running, each with a number and what to do with it.

60-90%

Where easy turns into hard differs hugely from person to person

Your maximum heart rate is the highest your heart can beat when you give everything. Your first threshold is the point where easy running turns into harder work: your breathing speeds up and lactate starts to build up in your blood, a substance your muscles make when they burn sugar fast. In 100 adults tested in a lab, that threshold sat anywhere between 60 and 90% of their maximum heart rate. So a percentage from a formula is only a start. Check it with the talk test below.

Lab study of 100 adults

17.8 vs 7.7

Beginners get injured more than twice as often per hour of running

Across 13 studies, beginners had 17.8 injuries per 1,000 hours of running, against 7.7 in runners with more experience. An injury here means pain in your legs or back that keeps you from running. The studies used different definitions, so don't take the exact numbers too literally. The direction is clear: build up slowly in your first weeks, even when it feels too easy.

Review of 13 studies

19%

In the heat, heart rate rose 19% in half an hour at the same pace

Cardiac drift is the slow rise of your heart rate during a run while your pace stays the same. At 35 °C, 7 active men ran at a steady, moderate pace. Between minute 15 and minute 45 their heart rate went up 19%, while their heart pumped less blood per beat. The researchers concluded the rise was real extra strain: the same pace cost a bigger share of what their bodies could do. So on warm days, slow down or walk as soon as your heart rate climbs above zone 2.

Lab study, 7 men at 35 °C

26

In 26 studies, caffeine left heart rate the same and made the effort feel lighter

People took 3 to 6 mg of caffeine per kilo of body weight, 30 to 90 minutes before a steady effort. For someone of 70 kg that's 210 to 420 mg, roughly 2 to 4 cups of coffee. Their heart rate was the same as with a placebo, a dummy pill. The effort did feel lighter. So after a coffee you can drift above zone 2 without noticing. Keep doing the talk test.

Part 1

Your zone 2 and your first 8 weeks

Find your zone in two minutes, then build up in minutes.

Find your zone 2 in two minutes

Heart rate zones split your effort into five bands, from very easy (zone 1) to everything you've got (zone 5). Zone 2 is the second band: think of a calm jog or an easy bike ride, below your first threshold. The way I put it: you can still just about talk, but you'd rather not. That's the top of your zone 2. Where it lies is personal. You find it in three steps, and the talk test has the last word.

  1. The talk test. While you run, say two or three full sentences out loud, such as your address and what you did today. Comfortable? Then you're in zone 2 or below. Do you have to stop mid-sentence to breathe? You're going too fast. Slow down or walk until you can talk again.
  2. The formula. Your maximum heart rate is about 208 minus 0.7 times your age. As a careful start, take 60 to 70% of that as your zone 2. At 30, that's 208 minus 21 = 187, so zone 2 is about 112 to 131. Find your age in the table below.
  3. Your wearable. Enter your age and run with your heart rate on screen. Do the talk test at the top of your zone. If the two disagree, go by the talk test, and adjust your zones in the app if it lets you.

The talk test is well researched. In 16 healthy adults, the moment speech first became hard matched the ventilatory threshold almost exactly: the point where your breathing starts to rise faster than your effort. That held on a treadmill and on a bike. A review calls the talk test valid, reliable and cheap, from athletes to heart patients. The formula comes from 351 studies with 18,712 people: maximum heart rate was about 208 minus 0.7 times age, the same for men and women. The well-known 220 minus your age puts the maximum too low for older adults. The table is a careful start: in the study of 100 adults, the first threshold sat between 60 and 90% of maximum, so 60 to 70% keeps you at the low end. On average, zone 2 sits somewhere around 120 to 140 beats a minute, as I tell clients. Yours can be lower or higher. If the talk test is still easy at the top of your zone, you can go a few beats higher.

Your first 8 weeks: three times a week, in minutes

Run three times a week, with a day off in between. Count in minutes, not kilometres, so you're not tempted to speed up to finish a distance. Walking breaks are part of the plan, and every minute stays in zone 2: if the talk test fails, walk until you can talk again. Start each session with 5 minutes of brisk walking; those don't count. In the beginning your heart rate jumps all over the place, and that's fine: you're training your heart. After about 4 to 8 weeks it gets more stable and you can go longer. Three times 30 minutes a week is the sweet spot I give for VO2max, the most oxygen your body can use per minute when you give everything. In week 8 you run three times 40 minutes: the 120 minutes a week in zone 2 that I advise.

Repeat the week. Missed a week? Go back one.

Why so slowly? Across 13 studies, beginners got injured more than twice as often per hour of running as experienced runners. In 874 Danish beginners with a GPS watch, those who ran more than 30% further over two weeks seemed to get more overuse injuries, such as runner's knee (pain around the kneecap). That study was exploratory, so it's no proof. A slower plan isn't a shield on its own either: in a Dutch trial with 532 beginners, about 1 in 5 got injured on a 13-week plan and on an 8-week plan alike. Walking breaks cost you little: in a marathon, 42 amateur runners finished in about the same time with or without walking breaks, and the run-walk group had less muscle pain and fatigue afterwards. And you don't need much: in 55,137 adults followed for 15 years on average, runners had a 30% lower risk of dying than non-runners, even at 5 to 10 minutes a day at a slow pace. That's a link, not proof that running is the cause.

Careful. Pain that changes the way you run, chest pain, dizziness or unusual shortness of breath: stop. Haven't exercised in years, or do you have a heart condition? Check with your GP before you start.

After week 8: lots of easy, a little very hard

Zone 3 is the band just above zone 2. It feels like work and it costs a lot of strain, yet in my view your body adapts little to it: little extra for your fitness, a lot of extra stress. So ideally you train a lot in zone 2, or very hard, and not much in between. That's how I do it: most of my week in zone 2, and once a week 4x4 intervals.

  1. 4 minutes hard: 90 to 95% of your maximum heart rate.
  2. 3 minutes of easy jogging, at about 70%.
  3. Do that four times, once a week, and only after your first 8 weeks.

Top endurance athletes train like this. In 11 national-level junior cross-country skiers, about 75% of sessions stayed below the first threshold, 8% were in between and 17% were hard. In a trial with 48 well-trained endurance athletes, this mix raised VO2max by 11.7% in 9 weeks; training mostly in the middle added nothing measurable. Across 17 studies the picture is more modest: the mix was slightly better for VO2max, mainly in highly trained athletes, and equal for race times. In 40 moderately trained men, 8 weeks of 4x4 intervals raised VO2max by 7%, more than the same amount of work at an easy pace or at threshold.

Careful. Hard intervals put real strain on your heart. Do you have chest pain, dizziness or a heart condition? Check with your GP first.

Why your heart rate shoots up

What's pushing your heart rate up?

Think of your last few easy runs and choose no, sometimes or often for each question. Each answer counts towards one or two causes.

  1. Your heart rate keeps climbing the longer you run, at the same pace.

  2. You run on warm days or in the middle of the day.

  3. You drink little in the hours before a run.

  4. You slept less than 7 hours the night before.

  5. Your easy pace feels harder than it did last week.

  6. You run after a long, busy working day.

  7. You find it hard to wind down in the evening.

  8. You have coffee, an energy drink or pre-workout (gym powder with caffeine) in the 2 hours before you run.

  9. You try to hold a set pace per kilometre.

  10. You can't say a full sentence in your first 10 minutes.

Heat: your heart rate climbs while your pace stays the same

As your body heats up and you lose fluid by sweating, your heart rate goes up and your heart pumps less blood per beat. That's the cardiac drift from above, and it usually starts after 10 to 20 minutes.

  1. Run on heart rate, not on pace. When you go above your zone 2, slow down or walk.
  2. On warm days, run early in the morning or in the evening, in the shade if you can.
  3. Start well hydrated, and take water with you on long runs.

In the study with the 19% above, the researchers concluded that the same pace now cost a bigger share of what the men's bodies could do. So your heart rate gives an honest picture of the strain, and slowing down to stay in zone 2 makes sense. In 8 trained cyclists who rode for 2 hours at 33 °C, the more fluid they lost, the higher their heart rate and body temperature rose. A review names heat, fluid loss, effort and the temperature around you as the main drivers.

After a short night or a busy day, the same pace feels harder

Perceived effort is how hard a run feels to you; researchers ask people to score it on a scale. After a short night or a day of hard thinking it goes up, even when your heart rate stays the same. If you then push to hold your usual pace, you come home wrecked. You want to keep your heart rate fairly steady, so it feels like you could go on endlessly.

  1. After a night of under 6 hours: keep it short and easy, or walk. If you can, run in the morning.
  2. After a mentally heavy day: go by the talk test and let go of the distance you had in mind.
  3. Check your HRV in the morning. Clearly lower than your weekly average? Make it an easy day.

Across 69 studies, sleep loss (6 hours or less) lowered performance by about 7.6% on average. Afternoon and evening tasks suffered most; morning tasks were largely unaffected. In an older study, 8 people walked hard on a treadmill after 36 hours without sleep: they stopped 11% sooner and the effort felt heavier, while their heart rate was the same. In 16 people, 90 minutes of demanding mental work made them stop cycling 15% sooner, with much the same heart rate; they rated the effort as harder. Both show up in your HRV: in 11 studies, sleep deprivation lowered RMSSD, the HRV measure many wearables show, and in most of 37 studies HRV changed with stress.

Caffeine makes the same pace feel lighter

Caffeine is the stimulant in coffee, tea, cola, energy drinks and pre-workout. Before a run it makes the effort feel lighter, while your heart rate stays about the same. So with caffeine in you, you can drift out of zone 2 without feeling it. Watch your heart rate and keep doing the talk test.

In the 26 studies above, heart rate didn't change. Perceived effort went down, and breathing and lactate went up a little. I take 30 to 60 mg a day myself. I advise staying under 100 mg a day, about one cup of coffee, with your last caffeine 10 hours before bed.

Why it gets easier after 4 to 8 weeks

In the beginning your heart rate shoots up quickly, even at a slow pace. That's normal: you're training your heart. Endurance training increases the amount of blood in your body, and that happens in steps.

  1. In the first 2 to 4 weeks, almost all the extra blood is plasma: the fluid part of your blood.
  2. After that, your red blood cells, which carry oxygen, increase as well.
  3. With more blood, your heart fills better and pumps more per beat. In one trial, 8 weeks of intervals raised that by about 10%.
  4. So your heart needs fewer beats for the same pace.

That's why the plan starts with walking breaks: in week 1 even a slow jog can push you out of zone 2. After about 4 to 8 weeks your heart rate gets more stable and you can go longer. You'll see it as a faster pace at the same heart rate.

Part 3

Running and your sleep

When to run if you want deeper sleep.

The best time to run for your sleep

Deep sleep is the stage in which you sleep most deeply and are hardest to wake. I always notice it myself: when I run during the day, my activity is higher and I get a lot more deep sleep. My focus is better, and my mood is often better because I've been outside. The view moving past you while you run relaxes me. At the start it takes getting into. Once it's a habit, it's a huge win.

  • Best for me: during the day and outside, so you get daylight as well.
  • An easy run in zone 2 in the evening is fine.
  • Keep hard sessions out of the last hour before bed.

The research finds a smaller effect than I feel. Across 66 studies, a single session of exercise gave small gains in total sleep and deep sleep, and exercising regularly gave a moderate gain in sleep quality. On timing there's no clear winner: 25 studies comparing morning and evening found no overall advantage for the morning. Evening exercise went with slightly more waking in the night, mostly after hard sessions. Across 23 studies, evening exercise didn't harm sleep; only hard exercise ending less than an hour before bed seemed to. In one small study, 20 adults with slightly raised blood pressure got more deep sleep after 30 minutes on a treadmill at 7 am than at 1 pm. Being outside counts too: in 11 small trials, walking or running outdoors gave more energy and less tension than the same exercise indoors. The results did hint that people felt a little less calm afterwards, and the studies were weak. In over 400,000 people in the UK, each extra hour outdoors during the day went with getting up more easily and feeling tired less often. That's a link, not proof of cause.

Part 4

How you know it's working

What to watch on your wearable, and what each number means.

What to watch on your wearable

A wearable is a ring, watch or band you wear day and night. It measures your heart rate and movement and works out the rest from those. For running, watch three numbers from the night, and one during the run.

  1. Resting heart rate: how many times your heart beats per minute when you're completely still. Many wearables show the lowest point of your night. With weeks of zone 2 it should slowly come down. Is it a few beats above your normal one morning? Make that an easy day.
  2. HRV: the small variation in time between your heartbeats. A higher HRV usually means your body has recovered well; it drops after a short night, alcohol or stress. Compare it with your own weekly average, never with someone else's. Lower than usual? Keep your run short and easy.
  3. Deep sleep: the stage in which you sleep most deeply and are hardest to wake. Treat it as a rough sign: wearables get your sleep stages right only about 50 to 65% of the time. Look at the trend over one to two weeks.
  4. Your heart rate during the run: a sensor on your wrist is less accurate than a chest strap. Does a number look odd? Check it with the talk test.
  5. After 4 to 8 weeks: a faster pace at the same heart rate. That's your heart getting fitter.

Across 191 studies, endurance training lowered resting heart rate, more in people who started higher. Training on HRV (easy when it's low) gave about the same gain in VO2max as a fixed plan across 8 studies, with fewer people whose performance didn't improve. In 53 adults in a sleep lab, six wearables got sleep stages right 50 to 65% of the time. During exercise, a chest strap matched an ECG (a heart trace) almost perfectly in 50 adults; wrist sensors were less accurate, depending on the device and the sport. At night, when you lie still, it's better: in 13 adults over 536 nights, resting heart rate and HRV from the best rings and bands came close to an ECG, and some brands did worse. HRV researchers advise looking at an average over several days. Always discuss an abnormal result with your GP.
