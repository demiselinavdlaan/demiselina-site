What to test for your heart

At 22 I could have had a heart attack. I thought I was healthy.

At 22 my LDL cholesterol was 7 mmol/L. I thought I was healthy. In reality I could have had a heart attack at any moment. This page is what I wish I'd known back then: which markers to test for your heart, what each one tells you, and the three changes that brought my LDL down to 1.4.

What almost nobody knows

Five findings about your heart, each with a number and what to do with it.

>90%

Lp(a) is set by your genes, so one test is enough

Lp(a), lipoprotein(a), is an LDL particle with an extra protein attached. More than 90% of your level comes from your genes, and food and exercise barely change it. About 1 in 5 people have a level above 50 mg/dL, where the European guideline counts it as a risk factor for heart and vascular disease. Your GP doesn't test it as standard. Ask for it once, and keep the result.

European guideline 2025

654,783

ApoB counts the particles that end up in your vessel walls

LDL cholesterol measures how much cholesterol your LDL particles carry. ApoB (apolipoprotein B) is a protein on each of those particles, exactly one per particle, so it counts them. In genetic studies of 654,783 people, heart risk followed ApoB, whether genes lowered LDL or triglycerides (another blood fat). Most of the time LDL and ApoB agree. When they don't, ApoB tells you more. Ask for ApoB with your cholesterol test.

Genetic studies, 654,783 people

54%

1 mmol/L lower LDL for 40 years: 54% less risk

LDL causes heart and vascular disease, and the damage adds up: what counts is how high your LDL is and for how long. In trials, every 1 mmol/L lower LDL cut the risk by about 22% within 5 years. In genetic studies, every 1 mmol/L lower LDL over 40 years went with about 54% lower risk. A newborn's LDL is about 0.5 to 1.0 mmol/L. So look at your LDL in your twenties and thirties, long before you notice anything.

European consensus, 2 million+ people

13%

Each step up in fitness: 13% lower risk of dying

VO2max is the most oxygen your body can use during an all-out effort: the standard measure of fitness. It's often given in METs, a unit of effort; 1 MET more is about 1 km/h faster running pace. Across 33 studies with 102,980 healthy people, every MET more went with a 13% lower risk of dying and 15% fewer heart and vascular events. In a trial, 8 weeks of 4x4 intervals raised VO2max by 7%. Your wearable can estimate yours.

33 studies, 102,980 people

7 mmol/L

My LDL at 22, while I felt healthy

You don't feel a high LDL. You only see it in your blood. Mine was 7 mmol/L at 22. The European guideline counts anyone above 4.9 as high risk on that number alone, whatever else is going on.

At 22, I thought I was healthy

I lived for my work. I drank more than eight coffees a day and ate almost only meat: the carnivore diet. I thought that was healthy. Meanwhile I had panic attacks and felt anxious all the time.

  • Then I had my blood tested. My LDL cholesterol was 7 mmol/L. LDL is the particle that carries cholesterol through your blood, and it can get stuck in the walls of your blood vessels.
  • With an LDL that high, I could have had a heart attack at any moment, at 22. The European guideline puts anyone above 4.9 mmol/L in the high-risk group on that number alone.
  • My data showed me what I was doing to my body. That was the turning point. From then on, I started working with my body.

I started testing regularly. At a later test my LDL was 4.2. With three changes I brought it down to 1.4: more than 50 grams of fibre a day, only lean steak, and 120 minutes of zone 2 training a week. Each change is in part 2. I wasn't the only one: in a survey of 2,029 people on a carnivore diet, the 462 who shared their LDL had a median of 4.5 mmol/L. That survey relied on what people reported themselves.

Part 1

What to test for your heart

What each marker is, how it works and the value to look for. The GP's standard test has LDL, HDL and triglycerides. ApoB, Lp(a) and hs-CRP you ask for separately.

Jorn Kirkels · What to test for your heart

LDL cholesterol

Cholesterol is a fatty substance your body uses to build cells and hormones. It travels through your blood in particles. LDL (low-density lipoprotein) is the main particle that carries it to your tissues. Your LDL cholesterol is how much cholesterol sits in those particles, in mmol/L.

  1. LDL particles are small enough to pass through the inner lining of your blood vessels, into the wall.
  2. The more particles in your blood, the more get stuck there.
  3. Immune cells take up the stuck LDL and fill with cholesterol. That sets off inflammation, and over years a plaque grows: a fatty thickening in the vessel wall.
  4. If the thin cap over a plaque tears, a blood clot forms on it. In an artery of your heart, that's a heart attack.

A European consensus pooled more than 200 studies with over 2 million people: LDL causes heart and vascular disease, and the risk rises with how high your LDL is and how long it stays high. At a newborn's level, about 0.5 to 1.0 mmol/L, little LDL gets stuck. Above that, the chance rises step by step. You don't need to fast for this test: after a normal meal your LDL is on average at most 0.2 mmol/L lower. The European guideline puts anyone with an LDL above 4.9 mmol/L at high risk on that number alone. A European lab consensus advises referral above 5 mmol/L, because it can point to familial hypercholesterolaemia, an inherited form of high cholesterol. Always discuss an abnormal result with your GP.

GP: standard · Flagged by labs from 3.0 mmol/L (EAS) · NHG goal with raised risk: below 2.6 mmol/L · ESC goal: below 3.0 at low risk, 1.8 at high risk

ApoB

ApoB (apolipoprotein B) is a protein on the outside of every particle that can build up in your vessel walls: LDL, but also VLDL, the particles that carry triglycerides (a type of fat), and Lp(a). Each of those particles carries exactly one ApoB. So your LDL cholesterol tells you how much cholesterol the particles carry, and ApoB tells you how many particles there are.

  1. Each particle can carry more or less cholesterol.
  2. When your particles are small and carry little cholesterol, your LDL can look normal while you have many particles.
  3. The number of particles decides how many end up in your vessel wall. Then ApoB shows a higher risk than your LDL.
  4. This happens more often with high triglycerides, diabetes, belly fat or a very low LDL.

In most people LDL and ApoB tell the same story: in 346,686 people in the UK, ApoB added almost nothing once total and HDL cholesterol were known. The European guideline estimates that the two don't match in about 1 in 5 people. In 389,529 people not on cholesterol medication, only ApoB stayed linked to heart attacks when it was compared side by side with non-HDL cholesterol and triglycerides. So I advise asking for both. When your ApoB is high compared with your LDL, your risk is higher than your LDL suggests. In my view, fibre has the highest return there.

GP: not standard (NHG) · Flagged by labs from 1.0 g/L (EAS) · ESC goal: below 1.0, 0.8 or 0.65 g/L at moderate, high or very high risk

Lp(a): lipoprotein(a)

Lp(a) is an LDL particle with an extra protein attached, called apo(a). More than 90% of your level is set by your genes, more than for any other blood fat. Your level is mostly fixed from childhood, although in women it can rise after menopause. Food and exercise barely change it.

  1. Like LDL, Lp(a) can get stuck in your vessel walls.
  2. It carries oxidised fats that fuel inflammation there.
  3. It's also linked to calcium build-up in the aortic valve, the valve where blood leaves your heart.
  4. The higher your level, the higher the risk: at 100 mg/dL, the risk of heart and vascular disease roughly doubles.

About 1.4 billion people have a raised Lp(a). European cardiologists advise measuring it at least once in every adult, especially with early heart disease or high cholesterol in the family. Above 50 mg/dL (about 105 nmol/L) they count it as a risk factor. One of our clients, a 33-year-old entrepreneur, came to us for his gut. His blood showed a total cholesterol of 6.8 mmol/L and a high Lp(a), set by his genes: a higher genetic risk he had no idea about. His plan: 40 to 50 g of fibre a day, zone 2 training and testing regularly. That plan won't lower his Lp(a) much. It lowers the rest of his risk. In 14,051 people, those with an Lp(a) of 50 mg/dL or more and the healthiest habits had a third of the risk of heart and vascular disease of those with the least healthy habits. Or as I put it: 'You can have the genes without them ever showing up, as long as what you do lines up.' With a high Lp(a), guidelines advise keeping your other risk factors low, LDL first, sometimes with medication via your GP. If yours is high, testing your parents, brothers, sisters and children can be worthwhile.

GP: not standard (NHG) · Raised: above 50 mg/dL or about 105 nmol/L (ESC 2025) · Units differ per lab and don't convert exactly; 500 mg/L is 50 mg/dL · Once is enough; women may retest after menopause

hs-CRP

Inflammation is how your immune system responds to damage or infection. CRP (C-reactive protein) is a protein your liver then makes. The hs-CRP test (high-sensitivity CRP) also picks up very small amounts, so you see low-grade inflammation: a level that stays slightly raised without an infection. For me it's one of the three most important values to check.

  1. Inflammation in your vessel wall helps plaques grow, and makes them more likely to tear.
  2. Belly fat, smoking and moving little can keep your CRP slightly raised.
  3. An infection, an injury or a very hard training session raises CRP for a short time.
  4. So only a level that stays raised tells you something about your heart.

In 160,309 people from 54 studies, a three times higher CRP went with a 37% higher risk of coronary heart disease (narrowed arteries of the heart), after taking the usual risk factors into account. CRP was also linked to other diseases, so it's a general alarm signal and isn't specific to your heart. American cardiologists now advise measuring hs-CRP in everyone, together with cholesterol: below 1 mg/L counts as lower risk, 1 to 3 as average and above 3 as higher. The European guideline counts a level that stays above 2 mg/L as a factor that can move you into a higher risk group. Measure when you're well, not during a cold and not right after a race. Above 10 mg/L, measure again after 2 to 3 weeks and use the lower value. Exercise training lowered CRP in 83 trials, most clearly when people also lost body fat. In my experience, food has the biggest effect on it, then poor sleep and stress.

GP: regular CRP, not the sensitive hs-CRP · Lower, average, higher risk: below 1, 1 to 3, above 3 mg/L (ACC) · ESC: stays above 2 mg/L

Triglycerides and HDL

Triglycerides are the most common fat in your blood: the form in which your body stores energy from food. HDL (high-density lipoprotein) is the particle that carries extra cholesterol from your tissues back to your liver. Both are in the GP's standard test, and many people read a high HDL with low triglycerides as a sign that all is well.

  1. Triglycerides travel in particles that also carry ApoB, so high triglycerides usually mean more particles.
  2. In genetic studies, lowering triglycerides lowered heart risk only as far as ApoB went down.
  3. Genes that raise HDL didn't lower the risk of a heart attack.
  4. Very high HDL even went with higher mortality: in men from about 2.5 mmol/L, in women from 3.5.

On carnivore this is easy to miss. In the survey of 2,029 carnivore dieters, the people who shared results had a good HDL (1.8 mmol/L) and low triglycerides (0.8 mmol/L), with a median LDL of 4.5 mmol/L. A good HDL doesn't cancel out a high LDL. You don't need to fast for these either: after a normal meal triglycerides are on average up to 0.3 mmol/L higher, and HDL doesn't change. Fasting only makes sense when your triglycerides come back above 5 mmol/L.

GP: standard · Flagged by labs: triglycerides from 2.0 mmol/L (1.7 fasting), HDL at 1.0 mmol/L or lower (EAS)

Resting heart rate and VO2max

Your resting heart rate is how many times your heart beats per minute when you're fully at rest. VO2max is the most oxygen your body can use per minute during an all-out effort, in millilitres per kilo of body weight. Both say a lot about your heart, and you measure them without a needle.

  1. Endurance training makes your heart pump more blood per beat. In one trial, 8 weeks of intervals raised that by about 10%.
  2. So at rest your heart needs fewer beats. Across 191 studies, endurance training lowered resting heart rate.
  3. With more blood per beat, more oxygen reaches your muscles, and your VO2max goes up.

In 46 studies with 1.2 million people, every 10 beats a minute higher resting heart rate went with a 9% higher risk of dying. For fitness the link is stronger: each MET more (about 1 km/h faster running pace) went with a 13% lower risk of dying, and the least fit group had a 70% higher risk than the fittest. A wearable tracks your resting heart rate every night. Its VO2max is an estimate: devices that use your runs are right on average, but for one person they can be off by up to about 10 ml per kilo per minute. So follow your own trend on the same device, and do a lab test on a treadmill or bike if you want the exact number.

Measure: with a wearable, no blood test needed · Low fitness in the 33 studies: below 7.9 METs, about 28 ml/kg/min

The basics your GP checks

A GP heart risk profile also includes these. You probably know them already.

  • Blood pressure, measured more than once.
  • Glucose, preferably fasting.
  • Kidney function (eGFR) and protein (albumin) in your urine.
  • Your BMI and waist.
  • Smoking, alcohol, food and how much you move: the GP guideline advises at least 150 minutes a week of moderate exercise, such as walking or cycling, and no more than 8 hours a day sitting.

Your GP estimates heart risk with these for men from 40 and women from 50, or earlier with early heart disease in the family or other risk factors. Under 40 without risk factors, the guideline doesn't advise a routine check.

Part 2

How I brought my LDL from 4.2 to 1.4

Three changes at once, so I don't know which did the most. Per change: what I did and what research says.

Fibre: 50 grams or more a day

Fibre is the part of plant food your gut can't digest: in vegetables, fruit, legumes (beans, lentils, chickpeas), whole grains, oats and nuts. Meat has none. Soluble fibre is the kind that forms a gel with water, as in oats, legumes and fruit. It lowers LDL through your bile.

  1. Your liver makes bile from cholesterol. Bile helps you digest fat, and your gut normally takes most of it back up.
  2. Soluble fibre holds on to bile in your gut, so more of it leaves your body.
  3. Your liver has to make new bile, and uses cholesterol from your blood to do it. Your LDL goes down.

The effect per gram is small. In 67 trials, each gram of soluble fibre lowered LDL by about 0.06 mmol/L: 3 grams from oats, about three bowls of 28 grams, lowered it by about 0.13. Those trials tested a few grams of soluble fibre, far less than my 50 grams of total fibre, so they can't tell how much of my drop came from fibre. Over the long term the picture is bigger. In 185 studies, the people who ate the most fibre had a 15 to 30% lower risk of dying and of heart and vascular disease than those who ate the least. The benefit was clearest at 25 to 29 grams a day, and the curves suggest more could help more. The Netherlands Nutrition Centre advises at least 30 grams a day for men and 24 for women, and says more isn't needed. My 50 grams is well above that. Still, in my view fibre has the highest return.

Careful. Going from little fibre to 50 grams at once can cause wind, cramps or constipation. Build it up step by step and drink enough with it.

Netherlands Nutrition Centre: at least 30 g (men) or 24 g (women) a day

Lean steak instead of fatty cuts

Saturated fat is the fat that's solid at room temperature: in fatty meat, butter, cheese, cream and coconut oil. I kept eating steak, but only non-fatty cuts such as tenderloin and beef shank.

  1. Your liver clears LDL from your blood with LDL receptors: docking points on its cells that pull LDL in.
  2. Some saturated fats make those receptors less active, so more LDL stays in your blood.
  3. Several unsaturated fats, as in olive oil and nuts, do the opposite.

In a trial, 113 healthy adults ate red meat, white meat or plant protein for 4 weeks each, on a diet high or low in saturated fat. High saturated fat raised LDL and ApoB whatever the protein. Red and white meat raised LDL equally, and plant protein gave the lowest LDL. So it's mostly the fat in the meat, and the protein source counts too. In 15 long trials with about 59,000 people, eating less saturated fat for at least two years lowered the risk of heart and vascular events by 21%. It made little or no difference to the risk of dying. Good to know: swapping saturated fat for carbs or unsaturated fat can raise Lp(a) by 10 to 15%, while your LDL goes down.

Zone 2 and 4x4 intervals

Zone 2 is easy endurance training: a heart rate low enough that you can still talk. When my LDL went down, I did 120 minutes of zone 2 a week. Now I train about 150 minutes a week in zone 2, plus Norwegian 4x4 intervals twice a week to raise my VO2max. Of everything, running lowered my stress the most, and it helped me lower my LDL. I'm training for a 50 km ultra in Dubai.

  1. 4 minutes all-out. In the study: 90 to 95% of your maximum heart rate.
  2. 3 minutes rest. In the study: easy jogging at about 70%.
  3. Repeat four times.
  4. I do this twice a week.

In 40 moderately trained men, 8 weeks of 4x4 intervals raised VO2max by 7%, more than the same amount of work at an easy or medium pace. On LDL, training alone does little: in 49 trials with 2,990 men, aerobic exercise lowered triglycerides by 9% and LDL by only 3%, too little to be sure of. Together with food it works better. In a one-year trial with 377 people with raised LDL and low HDL, a lower-fat diet only lowered LDL clearly when they also exercised: in men by about 0.5 mmol/L. The WHO advises adults 150 to 300 minutes of moderate exercise a week, or 75 to 150 minutes of hard exercise.

Careful. Hard intervals put real strain on your heart. New to hard training, or do you have chest pain, dizziness or a heart condition? Check with your GP first.

My resting heart rate: 53 to 37 in 12 months

Over 12 months my resting heart rate went from 53 to 37. I walked on a walking pad under my desk with a 10 kg weighted vest, for 30,000 to 40,000 steps a day. Each week I also did 200 minutes in zone 2 and 30 to 60 minutes in zone 4 or higher (so hard you can only say a few words). I changed more than one thing, so I don't know which part did the most.

Across 191 studies, endurance training lowered resting heart rate, more in people who started higher. On steps, the research is clear up to a point: in 47,471 adults, the risk of dying kept falling up to 8,000 to 10,000 steps a day for people under 60, and up to 6,000 to 8,000 for people over 60. My 30,000 to 40,000 is far beyond that, so the research can't tell whether the extra steps did anything for my heart. The GP guideline advises sitting no more than 8 hours a day, and a walking pad is one way to get there.

Careful. A resting heart rate this low is common in trained endurance athletes: without symptoms, a heart rate of 30 or more counts as normal for them. A low heart rate with dizziness or fainting is a reason to see your GP.
