Why you crash at 3pm

What happens in your body in the afternoon, what makes the dip deeper, and what you can do about it.

In the morning you're sharp. Around three it fades: you reach for your phone sooner and your decisions get worse. Most people think that's normal. It doesn't have to be. With most of the 400+ entrepreneurs I've worked with, the cause was their biology: things they do every day. I did everything by the book myself and still didn't feel good. Only when I started measuring did I see what worked. Below you'll find what almost nobody knows about that dip first, then my plan for your day.

What almost nobody knows

Five findings about your afternoon, each with a number and what to do with it.

4pm

Your dip comes even if you skip lunch

Your circadian rhythm is your body clock: cells in your brain that decide, over about 24 hours, when you're alert and when you get sleepy. In the early afternoon that clock sends a weaker wake signal. In a lab, people got a small meal every hour and still got sleepier in the middle of the day. In England, crashes caused by drivers falling asleep peak three times a day, and one of those peaks is around 4pm. So plan calls or work you can do on foot for that hour. Save your hardest work for the morning.

Lab study and 679 crashes

1 hour

Something sweet leaves you more tired within the hour

Across 31 studies, sugar didn't improve mood. In the first hour afterwards, people felt more tired and less alert. In another study, a chocolate bar gave a small lift at first and more tiredness an hour later. A brisk 10-minute walk gave the same 18 people more energy for up to 2 hours. So when your dip comes, walk briskly for 10 minutes first.

Review of 31 studies

30 or 90 min

A nap: 30 or 90 minutes, 6 hours after waking

A sleep cycle is one round through light sleep, deep sleep and dream sleep, and takes about 90 minutes. With 90 minutes you finish one cycle and wake from light sleep. With 30 minutes you're groggy for a bit when you wake: that's called sleep inertia, and it usually wears off within half an hour. Plan your nap about 6 hours after you get up: up at 7am, then around 1pm. Need a nap every day? Look at your nights first.

My advice

6 hours

Two weeks on 6 hours' sleep: you perform worse and barely notice

Sleep pressure is the urge to sleep that builds up while you're awake. If you sleep too little, you carry some of it into the next day. In a lab study, healthy adults spent 14 nights with 4, 6 or 8 hours in bed. At 6 hours their performance got a little worse every day. After the first few days, they hardly felt any sleepier. So count your hours, for example with a wearable (a ring or watch that tracks your sleep), and aim for at least 7.

Lab study, 14 nights

53 → 37

My resting heart rate went from 53 to 37 in 12 months

Your resting heart rate is how many times your heart beats per minute when you're fully at rest. Across 191 studies, exercise lowered it, endurance training and yoga most clearly. I walked on a walking pad under my desk with a 10 kg weighted vest: 30,000 to 40,000 steps a day. Each week I also did 200 minutes in heart rate zone 2 (easy: you can still talk) and 30 to 60 minutes in zone 4 or higher (so hard you can only say a few words). I changed more than one thing, so I don't know which part did the most. Your first step can be today: take your calls on foot.

Why it works

What happens with caffeine, your lunch, your carbs and a short night. Then the basics in short.

Caffeine: less, and earlier

Adenosine is a substance your brain makes while you're awake. The more adenosine builds up, the higher your sleep pressure and the sleepier you feel. Caffeine is in coffee, tea, cola, energy drinks, pre-workout and chocolate. It blocks the spots adenosine attaches to. This is how it works:

  1. You feel your sleep pressure less, while it's still there.
  2. Caffeine stays in your body for hours. How fast you break it down varies a lot from person to person.
  3. You sleep less, and less deeply, often without noticing.
  4. The next afternoon you have more sleep pressure and reach for coffee sooner. That keeps you in a loop.

Across 24 studies, caffeine cost an average of 45 minutes of sleep. A normal coffee (about 107 mg) had to be finished at least 8.8 hours before bed, a pre-workout 13.2 hours. In 23 young men, 100 mg had no measurable effect on sleep, even 4 hours before bed. 400 mg, about four cups, still disturbed sleep when taken 12 hours before bed, and at 8 and 12 hours the men didn't notice it themselves. So the amount counts, as well as the timing. If you use caffeine daily and stop all at once, you can get headaches and tiredness for 2 to 9 days, from as little as 100 mg a day. So cut down step by step. This is how I did it: decaf for the taste, caffeine from a capsule so I know how much I take, and then the dose down step by step. I went from more than eight cups a day, about 800 mg, to 30 to 60 mg. Over that period my HRV went from 86 to 152 ms (milliseconds). I changed more than one thing at the time, so I don't know how much of that came from the caffeine.

Never start without data. When you change your caffeine, your sleep changes with it. Track your sleep and HRV for a week before and after, so you see what it does for you.

Your lunch: smaller, with the carbs last

Glucose is the sugar in your blood: fuel for your cells and your brain. When you eat carbohydrates (bread, pasta, rice, potatoes, fruit, sugar), your glucose rises. Your pancreas, an organ behind your stomach, then makes insulin. Insulin is a hormone that opens your cells so the glucose can get in, and your blood sugar falls again. This is how your lunch can make your dip deeper:

  1. A big meal adds to your clock's dip. The effect only shows after half an hour, once you're back at work.
  2. Fast carbohydrates with no protein or fibre make your glucose rise fast, and your body makes a lot of insulin.
  3. 2 to 3 hours later, some people's glucose briefly drops below where it was before the meal. That goes with getting hungry sooner.
  4. Eat vegetables and protein first and the carbs last, and your glucose and insulin rise less.

In a driving simulator, 12 young men had a light lunch (305 kcal) or a heavy one (922 kcal) after a 5-hour night. After the heavy lunch they drifted out of their lane more often, although only after the first half hour. Whether fat or carbohydrates make you sleepier isn't clear: studies contradict each other. So the size of your lunch counts most clearly. In 8 healthy young people, glucose and insulin rose least when they ate the rice last. A glucose monitor is a sensor on your arm that measures your glucose day and night. In 1,070 people wearing one, a dip 2 to 3 hours after eating predicted more hunger later in the day, although the links were small. Doctors only speak of reactive hypoglycaemia, a real sugar crash after eating, when symptoms coincide with blood sugar below 3.3 mmol/L. That's why my biggest tip for your lunch is: never eat carbs on their own, and never drink them.

Enough carbs: at least 2 grams per kilo

Your thyroid is a gland in your neck that sets how fast your body uses energy. It makes a hormone called T4. Your body turns T4 into T3: the active form, which makes your cells work faster. If you eat very few carbs for weeks, your body can make less T3. That's why I don't cut carbs out. My rule:

  1. Eat at least 2 grams of carbs per kilo of body weight a day. At 70 kilos, that's 140 grams.
  2. On days you train, eat 50 to 100 grams more.
  3. Eat them with vegetables and protein, never on their own, and don't drink them.

In 11 healthy people, three weeks on a diet with the same calories but very few carbs (15% of their energy) lowered T3. On a diet with more carbs (55%), T3 stayed the same. TSH, the signal from your brain to your thyroid, didn't change on either diet. It was a small pilot study, and it didn't measure energy. For me, a blood test showed my thyroid was running slow at a time when I was on the carnivore diet: almost only meat, so very few carbs. Older blood tests showed it had been on the slow side for longer. The only change I made was to eat more carbs again, and I felt much better. For comparison: EFSA estimates your brain needs about 130 grams of glucose a day, and advises getting 45 to 60% of your energy from carbs. For most people that's more than my minimum.

After a short night: creatine

Creatine is a substance your muscles and brain use to make energy fast. You get 1 to 2 grams a day from meat and fish, and your body makes about the same amount itself. This is why it can help after a short night:

  1. Your cells run on a fuel called ATP. Creatine helps refill it quickly.
  2. When you stay awake for a long time, the fast energy in your brain runs lower and your thinking slows down.
  3. A normal daily dose raises the creatine in your brain only slowly, over weeks.
  4. In one study, a large single dose changed the energy in the brain within a few hours, and partly made up for the drop.

In a lab, 29 healthy people stayed awake for 21 hours. They got one dose of creatine (0.2 grams per kilo, 14 grams at 70 kilos) or a placebo, a dummy pill. With creatine, their scores on logic, number and reaction tests dropped less: up to 12% better. In an earlier study by the same team, a higher dose (0.35 grams per kilo) worked more strongly. A 2026 review found only 5 studies like this: the early results look good, but there's little research so far. What I do myself: I take creatine every morning. I advise 5 grams a day, at any time of day, every day; you don't need to build it up. After a bad night, I advise up to 20 grams. EFSA judged up to 3 grams a day unlikely to pose any risk, and advises against high doses. So 5 grams is above that, and 20 grams is far above it. In footballers, 10 grams in one go caused diarrhoea more often than two doses of 5 grams, so split a large dose.

Never start without data. There are doubts about high doses and your kidneys. Kidney problems, or taking medication? Ask your GP first. After a short night, note how your afternoon goes with and without creatine, so you see whether it does anything for you.

The basics

You probably know these. Here they are in short, with the research:

  • Sleep at least 7 hours a night; that's the advice for adults.
  • Get daylight in the morning: office workers with lots of morning light fell asleep faster and slept better.
  • Put your hardest work in your sharpest hours; these depend on whether you're a morning or an evening person.
  • Walk for 10 minutes after lunch: walking lowers your blood sugar after meals more clearly than standing.
  • Get up every half hour and walk for a few minutes: in two small studies that meant less tiredness than staying seated.
  • Don't drink your carbs: orange juice raised insulin more than whole oranges with the same sugar.
  • Keep a bottle of water on your desk: after losing just over 1% of their weight in fluid, young men felt more tired, although the research is mixed.

Part 3

What you can measure

So you don't have to guess. Start with your daily data from a wearable, then look at your blood. That order gives you the most.

Your wearable: sleep, HRV and resting heart rate

A wearable is a ring or watch you wear day and night, such as an Oura or WHOOP. It measures your sleep, your HRV and your resting heart rate. These are my rules for reading them:

  1. Look mainly at your sleep duration and bedtimes. Wearables measure those well, your sleep stages (deep sleep and REM, dream sleep) less so.
  2. Look at your average HRV over a week. One night says little.
  3. Only compare with yourself, on the same device. Brands calculate HRV differently.
  4. Put your numbers next to your afternoons: after which nights, lunches and busy weeks do you crash?

Six wearables, including Oura and WHOOP, were compared with a sleep lab in 53 adults. They measured well when and how long people slept. For sleep stages, about 60% matched. Across 11 studies, RMSSD dropped after sleep loss. RMSSD is the way Oura and WHOOP, among others, calculate your HRV. In most of 37 studies, HRV changed with stress, usually because the part of your nervous system that lets you relax and recover was less active.

Your blood: iron, thyroid and glucose

Your wearable shows your rhythm; your blood shows whether something sits underneath. Ferritin is your iron store; you need iron to carry oxygen around your body. Your thyroid is a gland in your neck that sets how fast your body uses energy. TSH is the signal from your brain to your thyroid: if your thyroid is too slow, your TSH goes up. For an afternoon dip, look first at:

  • Ferritin and haemoglobin: your iron stores, and the protein that carries oxygen in your blood.
  • TSH and free T4: how hard your thyroid is working. T4 is the hormone your thyroid makes itself. A clearly underactive thyroid often causes tiredness and feeling the cold easily.
  • Fasting glucose and insulin: how steady your blood sugar is when you haven't eaten, and how much insulin your body needs for that.
  • A CGM (continuous glucose monitor): a sensor with a tiny needle in your arm that measures your glucose day and night. It shows whether your dip lines up with a drop after lunch.

A study followed 198 menstruating women with fatigue and a ferritin below 50 µg/L, with no anaemia (so with enough haemoglobin). Extra iron cut their fatigue by 48%, against 29% with a placebo, a dummy pill. When the thyroid is only slightly slow, treatment didn't ease symptoms across 21 studies. On a CGM, people with normal blood results spent 15% of the time in the prediabetes range, an early stage before diabetes. What that means for your energy hasn't been studied well. I saw in a blood test that my own thyroid was running slow; that story is under 'Enough carbs'. In people who see their GP for fatigue, a physical cause is found in only about 8%. Tired for more than a month, or other symptoms as well? See your GP. Always discuss an abnormal result with your GP.

Never start without data. Of the supplements people take without a blood test, I'd say about 80% won't do anything for them. Don't take iron on a hunch: low iron has a cause your GP will want to know. The women in the study took 80 mg of iron a day for 12 weeks, through their GP. EFSA calls 40 mg a day safe for adults, from food and supplements together.

Your energy check

Ten questions. You'll see right away where to look first.

Where does your dip come from?

Think of a normal working week and choose no, sometimes or often for each question. Each answer counts towards one or two areas.

  1. You wake up tired, even after 7 hours in bed.

  2. After a short or restless night, your afternoon dip is much worse.

  3. After 1pm you still need coffee or an energy drink to keep going.

  4. Within an hour of lunch you feel heavy or sleepy.

  5. In the afternoon you reach for something sweet to keep going.

  6. In the afternoon you sit for hours without getting up.

  7. You're tired even on days when you slept well and had a light lunch.

  8. You feel the cold more than the people around you.

  9. You feel 'on' all day and find it hard to wind down in the evening.

  10. When the pressure lifts, like on a Friday evening or on holiday, you're suddenly exhausted.
