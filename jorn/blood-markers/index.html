The blood markers I get tested

And what they say about your energy and focus.

Your GP checks whether your blood results fall within the reference range: the range most healthy people fall within. That tells you whether you're probably ill. I also want to know where my results sit within that range, and how they move when I change something. That's how I found out my thyroid was running slow: I ate by the book, trained every day and still gained 10 kg. This page starts with what almost nobody knows, then how to prepare for your blood test and read it, then all 84 markers I get tested, and after that the 8 I find most important for energy and focus.

What almost nobody knows

Five things that change how you read a blood result.

1 in 20

Healthy people fall outside the range too

A lab sets its reference range by testing healthy people and keeping the middle 95% of their results. So about 1 in 20 healthy results falls outside it by definition. The more markers you test, the more likely one lands just outside. Is one result just outside the range? Then have it retested first, the same way.

Review of reference ranges

9 of 23

Biotin can throw off your thyroid test

Biotin is a B vitamin in many hair, skin and nail supplements. Many lab tests use biotin to catch the substance they measure. In 6 healthy adults who took 10 mg a day for a week, a common supplement dose, 9 of 23 of these tests gave a wrong result, thyroid tests included. Your thyroid is the gland in your neck that sets the pace of your metabolism. Do you take biotin? Mention it before your blood is taken, and ask whether to stop first.

Study of 6 healthy adults

< 50

A low ferritin matters, even with a normal haemoglobin

Ferritin is the protein that stores iron, mainly in your liver: your iron reserve. When you lose iron, that reserve empties first. Only later does your haemoglobin drop: the protein that carries oxygen in your blood. In 198 women with fatigue, a ferritin under 50 µg/L and no anaemia (enough haemoglobin), the fatigue score fell by 48% with iron and by 29% with placebo, a dummy pill. That's why you ask for ferritin separately.

Study of 198 women

5 years

Insulin sensitivity drops years before glucose rises

Insulin is the hormone that moves glucose, the sugar in your blood, into your cells. Insulin sensitivity is how well your cells respond to it. In British civil servants who developed diabetes, it fell steeply in the 5 years before. Their fasting glucose only rose quickly in the last 3. Ask for fasting insulin too, and always compare it at the same lab: of 10 lab methods for insulin, 7 were often more than 15% off.

Study of 6,538 people

4.2 → 1.4

My LDL fell by two thirds

LDL is the particle that carries cholesterol, a fatty substance, through your blood. A high LDL is linked to heart and vascular disease. Mine went from 4.2 to 1.4 mmol/L with three changes: more than 50 g of fibre a day, only lean beef such as tenderloin and shank, and 120 minutes of zone 2 a week. Zone 2 is steady cardio at a pace where you can still talk. For comparison, the Netherlands Nutrition Centre advises men aged 18 to 49 to eat at least 30 g of fibre a day. I changed three things at once, so I don't know which did most. LDL isn't one of the 8 markers below; I include it to show how far a blood value can move.

Before your blood test

How to get a result you can use, and how to read it.

How to prepare for your blood test

How and when you have blood taken partly decides your result. With these 7 steps you can also compare your tests fairly later:

  1. Have it done in the morning, ideally between 8 and 10. Testosterone, the main male sex hormone, is highest in the morning, and the guideline advises measuring it then.
  2. Come fasted: nothing to eat from the evening before; water is fine. Glucose and insulin need it.
  3. Take biotin? Mention it, and ask whether to stop first.
  4. Ill or just over a cold? Postpone your test. An infection can raise CRP, a protein your liver makes during inflammation, up to 1,000-fold, and your ferritin goes up too.
  5. Always test at the same lab, at the same time of day. Labs measure differently, and many markers swing through the day.
  6. Keep each result with its reference range. Ask your GP or lab for your older results too: you need them to compare.
  7. Ask for the 8 markers on this page. For fatigue, your GP usually tests only some of them.

How I read a result

One test is a snapshot. What I look at most is how a value moves. This is how I go about it:

  1. Compare with your old results. That's how I saw my thyroid had been on the slow side for longer: older tests showed it too.
  2. Start broad, and only go deeper when a value is off. For the thyroid, that means TSH first: the signal from your brain to your thyroid. Free T4 and free T3, the thyroid hormones themselves, only come next if your TSH is off.
  3. After a change, wait at least 6 to 8 weeks before you retest. In my experience, that's how long blood values take to move.
  4. After that, retest every quarter or every six months, the same way each time.

People differ from each other far more than one person differs from test to test. So your own results usually sit in a narrow part of the range, and a shift can mean something for you, even within range. The reference change value tells you whether a difference counts: the smallest gap between two of your results that is bigger than the normal swing of your body and of the test. A lab can work it out per marker. Ask for it when two results differ.

Every marker in my blood test

The full list: 84 markers in 10 groups, each explained in one line.

The full list

This is everything I get tested. I explain the 8 markers after this in depth, because they say the most about energy and focus. A single marker says little on its own: look at the whole picture and at how it changes. Always discuss an abnormal result with your GP.

84 markers · 10 groups

Haematology (full blood count) 15

  • White blood cells (WBC)

    The number of immune cells in your blood, which fight infections and often rise when you are ill.

  • Red blood cells (RBC)

    The number of red blood cells, which carry oxygen from your lungs to the rest of your body.

  • Haemoglobin

    The iron-rich protein in your red blood cells that carries oxygen, and a low level is linked to tiredness.

  • Haematocrit

    The share of your blood made up of red blood cells.

  • MCV (mean cell volume)

    The average size of your red blood cells: small cells are linked to low iron, large ones to low B12 or folate.

  • MCH (mean cell haemoglobin)

    The average amount of haemoglobin, the protein that carries oxygen, in one red blood cell.

  • MCHC (mean cell haemoglobin concentration)

    How full your red blood cells are with haemoglobin on average, measured as its concentration per cell.

  • Platelets

    Tiny cell fragments that help your blood clot when you have a wound.

  • Neutrophils

    The most common white blood cells and your first line of defence against bacteria.

  • Lymphocytes

    White blood cells that fight viruses and remember germs you met before, partly by making antibodies, proteins that tag germs.

  • Monocytes

    White blood cells that move into your tissues to clear away germs and dead cells.

  • Eosinophils

    White blood cells involved in allergies and in fighting parasites, such as worms.

  • Basophils

    The rarest white blood cells, which release histamine, a substance behind allergic reactions.

  • RDW (red cell distribution width)

    How much your red blood cells differ in size, and a wider spread is linked to low iron, B12 or folate.

  • MPV (mean platelet volume)

    The average size of your platelets: young platelets are larger, so it hints at how fast you make new ones.

Iron studies 5

  • Ferritin

    The protein that stores iron, so it shows your iron reserves: low stores are linked to tiredness, and inflammation raises it.

  • Serum iron

    How much iron is in your blood right now, which changes a lot during the day and from day to day.

  • TIBC / transferrin

    Transferrin is the protein that carries iron in your blood, and TIBC shows how much it can carry in total.

  • Transferrin saturation

    The percentage of transferrin, the iron carrier in your blood, that is actually carrying iron.

  • UIBC (unsaturated iron-binding capacity)

    The part of transferrin's carrying capacity that is still free and not yet holding iron.

Metabolic and glucose 15

  • Fasting glucose

    How much glucose is in your blood after a night without food: the sugar your body uses as its main fuel.

  • HbA1c

    The share of your haemoglobin with sugar attached, which shows your average blood sugar over the past 2 to 3 months.

  • Fasting insulin

    The hormone that moves sugar into your cells, and a high fasting level can mean your cells respond less well.

  • HOMA-IR

    Calculated from fasting glucose and insulin, it estimates insulin resistance: how poorly your cells respond to insulin.

  • Sodium

    The mineral in table salt that controls your body's water balance and helps your nerves and muscles work.

  • Potassium

    A mineral that keeps your nerves, muscles and heart rhythm working, and your kidneys keep its level steady.

  • Chloride

    A mineral that works with sodium to keep your water balance and the acidity of your blood steady.

  • CO2 (bicarbonate)

    Bicarbonate stops your blood from becoming too acidic and shows how well your lungs and kidneys keep that balance.

  • Calcium

    A mineral for your bones, teeth, muscles, nerves and heartbeat, and your body keeps its blood level within tight limits.

  • Phosphorus

    A mineral that builds bones and teeth with calcium, and helps your cells store and use energy.

  • Albumin

    The main protein in your blood, made by your liver, which keeps fluid in your blood vessels and carries substances around.

  • Globulin

    A group of blood proteins that includes your immune system's antibodies, usually calculated as total protein minus albumin.

  • Albumin/globulin ratio

    Compares albumin with globulins, the two main groups of protein in your blood.

  • Total protein

    All the protein in your blood together, mainly albumin and globulins.

  • BUN (urea)

    A waste product your liver makes when it breaks down protein, which your kidneys remove in your urine.

Kidney 4

  • Creatinine

    A waste product from your muscles that your kidneys filter out, so it depends on kidney function and muscle mass.

  • eGFR

    An estimate of how much blood your kidneys filter each minute, usually calculated from creatinine, age and sex.

  • Cystatin C

    A protein your cells make at a steady rate, showing kidney filtration with less influence from muscle mass than creatinine.

  • Uric acid

    A waste product from purines in cells and food, and high levels are linked to gout, a painful joint condition.

Liver 7

  • ALT

    A liver enzyme: a protein that drives reactions in your cells and leaks into your blood when liver cells are damaged.

  • AST

    An enzyme in your liver, heart and muscles, so it rises with liver strain but also after hard training.

  • GGT (gamma-GT)

    An enzyme in your liver and bile ducts that rises with alcohol, some medicines and strain on your liver.

  • ALP (alkaline phosphatase)

    An enzyme mainly from your liver, bile ducts and bones, which rises when bile flow is blocked or bones grow faster.

  • Total bilirubin

    A yellow pigment from the breakdown of old red blood cells, which your liver processes and removes with bile, a digestive fluid.

  • Direct bilirubin

    Bilirubin your liver has already processed, ready to leave your body with bile.

  • Indirect bilirubin

    Bilirubin your liver has not processed yet, usually calculated as total minus direct bilirubin.

Lipids / cardiovascular 14

  • Total cholesterol

    All the cholesterol in your blood, a fatty substance your body uses to build cells and hormones.

  • HDL cholesterol

    Cholesterol in HDL particles, which carry excess cholesterol from your tissues back to your liver.

  • LDL cholesterol

    Cholesterol in LDL particles, which carry it to your tissues, and a high level is linked to heart disease.

  • Triglycerides

    The main type of fat in your blood, used for energy, with levels that rise after eating and with alcohol.

  • Non-HDL cholesterol

    Total cholesterol minus HDL: all the cholesterol in particles that can build up in your blood vessel walls.

  • TG:HDL ratio

    Compares triglycerides with HDL cholesterol, and a high ratio is linked to insulin resistance, when cells respond poorly to insulin.

  • ApoB

    A protein on every LDL and similar particle, so it counts the particles that can build up in your vessel walls.

  • Lipoprotein(a)

    An LDL-like particle with an extra protein attached, whose level is mostly set by your genes and linked to heart disease.

  • Omega-3 index

    The share of omega-3 fats EPA and DHA in your red blood cell membranes, reflecting your intake over recent months.

  • ApoA1

    The main protein in HDL particles, so it reflects how many cholesterol-clearing particles you have.

  • ApoB/ApoA1 ratio

    Compares ApoB, on particles that can build up in vessel walls, with ApoA1, on particles that clear cholesterol away.

  • VLDL cholesterol

    Cholesterol in VLDL particles, which your liver makes to carry triglycerides, usually calculated from your triglycerides.

  • LDL/HDL ratio

    Compares LDL cholesterol, which travels to your tissues, with HDL cholesterol, which goes back to your liver.

  • Total cholesterol/HDL ratio

    Compares your total cholesterol with your HDL cholesterol, the part that carries cholesterol back to your liver.

Inflammation 2

  • hs-CRP

    A protein your liver makes during inflammation, and this sensitive test also picks up mild, long-lasting inflammation.

  • Homocysteine

    A by-product of protein that B12, folate and B6 help clear, and high levels are linked to heart disease.

Thyroid 5

  • TSH

    A hormone from your pituitary gland, at the base of your brain, that tells your thyroid how hard to work.

  • Free T3

    The active thyroid hormone, mostly made from T4 in your tissues, which sets how fast your cells use energy.

  • Free T4

    The main hormone your thyroid makes: your tissues take up the free part and turn it into active T3.

  • Anti-TPO

    Antibodies against TPO, an enzyme your thyroid needs for its hormones, and high levels point to an immune attack on it.

  • Anti-Tg

    Antibodies against thyroglobulin, the protein your thyroid uses to make and store hormones: also a sign of an immune reaction.

Hormones 11

  • Total testosterone

    The main male sex hormone, which women also make in smaller amounts, and it supports muscle, bone and sex drive.

  • Free testosterone

    The small part of your testosterone not bound to proteins, which your tissues can use directly.

  • SHBG

    A protein from your liver that binds sex hormones in your blood, which affects how much testosterone and oestradiol stay free.

  • DHEA-S

    A hormone from your adrenal glands that your body can turn into testosterone and oestrogen, and levels fall with age.

  • Cortisol (morning)

    The stress hormone from your adrenal glands, on top of your kidneys, which peaks in the morning to help you wake up.

  • Free androgen index (FAI)

    Compares total testosterone with SHBG to estimate how much testosterone is free to act, used mostly in women.

  • LH

    A pituitary hormone that steers the ovaries or testes: it triggers ovulation in women and testosterone production in men.

  • FSH

    A pituitary hormone that steers the ovaries or testes: it helps eggs mature in women and sperm form in men.

  • Oestradiol

    The main form of oestrogen, made mostly in the ovaries in women and in small amounts from testosterone in men.

  • Prolactin

    A pituitary hormone that drives breast milk production, and high levels can disturb the menstrual cycle and lower testosterone.

  • IGF-1

    A growth factor your liver makes on a signal from growth hormone, so it reflects how much growth hormone you make.

Vitamins and minerals 6

  • Vitamin D (25-OH)

    The storage form of vitamin D in your blood, from sunlight and food, needed for your bones, muscles and immune system.

  • Vitamin B12

    A vitamin you need to make red blood cells and keep nerves healthy, and a clear shortage is linked to tiredness.

  • Folate (B9)

    A B vitamin for cell division and making red blood cells, which also helps B12 clear homocysteine.

  • Copper

    A mineral you need to process iron, make energy and build connective tissue, such as skin and tendons.

  • Zinc

    A mineral you need in small amounts for your immune system, wound healing, taste and hundreds of enzymes.

  • Magnesium (serum)

    A mineral for muscles, nerves and energy, and since little sits in your blood, a normal result can hide low stores.

Oxygen and iron

How your blood carries oxygen to your muscles and brain.

Haemoglobin

Haemoglobin (Hb) is the protein in your red blood cells that carries oxygen to your muscles and brain; the oxygen binds to the iron inside it. To make red blood cells, your body needs iron, vitamin B12 and folate (another B vitamin), among other things. Too little haemoglobin is called anaemia.

Anaemia is linked to fatigue, so Hb is part of the GP's standard blood test for fatigue. A normal Hb says little about your iron reserve, though: that empties first, and only then does your Hb drop. So look at your ferritin too.

Ferritin

Ferritin is the protein that stores iron: your iron reserve. A small amount enters your blood, so the bigger your reserve, the higher your ferritin. An infection or inflammation raises ferritin regardless of your reserve. So a normal ferritin can hide a low reserve. That's why you read it together with your CRP: if that's raised, your ferritin tells you less.

A review of 18 studies confirms the trial of 198 women above: with a low reserve and no anaemia, iron eased fatigue. Fitness in exercise tests didn't improve. 15 of those 18 studies included only women; in men this has been studied much less.

Never start without data. Is your ferritin low? Don't start iron on your own. Discuss it with your GP, who first looks for the reason it's low and then decides on the treatment.

Vitamins

Two vitamins many people take without a test.

I'd estimate that 80% of the supplements people take without a blood test do nothing specific for them. If your store is already full, extra does nothing. That's why I measure first.

Vitamin B12

Vitamin B12 is found mainly in meat, fish, eggs and dairy. You need it for your nerves and to make red blood cells. When there's too little, a substance called MMA (methylmalonic acid) builds up in your blood.

A single B12 result can mislead: a low level isn't always a deficiency, and a deficiency can occur with a normal level. In the grey zone (148-260 pmol/L)? Ask for MMA as well; I read the two together. In vegetarians, and most of all vegans, a review of 18 studies often found a deficiency. Without a clear deficiency, extra B12 didn't help memory or mood across 16 studies. Whether a slightly low level is linked to fatigue is unclear, according to Dutch GPs.

Never start without data. I take B12 in its methylated form, methylcobalamin, one of the forms also found in food. I've never named a dose for it, so the numbers here aren't mine. According to the Netherlands Nutrition Centre, most people don't need a B12 supplement. If you take one anyway, adults are advised to keep it to 2.8 µg a day; vegans are advised to take that amount every day. There's no official upper limit, but in some people B12 causes spots that look like acne. In a series of 5 women, they cleared 3 to 6 weeks after stopping.

Vitamin D

You make vitamin D mainly in your skin, under sunlight. Your liver turns it into 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the storage form the lab measures. Vitamin D helps you absorb calcium for strong bones, and plays a part in your muscles and your immune system.

Your level is often lower in winter: among 55,844 Europeans, 18% were below 30 nmol/L in winter, against 8% in summer. So note the month of your test, and compare winter with winter. In 120 young adults with fatigue and a level below 50 nmol/L, fatigue eased more after a single dose of vitamin D than after placebo. Experts don't agree on a target: the Endocrine Society (2024) names none, and advises against routinely testing healthy people without a reason. Do you take vitamin D? I advise taking it with your first meal. If that meal contains fat, more gets in: in 50 older adults, a meal with fat gave 32% more vitamin D3 in the blood than a fat-free meal.

Never start without data. In 2023 I took vitamin D3 with K2 every morning; K2 is another vitamin that's often in the same capsule. I've never named a dose, so these aren't my numbers. The Netherlands Nutrition Centre advises 10 µg a day (400 IU, the unit on many labels) for certain groups. Examples are people with dark skin, people who rarely go outside in the daytime and women aged 50 to 69. Everyone aged 70 and over is advised 20 µg. EFSA, the European food safety authority, sets the safe upper limit for adults at 100 µg (4,000 IU) a day. For vitamin K, EFSA has set no upper limit.

Metabolism

Your pace and your fuel: thyroid, blood sugar and insulin.

Thyroid: TSH, free T4 and free T3

Your thyroid is a gland in your neck. Its hormones set the pace of your metabolism: how much energy your cells use, your heart rate, your body heat. It works like a thermostat:

  1. Your pituitary, a small gland under your brain, makes TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone): the signal to your thyroid to make hormone. When your thyroid is too slow, TSH rises. That's why TSH is usually the first test.
  2. Your thyroid mainly makes T4, a storage form. Your tissues turn it into T3, the active form. Free T4 and free T3 are the part not bound to proteins in your blood, so the part that can work.

For a mild change, a high TSH with a normal free T4, treatment with thyroid hormone brought TSH back into range across 21 studies with 2,192 adults. Symptoms didn't ease. Since 2025 the Dutch GP guideline no longer recommends that treatment, and says free T3 adds nothing in general practice. Is your TSH slightly high? Then look at the other markers on this page too.

My own data. My TSH was around 5, above the range. I was eating keto (almost no carbohydrates), by the book, and training every day. I still gained 10 kg. Older tests showed my thyroid had been on the slow side before. The only thing I changed was eating more carbohydrates, about 200 to 250 g a day. In 2 months I lost 10 kg, my energy came back and my next blood test looked good. Diet can shift these values: in 11 healthy adults, 3 weeks of keto lowered T3, while TSH stayed the same. Do you eat keto? Then have your thyroid retested.

Glucose and insulin (fasting)

Glucose is sugar in your blood, the main fuel for your cells and your brain. Insulin is the hormone from your pancreas, an organ behind your stomach, that moves glucose into your cells, like a key. When someone's energy swings through the day, blood sugar is one of the first things I look at.

  1. When your cells respond less well to insulin, that's called insulin resistance. Your pancreas then makes extra insulin, so your fasting glucose can stay normal for a long time.
  2. HOMA-IR is a calculation using your fasting glucose and insulin. It estimates how well your cells respond to insulin.

That's why you ask for fasting insulin too (see the 5 years at the top), and always compare it at the same lab. HOMA-IR has no fixed Dutch cut-off, so follow your own trend above all.

Reference range: fasting glucose below 6.1 mmol/L · 6.1-6.9 raised · 7.0 or higher is the diabetes threshold (NHG) · Fasting insulin and HOMA-IR: no fixed Dutch cut-off, varies by lab · GP: glucose standard for fatigue, insulin not

Inflammation and hormones

Low-grade inflammation, and testosterone in men.

hs-CRP

Inflammation is how your immune system responds to damage or infection. CRP (C-reactive protein) is a protein your liver then makes. The hs-CRP (high-sensitivity CRP) test also picks up very small amounts, so you see low-grade inflammation: a level that stays slightly raised without an infection. For me, it's one of the three most important values to check. The other two are VO2 max (how much oxygen your body can use during hard exercise) and HRV (how much the time between your heartbeats varies).

Among 4,847 British civil servants without fatigue, people with a CRP of 1 mg/L or higher had 28% higher odds of becoming fatigued over the next 3 years. CRP also reacts to small triggers: about a third of Americans have a level between 3 and 10 mg/L. Never judge a single result, and ask for hs-CRP: the regular CRP test doesn't show these small rises. In my experience, food has the biggest effect on it, then poor sleep and stress.

Reference range: below 10 mg/L (varies by lab) · sensitive cut-offs 1 and 3 mg/L · GP: regular CRP or ESR (another measure of inflammation), not the sensitive hs-CRP

Testosterone and SHBG (men)

Testosterone is the main male sex hormone. It plays a part in your muscles, your bones and your sex drive. Most testosterone in your blood is bound to SHBG (sex hormone binding globulin), a protein from your liver. While bound, it can't reach your cells. Only the small free part can.

In 21,074 men, testosterone barely differed between the ages of 17 and 70. A higher BMI (your weight relative to your height), at most 75 minutes of vigorous exercise a week and diabetes were linked to lower testosterone. In men aged 65 and over with a low level, a year of testosterone treatment didn't improve energy or memory. The guideline advises repeating a low result. Is your SHBG abnormal, or your testosterone near the lower limit? Then ask for free testosterone. I look at the value the lab calculates from your total testosterone and your SHBG: it's more accurate than a direct test, and the guideline names that calculation too.

Reference range: testosterone 8.6-29 nmol/L (men) · SHBG varies by lab · GP: not standard
