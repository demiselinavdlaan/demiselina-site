Full body, 3 days a week: every muscle on time

A ready-to-use plan with sets and reps, a 2-day version, the rule for getting stronger every week, and how to tell you've recovered. With the research behind every step.

Many people train each muscle too late. Chest on Monday, and the next chest session a week later: 72 hours or more in between. You do train, but you make less progress than you could, and you spend a lot of time in the gym for it. I used to train in splits myself, with different muscles on different days, one and a half to two hours a session. The window is shorter: about 48 hours. Once a muscle has recovered, that's where I want its next session. That's why the training has to be full body, and it has to fit with your cardio. Below is the full-body week I build for clients, ready to use: three days, or two if that's what you have. Then why it works, how to get a little stronger every week, and how to tell whether you've recovered. Under every step is the research, including where it's weak.

Part 1

Your full-body week

Ready to use. Three days, or two.

Your week

Three strength sessions, each for your whole body, with a day in between. That puts about 48 hours between two sessions for the same muscle, and 72 over the weekend. Your cardio goes on the days in between. Zone 2 is easy endurance training: a heart rate low enough that you can still talk.

Monday: workout A

Strength, full body. 45 to 60 minutes.

Tuesday: zone 2

45 to 60 minutes: cycling, an uphill walk or an easy run.

Wednesday: workout B

Strength, full body. 45 to 60 minutes.

Thursday: zone 2

45 to 60 minutes.

Friday: workout C

Strength, full body. 45 to 60 minutes.

Saturday: 4x4 intervals or a sport

Intervals are blocks of hard work with easy blocks in between; see part 2. Padel or tennis can take their place.

Sunday: rest

A walk is always fine.

How each session works

A rep is one movement, up and down. A set is a run of reps without a break. 3 × 6-10 means 3 sets of 6 to 10 reps. Reps in reserve are the reps you could still have done at the end of a set.

  1. Warm up with 5 minutes of easy cardio, then 1 or 2 light sets of your first exercise.
  2. Do the exercises in the order given. The big ones, which move several joints, come first.
  3. Pick a weight with which you reach the reps with 1 to 3 reps in reserve. The last reps feel hard, and your form stays the same.
  4. Rest 2 to 3 minutes between sets of the first two exercises, and 1 to 2 minutes for the rest.
  5. Write down the weight and reps of every set. Part 2 tells you when to go heavier.

The American College of Sports Medicine advises doing big exercises before small ones. In 21 trained men who did three full-body sessions a week for 8 weeks, resting 3 minutes between sets built more strength and more thigh muscle than resting 1 minute.

Monday: workout A

About 16 sets. Every big muscle group gets work: legs, glutes, chest, back, shoulders and core. By core I mean the muscles of your stomach, your sides and your lower back.

Squat: 3 × 6-10

Legs and glutes. Sit down as if onto a low chair, with a barbell on your back or a dumbbell held at your chest (goblet squat).

Bench press: 3 × 6-10

Chest, front of your shoulders and triceps (the back of your upper arm). With a barbell or two dumbbells.

Seated cable row: 3 × 8-12

Back and biceps. Sit, pull the handle to your stomach and squeeze your shoulder blades together.

Romanian deadlift: 3 × 8-12

Hamstrings (the back of your thighs), glutes and lower back. Knees slightly bent, push your hips back with the weight close to your legs until you feel a stretch, then stand up.

Lateral raise: 2 × 12-15

Side of your shoulders. Raise two light dumbbells out to the side, up to shoulder height.

Plank: 2 × 30-45 seconds

Core. Hold your body in a straight line on your forearms and toes.

Wednesday: workout B

About 17 sets, with other exercises for the same muscle groups.

Leg press: 3 × 8-12

Legs and glutes. Push the platform away with your whole foot.

Lat pulldown or pull-up: 3 × 6-10

Back and biceps. Pull the bar down to your upper chest, or pull yourself up to a bar.

Dumbbell shoulder press: 3 × 8-12

Shoulders and triceps. Press two dumbbells overhead, seated or standing.

Hip thrust: 3 × 8-12

Glutes and hamstrings. Upper back against a bench, weight on your hips, push your hips up until your body is straight.

Leg curl: 2 × 10-15

Hamstrings. On a machine, bend your knees against the pad.

Push-ups or dips: 3 × 8-15

Chest and triceps. Can't do push-ups yet? Do them on your knees or with your hands on a bench.

Friday: workout C

About 16 sets, partly with one leg or one arm at a time.

Bulgarian split squat: 3 × 8-12 per leg

Legs and glutes. A lunge with your back foot on a bench behind you.

Incline dumbbell press: 3 × 8-12

Upper chest and shoulders. Press two dumbbells up on a bench set at an angle.

One-arm dumbbell row: 3 × 8-12 per arm

Back and biceps. One hand and knee on a bench, pull the dumbbell to your hip.

Back extension: 3 × 10-15

Lower back, glutes and hamstrings. On a back extension bench, bend forward at your hips and come up until your body is straight.

Face pull: 2 × 12-15

Back of your shoulders and upper back. Pull a rope on the cable towards your face, elbows high.

Dead bug: 2 × 10-12 per side

Core. On your back, arms and legs up. Slowly lower one arm and the opposite leg, keeping your lower back on the floor.

Only two days a week?

Then do workout A and workout B, with as many days in between as you can: Monday and Thursday, or Tuesday and Friday. Each muscle is still trained twice a week. Add one set to the first three exercises of each workout, 4 instead of 3, so your weekly number of sets stays close to the 3-day week. Put zone 2 on two other days, if you can.

In 10 studies, training a muscle twice a week built more muscle than once a week. Whether three times beats twice is still unclear. The plan works the same for women: in 10 studies, women and men gained the same amount of muscle relative to where they started, and women gained relatively more upper-body strength.

What I use myself

This is what I use every week. I don't name brands. For the supplement, read the line at the bottom first.

A wearable, every morning

First I write down how I think I slept. Then I look at my HRV and my resting heart rate from the night. My lowest heart rate at night was 42 to 43 beats a minute, as a monthly average from April to September 2026. Part 3 explains what both numbers tell you.

Zone 2 and one interval session a week

I train at least 240 minutes a week in zone 2, plus Norwegian 4x4 intervals once a week on an airbike, an exercise bike with moving handles. I'm training for a 50 km ultra in Dubai.

A walking pad under my desk

My goal is 30,000 steps a day, sometimes with a 10 kg weighted vest.

Creatine: 5 g a day

Creatine is a substance your muscles and brain use to make energy fast. I take 5 g a day; the time of day doesn't matter. EFSA, the European food safety authority, says up to 3 g a day is unlikely to pose any risk, and 3 g is also the amount behind the EU's approved claim for short, intense exercise. Above 3 g there's too little research to say whether it's safe. If you have kidney problems, don't take it.

Never start without data. The wrong supplement can make symptoms worse, and without measuring, most of it is guesswork. Do you take medication, are you pregnant or do you have kidney problems? Check with your GP or pharmacist first.

Part 2

Why it works

Five findings first, then the evidence per step, including where it's weak.

What almost nobody knows

Five findings about training and recovery, each with its number and what to do with it.

28 hours

In a trained muscle, the extra building was over by 28 hours

Muscle protein synthesis is how a muscle builds new protein after training, to repair and grow. Ten young men trained one leg for 8 weeks. After a new session, building rose in both legs. In the trained leg it was back to its resting level by 28 hours; in the untrained leg it was still raised. The more trained the muscle, the shorter that window seems to be. Train a muscle once a week, and the extra building has long stopped before the next session.

Study of 10 young men

48 hours

Training to failure slowed recovery for up to 48 hours

Failure is the point where you can't finish another rep. Ten trained men did bench press and squats in three ways. After sets to failure, their recovery was still slower 24 to 48 hours later than after stopping each set halfway, even with the same total work. The researchers saw it in their speed, their jump height and markers in their blood. In this plan you stop each set with 1 to 3 reps in reserve, so the same muscle is ready again sooner.

Study of 10 trained men

60%

Light weights built as much muscle as heavy ones

Your one-rep max is the most weight you can lift once. In 21 studies, weights of 60% of that or less built as much muscle as heavier weights, as long as every set went to failure. Heavy weights did give more maximal strength. So training at home with light dumbbells works for muscle, as long as the last reps are really hard. If you want to get stronger, go heavier.

21 studies

43

Cardio didn't cost muscle in 43 studies

Concurrent training means strength and cardio in the same week. In 43 studies, adding cardio didn't reduce muscle growth or maximal strength. Only explosive strength, such as how high you jump, rose less, mainly when cardio and weights were in the same session instead of at least 3 hours apart. So keep your cardio, and give it its own day.

43 studies

18%

One night without sleep cut muscle building by 18%

13 healthy young adults stayed awake for one night. The next afternoon, their muscle building after a meal was 18% lower than after a normal night. Their cortisol, a stress hormone, was 21% higher and their testosterone 24% lower. Testosterone is the main male sex hormone; women make it too, in smaller amounts. Sleeping badly? Then sleep is the first thing to fix, before you add training.

Study of 13 adults

Why every muscle again within about 48 hours

Muscle protein synthesis is how a muscle builds new protein, to repair and grow. Training switches it on, and it doesn't stay on for a week. That's why timing counts:

  1. A hard session switches muscle building on within hours.
  2. About a day later it is at its highest, roughly double the normal rate.
  3. After a day and a half to two days it is close to normal again. The more trained the muscle, the sooner.
  4. Train that muscle again around then, and a new rise begins. Wait a week, and the days in between add nothing extra.

In 6 young men, muscle building in the trained arm was more than double at 24 hours and almost back to normal at 36 hours. In 8 untrained people it was still 34% higher after 48 hours. After 8 weeks of training, a trained leg was back to its resting level by 28 hours. In 10 studies, training a muscle twice a week built more muscle than once a week. There's a catch: in 25 studies, once the number of sets per week was equal, how often people trained made little difference to muscle growth. A 2026 analysis of 67 studies with 2,058 people found the same for muscle, while strength did rise with more sessions a week. Split or full body made no difference in 14 studies, again with equal sets. So the number of hard sets a week counts at least as much as how often you train. Full body three times a week makes it easy to fit those sets into short sessions, with every muscle back on time.

The progression rule: reps first, then weight

Progressive overload means asking a little more of a muscle over time: more weight, more reps or more sets. If you lift the same weight for the same reps every week, your body gets no new reason to adapt. The rule in this plan is called double progression: first your reps go up, then your weight.

  1. Every exercise has a rep range, such as 6 to 10.
  2. Start with a weight you can lift for the bottom of that range, with 1 to 3 reps in reserve.
  3. Each session, try to add a rep with the same weight.
  4. Reach the top of the range on every set with good form? Then go 2 to 10% heavier next time, usually the smallest step your gym has, and climb back up through the range.
  5. Stuck at the same reps for three sessions in a row? Look at your sleep, your food and your recovery before you add training.

The American College of Sports Medicine advises going 2 to 10% heavier once you can do 1 to 2 reps more than your target. In 43 people with at least a year of training, adding reps with the same weight built as much muscle over 8 weeks as adding weight; strength rose slightly more with weight. More sets went with more growth: in 15 studies, each extra set a week added about 0.4% to the gain in muscle size. This plan gives each big muscle group about 9 to 11 hard sets a week. How close to failure? In 15 studies, going all the way to failure gave no clear extra muscle. A later analysis of the research found somewhat more muscle when sets ended closer to failure, with similar strength across a wide range; its authors call it exploratory. That's why you stop with 1 to 3 reps in reserve: close enough to grow, far enough to recover in time.

Cardio on the days in between

In the plan, zone 2 and intervals sit on the days between your strength sessions. Intervals are blocks of hard work with easy blocks in between. Your VO2max is the most oxygen your body can use per minute during an all-out effort.

  1. Zone 2 on Tuesday and Thursday, 45 to 60 minutes, at a pace where you can still talk.
  2. 4x4 intervals on Saturday: 4 minutes at 90 to 95% of your maximum heart rate, then 3 minutes easy at about 70%. Repeat four times.
  3. Cardio and strength on the same day? Keep at least 3 hours between them.

In 43 studies, adding cardio to strength training didn't reduce muscle growth or maximal strength. Explosive strength, such as jumping, rose less, mostly when both were in the same session rather than at least 3 hours apart. Running or cycling made no difference to that. In 40 moderately trained men, 8 weeks of 4x4 intervals raised VO2max by 7%, more than the same amount of work at an easy or medium pace. The WHO advises adults 150 to 300 minutes of moderate exercise a week, or 75 to 150 minutes of hard exercise, and regular muscle strengthening at every age.

Careful. Hard intervals put real strain on your heart. New to hard training, or do you have chest pain, dizziness or a heart condition? Check with your GP first.

Protein: about 1.6 grams per kilo a day

Protein is the building material for new muscle. It's made of amino acids: small building blocks your body puts back together into muscle. Training switches muscle building on, and protein supplies the material. Whatever way you eat, make protein your priority.

  1. Aim for about 1.6 g of protein per kilo of body weight a day. At 75 kg, that's 120 g.
  2. Spread it over 4 meals of about 0.4 g per kilo: around 30 g a meal at 75 kg.
  3. Prefer fewer, bigger meals? Then a big portion of protein isn't wasted.
  4. On days you train, cardio included, your protein stays the same and your carbs go up. I advise at least 2 g of carbs per kilo a day, and 50 to 100 g more on training days.

In 49 studies with 1,863 people doing strength training, extra protein gave a little more muscle and strength. Above about 1.6 g per kilo a day, it added no more muscle. The ISSN, the International Society of Sports Nutrition, calls 1.4 to 2.0 g per kilo a day enough for most people who exercise, also to build muscle, and advises endurance athletes to eat enough carbohydrates. A review of the per-meal research advises 0.4 g per kilo across at least four meals. In Maastricht, young men got 100 or 25 g of protein after a full-body workout. With 100 g, muscle building was larger and lasted over 12 hours. You can get your protein from normal food; a shake is just a practical way to reach your total.

Careful. Do you have a kidney condition? Discuss your protein intake with your GP first.

The basics

You probably know these already. They still count, so here they are in one list.

  • Sleep at least 7 hours a night; that's the advice for adults.
  • New to strength training? Then use 8 to 12 reps on every exercise for your first months. That's the range the American College of Sports Medicine advises for beginners.
  • Learn each movement with a light weight before you go heavy. Filming yourself once helps.
  • Pain in a joint, or a heart condition? See your GP or a physiotherapist first.

Part 3

How you know you've recovered

What your logbook, your body and your wearable tell you.

More training isn't always more progress

After a period of overtraining, my testosterone was 8.8 nmol/L. Just inside the range, so 'normal' on paper. Overtraining means training more than you recover from, for weeks, until your performance drops and the tiredness doesn't go away. Testosterone is the main male sex hormone; women make it too, in smaller amounts. It plays a part in your muscles, your bones and your sex drive.

In a review of 38 studies, the resting hormone levels of overtrained athletes were mostly normal. So a blood test alone won't tell you whether you're overtrained. Your logbook and how you feel tell you more, and so do the numbers below. Always discuss an abnormal result with your GP.

Hormonal aspects of overtraining syndrome: a systematic review.

Four signs you've recovered

Muscle soreness that starts a day or two after training is called DOMS: delayed onset muscle soreness. HRV (heart rate variability) is the small variation in time between your heartbeats. A higher HRV usually means your body has recovered well; it often drops after a short night or a stressful week. Your resting heart rate is how many times your heart beats per minute when you're fully at rest. Read them in this order:

  1. Your logbook. Can you match last session's weight and reps? Then that muscle has recovered enough. Fewer reps two sessions in a row? Then take an extra rest day before you add anything.
  2. Soreness. Mild soreness that fades during your warm-up is no reason to skip a session. How sore you are says little about how much your muscle was damaged.
  3. HRV. Compare it with your own average over the past week. One low night says little.
  4. Resting heart rate. Several mornings in a row clearly above your normal, and you feel flat? Then swap intervals for zone 2, or take a rest day.
  5. Two or three signs together count for more than one number on its own.

In 110 men, soreness after training barely matched other signs of muscle damage, such as loss of strength and swelling. HRV researchers advise looking at averages over several days. Across 11 studies, HRV dropped after sleep loss, and in most of 37 studies it changed with stress. Adjusting training to daily HRV gave a better HRV and fewer people who responded badly, but the gain in fitness was small and uncertain; that research is mostly in endurance training. In competitive athletes, up to 2 weeks of extra-hard training raised resting heart rate moderately. The changes were small, often within normal day-to-day swings, so the researchers advise reading them next to other signs. Night-time resting heart rate and HRV from Oura and WHOOP came close to an ECG (a heart trace) in 13 adults over 536 nights.

Delayed-onset muscle soreness does not reflect the magnitude of eccentric exercise-induced muscle damage.
