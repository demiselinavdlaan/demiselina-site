More fibre, without the bloating

Your gut is like a muscle: you build it up. A 6-week plan, with the research behind every step.

Fibre is the food trend after protein. Many people add a lot of it at once, have a bloated belly within days and give up. Usually they went too fast. I eat more than 50 grams of fibre a day, and it was one of the three changes that brought my LDL cholesterol from 4.35 to 1.42. Right below is a plan to build up in 6 weeks without the bloating. Then why fibre bloats at first, which fibre to start with, and when it's something else. Under every step is the research, including where it's weak.

Part 1

Your 6-week build-up

About 5 grams more each week, one food at a time.

Build it up like training

Fibre is the part of plant food your gut can't digest. The best way to see it: your gut is a muscle. Just like in the gym: if you've never been, you don't do a 20 kg curl in one go; you build up. If you add fibre too fast, you're trying to lift that 20 kg dumbbell while your gut can only handle 12 kg, and everything comes loose. So add about 5 grams a week, one food at a time. Dutch adults eat 21 grams a day on average. Start there, and you're at 50 grams after 6 weeks. Over time you can go to 40 to 60 grams a day. That's above the Dutch Health Council's advice; why I aim there is in part 4.

Week 1: about 25 g

Add 40 g of oats at breakfast: about 4 g of fibre. Your day: oats for breakfast, your usual lunch and dinner.

Week 2: about 30 g

Add 2 green kiwis, at breakfast or as a snack: about 4 to 5 g. Your day: oats with 2 kiwis, your usual lunch and dinner.

Week 3: about 35 g

Add 150 g of cooked carrots or green beans to dinner: about 4 to 5 g. Your day: oats with kiwis, your usual lunch, dinner with an extra portion of cooked vegetables.

Week 4: about 40 g

Add 5 g of psyllium husk, stirred into yoghurt or a large glass of water: about 4 g. Psyllium is the husk of a plant seed that forms a gel with water; read the line about it in part 3 first. Your day: as in week 3, with psyllium in the morning.

Week 5: about 45 g

Add 15 g of chia seeds, a heaped tablespoon, soaked in yoghurt or stirred into your oats: about 5 g. Your day: oats with kiwis and chia, your usual lunch, dinner with extra vegetables, psyllium.

Week 6: about 50 g

Add 60 g of cooked lentils or chickpeas, about 4 tablespoons, to lunch or dinner: about 5 g. Your day: everything above, plus lentils or chickpeas.

After week 6

Stay at this level for a few weeks. Is your gut calm? Then swap white bread, pasta and rice for wholegrain to move towards 60 g.

Why the slow build-up? When 20 healthy people took a fast-fermenting fibre every day, they passed wind 18 times a day at first instead of 12. After 3 weeks it was down to 11, while they kept taking it. Their gut bacteria had adapted. Drink an extra glass of water with every step too: fibre holds water. More on that in part 3.

When your belly reacts

A bit more wind in the first days is normal. In studies it eased within 1 to 3 weeks while people kept eating the same amount. When it's more than a bit, the steps are simple.

  1. Add one food at a time and give it a full week. Then you know what your gut reacts to.
  2. Too much wind, cramps or a tight, bloated belly? Go back one step and stay there for a week. You should notice it gets less. Then build up again.
  3. Still bloated after a week at the lower step? Then the type of fibre may matter more than the amount. Swap the last food you added for another one from this page, such as kiwi for lentils.
  4. Pain, blood, or a change in your stools that stays? That doesn't fit with building up. See part 5.

Part 2

Why fibre bloats at first

What happens in your gut, and why it gets better.

What almost nobody knows

Five findings about fibre and your gut, each with a number and what to do with it.

3 weeks

The extra wind went away while people kept going

Fermentation is how your gut bacteria break down fibre you can't digest yourself. It makes gas. 20 healthy people took 2.8 g a day of a prebiotic: a fibre meant to feed your gut bacteria, which ferments fast. In the first days they passed wind 18 times a day instead of 12. After 3 weeks it was 11, while they still took it every day. Their bacteria had adapted. So a bit more wind at the start is no reason to stop.

Study of 20 people, partly by the maker

6%

Wind from beans: half in week 1, 6% in week 2

In three trials, adults ate half a cup of beans a day, about 90 grams, for 8 to 12 weeks. With pinto beans, half noticed more wind in the first week. In the second week, 6% did. Most people who had more wind felt it was gone by week 2 or 3. With black-eyed beans only 19% noticed anything in week 1. So beans get easier if you keep going.

3 trials with beans

0.7 L

Passing up to 1.5 litres of gas a day is normal

10 healthy people ate their normal diet plus 200 g of baked beans. Over 24 hours they passed 0.5 to 1.5 litres of gas, 0.7 litres in the middle. On a fibre-free liquid diet for 2 days it fell to about 0.2 litres, and the gases from fermentation almost disappeared. So most of your wind comes from bacteria feeding on fibre. Wind on its own isn't a sign that something's wrong.

Study of 10 healthy people

80%

Psyllium cut the gas from inulin by about 80%

Inulin is a fibre from chicory root that's added to many high-fibre products. It ferments fast. Psyllium ferments slowly. In 19 people with IBS (irritable bowel syndrome: a sensitive gut with pain and changing stools), 20 g of inulin filled the large intestine with gas on an MRI scan, a scan that shows the inside of your body. Taken together with 20 g of psyllium, there was about 80% less gas. Check labels for inulin, chicory fibre and oligofructose.

MRI study, 19 people with IBS

57%

In Dutch GP practices, psyllium helped from the first month

Soluble fibre dissolves in water, like psyllium. Insoluble fibre doesn't, like wheat bran. 275 people with IBS took 10 g a day of psyllium, bran or a dummy powder for 12 weeks. In the first month, 57% on psyllium had enough relief, against 35% on the dummy. Bran only did better than the dummy in the third month, and not in every analysis. Most early drop-outs were in the bran group, mainly because their complaints got worse. So go easy on bran at the start.

What fibre does in your gut

Your small intestine absorbs most of what you eat. Fibre passes through and reaches your large intestine, where most of your gut bacteria live. What happens there depends on the type of fibre:

  1. Soluble fibre dissolves in water. Some kinds, as in oats and psyllium, form a gel that holds water and makes your stools softer.
  2. Insoluble fibre doesn't dissolve, as in wheat bran and the skins of seeds. It adds bulk.
  3. Your gut bacteria ferment part of the fibre: they break it down as food. That gives short-chain fatty acids, small fats your body takes up and uses, and gas: mostly hydrogen and carbon dioxide, and in some people methane.
  4. Some fibres ferment fast, such as inulin and the sugar chains in beans, and make a lot of gas in a short time. Others, such as psyllium, ferment slowly and only in part.
  5. Gas stretches your gut. Most people barely feel it. With a sensitive gut you feel it sooner, as bloating, cramps or pain.

A large review of fibre and gut health sums it up: how a fibre behaves depends on how well it dissolves, how thick a gel it forms and how fast it ferments, and that differs by source and processing. In a lab test with stool from people with IBS, inulin made more gas than psyllium. In 16 healthy people, 40 g of inulin clearly filled the large intestine with gas on an MRI scan, yet gave few complaints. So gas is normal; how much you feel it differs from person to person.

FODMAPs: what ferments fastest

FODMAP stands for fermentable oligo-, di- and monosaccharides and polyols: short sugar chains and sugar alcohols that your small intestine absorbs poorly. Not all of them are fibre, but they act in a similar way. They pull water into your small intestine and reach your large intestine fast, where bacteria ferment them quickly. The main groups:

  • Fructans: chains of fructose, in wheat, onion and garlic. Inulin from chicory root is one of them.
  • Galacto-oligosaccharides (GOS): sugar chains in beans, lentils and chickpeas.
  • Lactose: the sugar in milk, for people who don't digest it well.
  • Extra fructose: fruit sugar in fruits that have more of it than glucose, such as apple, pear, mango and watermelon.
  • Polyols: sugar alcohols such as sorbitol, in some fruits and sugar-free sweets.

On an MRI scan, fructose drew water into the small intestine, while inulin filled the large intestine with gas. A diet low in FODMAPs reduces gut complaints in people with IBS, and the complaints came back when the FODMAPs did. That diet is meant for a few months, with a dietitian, to find out what triggers you. Cutting out many foods on your own can leave you short of nutrients, and eating less fibre can even give you more complaints.

Part 3

Which fibre to start with

The gentle ones first, the fast fermenters later.

Start with these

These four come first in the plan. Per food: what it gives you, and how strong the evidence is.

Oats

40 g gives about 4 g of fibre. Oats contain beta-glucan, a soluble fibre that forms a gel. In 28 trials, 3 g of oat beta-glucan a day lowered LDL cholesterol by 0.25 mmol/L. Oats are on this list for that. Whether they give fewer complaints than other grains has hardly been tested.

Green kiwi

Two a day give about 4 to 5 g. In a trial with 184 people in New Zealand, Italy and Japan, 2 green kiwis a day for 4 weeks gave people with constipation 1.5 more complete bowel movements a week and a calmer gut. In a US trial, kiwi gave the fewest side effects of kiwi, prunes and psyllium, and the kiwi group reported less bloating.

Cooked vegetables

150 g of cooked carrots or green beans gives about 4 to 5 g. Start with vegetables such as carrots, green beans, courgette and spinach: cabbage, onion and garlic give some people more gas. Cooking makes vegetables softer. When 29 vegetables were steamed, part of the insoluble fibre turned soluble in 18 of them, and the fructans stayed. So cooking doesn't remove FODMAPs; which vegetable you choose counts.

A small amount of psyllium

Psyllium husk is the husk of a plant seed. 85% of it is soluble fibre: it holds water, forms a gel and ferments slowly. Start with 5 g, about 4 g of fibre. In the Dutch GP trial, 10 g a day helped people with IBS. In the US trial, 12 g a day gave more side effects than kiwi or prunes. So start small.

Never start without data. The wrong amount of psyllium can make your complaints worse. Take at least 30 ml of water per gram, so 150 ml for 5 g, and an extra glass after. Not just before bed, and 30 to 60 minutes apart from medicines, because it can slow how your body takes them up. On thyroid or diabetes medication? Talk to your GP first. Trouble swallowing? Don't take it.

Hold these back for now

These ferment fast, or often give complaints at the start. You don't have to avoid them for good. Add them from week 6, one at a time, in small amounts.

Beans, lentils and chickpeas

Rich in fibre and in GOS, sugar chains that ferment fast. With half a cup of beans a day, up to half the people noticed more wind in week 1; most found it was gone by week 2 or 3. Start with 3 to 4 tablespoons.

Chicory root fibre (inulin)

Added to many high-fibre breads, bars, yoghurts and shakes. On the label: inulin, chicory fibre, oligofructose or FOS. In 26 healthy adults, up to 10 g of inulin a day was well tolerated, but 10 g of oligofructose, a short-chain form, clearly gave more wind and bloating.

Wheat bran

Insoluble fibre. In the Dutch GP trial, most early drop-outs were in the bran group, mainly because their complaints got worse.

Cabbage, onion and garlic

They give some people gas and pain, as do spicy food and nuts. They're healthy, so only cut down for a few weeks if you notice it every time.

Drink more as you go

Fibre holds water: psyllium can take up to 40 times its own weight. Drink too little, and more fibre can make constipation worse.

  1. Drink 1.5 to 2 litres a day, about 6 to 10 glasses, mostly water or tea without sugar.
  2. Add one extra glass of water with every step of the plan, and an extra glass after psyllium.

In a trial, 117 people with constipation ate about 25 g of fibre a day for two months. Both groups went to the toilet more often. The group told to drink 2 litres of water a day drank 2.1 litres on average, against 1.1 in the other group, and improved clearly more: more bowel movements and fewer laxatives.

Part 4

My own 50 grams

What fibre did for my LDL, and what I do myself.

From 4.35 to 1.42

LDL cholesterol is a blood value for your heart: LDL is the particle that carries cholesterol through your blood, and a high LDL is linked to heart and vascular disease. In February 2024 mine was 4.35 mmol/L. In January 2026 it was 1.42. I changed three things at once: more than 50 grams of fibre a day, only lean steak, and 120 minutes of zone 2 a week, easy endurance training at a pace where you can still talk. So I don't know how much of the drop came from fibre.

Soluble fibre works through your bile, the fluid your liver makes from cholesterol to digest fat. Soluble fibre holds on to bile in your gut, so more of it leaves your body, and your liver takes cholesterol from your blood to make new bile. Per gram the effect is small. In 67 trials, each gram of soluble fibre lowered LDL by about 0.06 mmol/L; 3 g of soluble fibre from oats, about 84 g of oats, lowered it by about 0.13. Psyllium lowered LDL by about 7% in people with mildly to moderately raised cholesterol. Why aim for 40 to 60 grams? In 185 long-term studies, the people who ate the most fibre had a 15 to 30% lower risk of dying and of heart and vascular disease than those who ate the least. The benefit was clearest at 25 to 29 grams a day, and the curves suggested that more could help more. The Dutch Health Council advises 3.0 to 3.5 grams per megajoule, a unit of energy of about 240 kcal: about 25 to 29 grams if you eat 2,000 kcal a day, and 32 to 37 at 2,500. The Netherlands Nutrition Centre says more isn't needed. My 50 grams is well above that. Still, in my view fibre has the highest return.

Part 5

When it isn't the fibre

Signs to take to your GP.

See your GP if you notice this

Wind and some bloating while you build up are normal. The signs below don't fit with that. Make an appointment with your GP if you notice one or more of them:

  • Blood in or on your stools.
  • You suddenly go much more or much less often than you're used to, or your stools are suddenly much thinner or much harder.
  • You often feel you need to go, but nothing comes.
  • You lose weight without knowing why.
  • You're 50 or older and have bowel complaints for the first time.
  • Pain that doesn't go away or slowly gets worse, or a fever of 38 °C or higher.
  • Bowel disease runs in your family.

Call your GP or the out-of-hours GP service straight away with sudden severe pain, pain that keeps getting worse, black and sticky stools, or blood from your back passage. Your GP can also check for coeliac disease, an inflammation of the gut caused by gluten, a protein in wheat, barley and rye, with a blood or stool test. Do several foods give you complaints? Don't cut them all out on your own: a dietitian can work out with you what triggers it. Always discuss complaints that stay with your GP.
