Wake up rested every morning

Why you wake up tired, even after 8 hours of sleep, and my evening and morning routine. With the research behind every step.

Sleep sits at the bottom of my pyramid: first sleep, then food, then movement. Yet many entrepreneurs tell me the same thing: I sleep 8 hours and still wake up tired. There's often a reason you can see in your data and change. This page starts with five things almost nobody knows. Then comes your schedule: pick your wake-up time and you'll see what to do when. Under every step is the research, including where it's weak.

What almost nobody knows

Five findings you rarely hear, each with its number and what to do with it.

6 min

30 minutes of snoozing cost only 6 minutes of sleep

31 people who usually snooze slept in a sleep lab. After 30 minutes of snoozing, they did as well or better on thinking tests right after getting up. Snoozing kept them from being woken out of deep sleep, the stage in which you sleep most deeply and are hardest to wake. Do you snooze? Then you don't need to feel bad about it. Only 30 minutes was tested.

Study of 31 habitual snoozers

2 hours

A week of camping set the body clock 2 hours earlier

Your body clock is the roughly 24-hour rhythm in your brain that decides when you get sleepy and when you wake up. After a week with only natural light, people's melatonin started about 2 hours earlier. Melatonin is the hormone that tells your body it's night. Night owls shifted most. In their first 2 hours awake they got over 3 times as much light, measured in lux: a candle at 1 metre gives 1 lux, a normal room about 200. So get outside early, and keep your lamps low after sunset.

Study of 8 campers

100 lux

One night with a dimmed lamp on: higher heart rate, lower HRV

HRV (heart rate variability) is the small variation in time between your heartbeats. A higher HRV usually means your body has recovered well; it drops after a short night, alcohol or stress. Healthy adults who slept one night at 100 lux, about a dimmed room lamp, had a higher heart rate and lower HRV than in a dark room, and the next morning their body responded less well to insulin, the hormone that moves sugar from your blood into your cells. Make your room so dark you can't see your hand in front of your face.

Lab study in healthy adults

2 drinks

Even 1 or 2 drinks lowered recovery in the first 3 hours of sleep

Finnish employees wore a heart rate monitor on days with and without alcohol. Their recovery, a percentage the researchers worked out from HRV, fell by 9 points after a little alcohol (for an 80 kg man, up to about 2 standard drinks), 24 after a moderate amount and 39 after a lot. Being young or active didn't protect them. If you test one thing with your wearable (a ring, watch or band that tracks your heart rate), test this.

4,098 employees

50-65%

Wearables get your sleep stages right only 50 to 65% of the time

Sleep stages are the phases you go through every night: light sleep, deep sleep and REM sleep (dream sleep, in which your brain is active and your muscles lie still). Compared with a sleep lab, six wearables, including Oura and WHOOP, told asleep from awake 86 to 89% of the time; the right stage only 50 to 65% of the time. So don't let a low deep sleep score decide your morning. How long you slept and your night-time HRV are more reliable.

Study of 53 adults

Part 1

Your evening and morning

Pick your wake-up time. I'll work out your evening.

Your schedule

This is my 10-3-2-1 rule and my morning, in your times. The idea behind it: in the evening I bring light, body temperature and heart rate down, and in the morning I push all three up. That tells your body when it's night and when it's day. I work out your bedtime from your wake-up time: 7.5 hours of sleep plus 15 minutes to fall asleep. That's five sleep cycles of about 90 minutes. In each cycle you go from light sleep, where you still wake easily, to deep sleep and then REM sleep. How long a cycle lasts differs from person to person, so 7.5 hours is a rule of thumb.

So you go to bed at
  1. No more caffeineTea, dark chocolate, energy drinks and pre-workout (gym powder with caffeine) count too.
  2. No more food or hard trainingYour last meal goes here, and earlier is better. Eat between 5 and 6 pm and you're already ahead of most people. A gentle walk is still fine.
  3. Stop working and drinkingLaptop shut, chats off, glass away. Your mind needs time to land, like a plane. Set an app blocker, an app that locks your other apps, to switch on now. Then it doesn't come down to willpower.
  4. No more bright lightLamps low and the ceiling light off, no screen close to your eyes. Need light? A small red clip-on book light is enough. Now I take magnesium L-threonate, a form of the mineral magnesium, and glycine, an amino acid (more on both below). Then I put on red-lensed glasses and watch something light, like a nature documentary.
  5. Wind downI lie on an acupressure mat, a mat full of small plastic points, and listen to Stoic quotes: short lines from ancient Greek and Roman thinkers about what lies outside your control.
  6. Lights outAs dark as you can get it: blackout foil on the window, tape over the small lights on devices, and no clock in sight.
  7. Get up, same time every dayWeekends too. That keeps your body clock steady.
  8. No phoneMy first 15 to 20 minutes are phone-free, with something positive to listen to.
  9. Light and cold waterOutside or by a bright lamp. Then 30 seconds of cold water in the shower.
  10. Move and breathe30 kettlebell swings (a kettlebell is a heavy iron ball with a handle), push-ups and stretching. Then 50 fast, deep breaths (Wim Hof style) while I run through my day and what is in my control today. Sitting down, never in water, in the shower or while driving.
  11. Write down your sleepFirst how you think you slept, only then your HRV.

Change the time and your plan updates. Nothing is saved.

Shift work, or a different wake-up time every day? Fill in the time you'll get up after the night ahead. Then the steps fall before the sleep you're actually going to get.

What I use myself

This is what I use every day. The tools you can get today. For the supplements, read the line at the bottom first.

Red-lensed glasses

For the last hour before bed. Red lenses block the blue part of light, the part your body clock reacts to most. The evidence is mixed; I felt calmer from the first week. The glasses are no reason to keep answering emails: work keeps your head busy, glasses or not.

An acupressure mat

15 to 30 minutes, half an hour before bed. Since I started, my wearable shows me falling asleep in about 5 minutes instead of 10. In a study people felt less stressed with a mat, and relaxing without one worked just as well. For me it's a fixed signal that the day is done.

No alarm and no clock in the bedroom

I can't see what time it is, so I don't lie there worrying about it. That only works once your rhythm is steady.

Light therapy glasses

Glasses with small built-in lamps that shine bright light towards your eyes. For dark mornings.

Magnesium L-threonate: about 400 mg of magnesium

60 minutes before bed. Magnesium is a mineral your muscles and nerves need. In a supplement it's always bound to another substance; that's the form on the label. I mainly take magnesium L-threonate. In rats, this form in particular raised the amount of magnesium in the brain; in people that's still barely studied. For the evening I skip citrate and oxide, the forms most shops sell. I take about 1.5 g of powder, with about 400 mg of pure magnesium in it. That's above the 250 mg a day that EFSA, the European food safety authority, and the Netherlands Nutrition Centre give as the safe upper limit for magnesium from supplements. Above that, some people get diarrhoea.

Glycine: 10 g

60 minutes before bed. Glycine is an amino acid, a building block of protein. I take 10 g, about 2 tablespoons of powder. That's a lot more than the 3 g in the sleep studies. It's also more than the 1.4 g a day that the Danish and Norwegian food safety agencies call safe from supplements. They based that limit on a rat study, with a wide safety margin. Harm in people hasn't been shown. Then again, nobody has tested 10 g for safety.

Caffeine: 30 to 60 mg a day

In the morning, from capsules, so I know exactly how much it is. I used to drink more than eight cups of coffee a day; now I drink decaf. EFSA sees no safety concern up to 400 mg a day for healthy adults (200 mg if you're pregnant). For sleep, 100 mg in one go can already affect how long and how well some adults sleep, especially close to bedtime.

Creatine: 5 g a day

In the morning. Creatine is a substance your muscles and brain use to make energy fast. I take 5 g a day; the time of day doesn't matter. EFSA says up to 3 g a day is unlikely to pose any risk, and 3 g is also the amount behind the EU's approved claim for short, intense exercise. Above 3 g there's too little research to say whether it's safe. If you have kidney problems, don't take it.

A wearable

That's how I see each morning whether my evening worked.

The evidence for both evening supplements is weak. In 3 small trials, older adults with poor sleep fell asleep 17 minutes faster with magnesium; the researchers rated the quality as low. In a trial paid for by the maker, 100 adults took 2 g of magnesium L-threonate a day (145 mg of magnesium) for 6 weeks. Their ring showed no better sleep. Their night-time heart rate dropped more and their HRV rose more than with a placebo, a dummy capsule. For glycine there are only small studies, also from a maker. In one, 3 g before bed meant less tiredness the next day after short nights.

Never start without data. The wrong supplement can make symptoms worse, and without measuring, most of it is guesswork. Do you take medication, are you pregnant or do you have kidney problems? Check with your GP or pharmacist first.

Part 2

Why it works

The evidence per step, including where it's weak.

The 10-3-2-1 rule: how strong is the evidence?

Why count back from your bedtime? You get your deepest sleep early in the night: in a sleep lab, 20 healthy men got 5 times as much deep sleep in the first half of the night as in the second. So what you do in the evening carries over into the part of the night with the most deep sleep. Here's how strong the evidence is for each step:

10 hours before bed: no more caffeine

Strong evidence. While you're awake, adenosine builds up in your brain, a substance that makes you sleepy. That build-up is called sleep pressure. Caffeine sits where adenosine should, so you feel less sleepy while the sleep pressure stays. Across 24 studies, caffeine cost 45 minutes of sleep on average and some deep sleep; a normal coffee needs at least 8.8 hours, a pre-workout over 13. EFSA says 100 mg, about 2 small cups of coffee, can already change sleep in some adults when taken close to bedtime. Dark chocolate has a little too: 2 squares hold about 7 mg.

3 hours before bed: no more food or hard training

Food: weak and mixed evidence. In 793 students, eating within 3 hours of bed went with waking more often; in a lab study, dinner 1 hour before bed left sleep stages unchanged, though blood sugar was higher at night. What I see in client data: after a late meal, the heart rate is often 5 to 10 beats per minute higher when they go to sleep. Training: across 23 studies, evening exercise didn't harm sleep. Only hard training that ended less than 1 hour before bedtime seemed to disturb it. I keep 3 hours for both.

2 hours before bed: no more work or drinking

Habit, no trial. I see it as landing a plane: after work, your mind needs time to come down. Drinking less in the evening is standard advice against night-time toilet trips, without a trial in healthy adults.

1 hour before bed: no more bright light

Supported. Ordinary room light (under 200 lux) in the hours before bed made melatonin start later in 99% of 116 people, and cut the time their body made melatonin by about 90 minutes. Where the light comes from may matter too. In 9 people, light on the lower half of the eye lowered melatonin; that's where light from above lands. Light on the upper half did no more than darkness. A later study didn't find that difference, so lamps low is a small extra.

Your first half hour

Sleep inertia is the groggy, slow feeling just after you wake up: your brain is still half in sleep mode. It's strongest when you're woken from deep sleep, and after a few short nights, because you then get more deep sleep. At the same time your cortisol rises sharply. Cortisol is a hormone your body makes in a daily rhythm, and also under stress. In the morning I do the opposite of my evening: light, heart rate and body temperature go up. This is how I use that half hour:

  1. Give it 30 minutes. Without major sleep loss the grogginess rarely lasts longer, so don't judge your night by your first few minutes.
  2. Get light: outside if you can, otherwise by a bright lamp. On dark mornings I wear light therapy glasses.
  3. Take a short cold shower. For me, 30 seconds is enough.

Office workers who got a lot of light in the morning fell asleep faster at night, especially in winter. In over 400,000 people in the UK, each extra hour outdoors during the day went with getting up more easily and feeling tired less often. These are links; they don't prove cause. In a Dutch trial of 3,018 people, ending a warm shower with 30 to 90 seconds of cold cut sick days off work by 29%, though people weren't ill less often. How your cortisol should run in the morning, and what stress does to it, Demi explains in her guide.

The basics

You probably know these already. They still count, so here they are in one list.

  • Sleep at least 7 hours. After 14 nights of 6 hours, people performed as badly as after up to 2 nights without sleep, and barely noticed.
  • Get up at the same time every day, weekends too. The gap between workdays and weekends is called social jetlag; in 61 students, irregular sleepers' body clocks ran about 2.5 hours later.
  • Keep your bedroom cool; we advise 16 to 18 °C. In research, heat meant more time awake and less deep and REM sleep.
  • No screen in bed. Reading on a screen meant falling asleep later and a groggier morning than with a paper book.
  • Alcohol helps you fall asleep faster; then the second half of the night gets more restless and your first REM sleep comes later.
  • Waking briefly at night is normal, and it happens more as you get older.
  • Do you snore, or does your partner hear your breathing stop? That can be sleep apnoea: your throat keeps closing for a moment while you sleep, 5 times an hour or more. Discuss it with your GP; only a sleep study can show whether you have it.

Part 3

How you know it's working

What your wearable shows, and how to read it.

How to read your wearable

A wearable is a ring, watch or band you wear day and night. It measures your heart rate and movement and estimates your sleep from those; it doesn't measure your brain, as a sleep lab does. Every morning I look at two numbers from the night: my HRV and my resting heart rate, how many times your heart beats per minute when you're completely still. This is how to read them:

  1. First write down how you slept, from 1 to 10. Only then look at your data.
  2. Look at when your heart rate drops. I like to see it drop early in the night and stay low. If it only drops just before you wake, your body spent much of the night still working. With clients I often see that after alcohol or a late meal.
  3. Deep sleep comes mostly in the first half of the night, REM sleep in the second half. Do you see little deep sleep and a high heart rate early in the night? Then I look at the evening first. Treat the score as a rough sign: wearables often get your sleep stages wrong; see the 50 to 65% above.
  4. Compare averages over one to two weeks, never a single night, and only with yourself on the same device. Your HRV goes up and down from day to day, and two brands can show a different amount of deep sleep for the same night.
  5. Test one change at a time, such as a week without alcohol. Does it work? Keep it and take the next.

Night-time resting heart rate and HRV from Oura and WHOOP came close to an ECG (a heart trace) in a study of 13 adults over 536 nights. HRV researchers advise looking at an average over several days.
