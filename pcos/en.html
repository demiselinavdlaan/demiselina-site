Hormone imbalance and PCOS

What PCOS is, which signs go with it, and why one high value doesn't mean PCOS.

My symptoms came back every cycle, so for a long time I thought it was hormonal. In May 2026 there was an arrow next to my testosterone: 2.04, above the lab's range. In September it was 0.74, back to normal. So one high value doesn't mean you have PCOS. PCOS is common, though: about 1 in 8 women has it, and since May 2026 it's called PMOS. Here you'll read what it is, which signs go with it and what you can have measured. Only a doctor can tell whether you have it.

What almost nobody knows

PMOS

PCOS got a new name in May 2026

The new name is polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome. Polyendocrine means several hormone systems are involved. Metabolic means your metabolism is involved too: how your body handles sugar and fat. The word 'cysts' is gone, because the little sacs on the ultrasound aren't cysts. More than 14,000 people with PCOS and health professionals had their say through surveys. The switch takes time, so your doctor may still say PCOS.

Global consensus, The Lancet 2026

2 of 3

You need 2 of 3 features for PCOS

The three features are an irregular cycle, too many androgens (hormones such as testosterone) and many small follicles on the ultrasound or a high AMH, a hormone those follicles make. A doctor rules out other causes first. If you have an irregular cycle and too many androgens, the guideline says you don't even need an ultrasound.

International guideline 2023

43%

Almost half of women with PCOS have acne

Across 60 studies, 43% of women with PCOS had acne, against 21% of women without. Androgens make your oil glands produce more sebum, the oily layer on your skin. Still, the guideline calls acne on its own a fairly weak sign of too many androgens in your blood. A lot of hair growth in places like your chin or belly tells you more.

60 studies, 240,213 women with PCOS

75%

Insulin resistance is common, even if you're lean

Insulin resistance means your cells respond less well to insulin, the hormone that helps sugar move from your blood into your cells. In a small study, 75% of lean women with PCOS had it, measured with the most precise test: a clamp, where you get insulin and sugar through a drip. Even so, the guideline advises against fasting insulin and HOMA-IR as routine tests, because they're of little use in practice. HOMA-IR is a calculation from your fasting insulin and glucose. For your blood sugar, the guideline calls a glucose tolerance test (OGTT) the most accurate: you drink sugar water and your blood sugar is measured afterwards.

Part 1

What is PCOS, really?

How your hormones and insulin fuel each other

PCOS is a hormonal and metabolic condition: it's about your hormones and about how your body handles sugar and fat. Your pituitary, a gland below your brain, makes LH and FSH. FSH makes follicles grow, the small sacs in your ovaries that each hold an egg. LH prompts your ovaries to make hormones, including testosterone. Insulin, a hormone from your pancreas, helps sugar move from your blood into your cells. In insulin resistance your cells respond less well to insulin, so your pancreas makes more of it.

  1. Your brain sends the LH signal more often, while FSH lags behind.
  2. High LH and high insulin push your ovaries to make more testosterone.
  3. High insulin slows the production of SHBG in your liver. SHBG is the protein that holds testosterone in your blood. With less SHBG, more free, active testosterone is left.
  4. More testosterone makes your brain less sensitive to progesterone, the hormone that normally slows the LH signal. So LH stays high and the cycle keeps itself going.

It works the other way round too: in PCOS, androgens add to insulin resistance. The guideline also advises having your blood sugar and blood fats measured in PCOS, whatever your weight.

Part 2

How do you know if it's PCOS?

Two of three features

Normally one follicle grows each cycle until it bursts and releases an egg: that's ovulation. In PCOS many follicles stay small. On an ultrasound they look like little sacs, which is why 'cystic' was in the old name. They aren't cysts. According to the 2023 international guideline, you have PCOS if you have 2 of these 3 features and a doctor has ruled out other causes.

  1. An irregular cycle. In adults that's a cycle that's often shorter than 21 or longer than 35 days, or fewer than 8 periods a year. Ovulation can also fail with a regular cycle; a low progesterone shows that.
  2. Too many androgens: hormones such as testosterone, often called 'male' but made by women too. You can see it as hirsutism (a lot of hair growth in places such as the chin, chest or belly), as acne or thinning hair, or in your blood.
  3. Many small follicles on the ultrasound: 20 or more in one ovary. Or a high AMH, the hormone those small follicles make. AMH only counts in adults, and never as the only test.

Other causes your doctor rules out first include your thyroid, high prolactin (a hormone from your pituitary) and your adrenal glands.

Do you recognise these signs?

Choose no, not sure or yes for each question. The questions cover the signs the guideline names. If you use hormonal contraception, think back to before you started.

  1. Your cycle is often shorter than 21 or longer than 35 days.

  2. You have fewer than 8 periods a year, or your period has stayed away for months without you being pregnant.

  3. A doctor saw lots of small 'cysts' or sacs on your ovaries on an ultrasound.

  4. You have more hair growth than you'd like in places such as your chin, upper lip, chest or belly.

  5. You have acne, even as an adult.

  6. Your hair is getting thinner, especially on top of your head or along your parting.

  7. You put on weight easily, especially around your belly.

  8. One of your parents, brothers or sisters has type 2 diabetes.

Part 3

What if one value is too high?

My testosterone: high in May, normal in September

For years I had everything tested: my blood, my DNA, my hormones and my gut, including at clinics in the US. It cost more than €10,000, and nobody could help me. In May 2026 there was an arrow next to my testosterone.

  1. May 2026: testosterone 2.04 nmol/L, above the range of 0.29 to 1.67. My SHBG, the protein that holds testosterone in your blood, was 39.2: within range, but on the low side.
  2. My FAI was 5.2, just within range. The FAI (free androgen index) is your testosterone divided by your SHBG, times 100. My DHEA-S, an androgen from your adrenal glands, was 8.7, just under the upper limit of 8.8.
  3. September 2026: testosterone 0.74, back within range, and SHBG 55.6.

Too many androgens is only one of the three features, and one result is one moment. Your testosterone shifts with your cycle: in 31 healthy women it was highest around ovulation and stayed higher afterwards. On the pill the result is unreliable, and ranges differ a lot between labs. I still don't know exactly what causes my symptoms. They come and go. Always discuss an abnormal result with your GP, including whether testing again makes sense.

Part 4

What can you have measured?

Which markers are often off in PCOS

In PCOS a few of these markers are often off, rarely all of them. One marker means little without the rest, your symptoms and your cycle.

  1. Androgens. Testosterone is measured as a total and as the free part. The FAI is your testosterone divided by your SHBG, times 100. The guideline wants testosterone measured with LC-MS/MS, an accurate lab method. DHEA-S comes mainly from your adrenal glands.
  2. Your cycle. LH, FSH and AMH show how your ovaries are being steered. You only make progesterone after ovulation, so a low level about a week before your period suggests you didn't ovulate.
  3. Your metabolism. Triglycerides and HDL are fats in your blood, glucose is your blood sugar. In an OGTT you drink sugar water and measure how your blood sugar responds.
  4. Ruling out. TSH (your thyroid), prolactin and 17-OH-progesterone (your adrenal glands) show whether something else is causing your symptoms. If your testosterone is far above the range, your doctor will look for other causes too.

Part 5

Try supplements first?

Myo-inositol, berberine and chasteberry

Many women with PCOS try a supplement first. Myo-inositol is a substance your body makes itself and that is also found in food. Berberine is a bitter substance from plants such as barberry. Chasteberry (vitex) is an extract from the berries of a shrub. A supplement can also have side effects and clash with your medicines.

Berberine

Blocks liver enzymes that clear medicines, so medicines can act more strongly. Not in pregnancy or breastfeeding.

Chasteberry (vitex)

Can affect prolactin and so hide a high level, which is exactly what you want to rule out. Disrupted an IVF cycle in one case report.

Myo-inositol

Few side effects, but the evidence is limited and uncertain.

The international guideline sees little evidence. Inositol 'could be considered', with limited benefit, and for women trying to conceive the guideline calls it experimental. The guideline can't recommend any particular form or amount. If you take inositol or another supplement, tell your doctor.

Never start without data. The wrong supplement can make your symptoms worse: through side effects, an interaction with your medicines, or because it doesn't fit what's going on for you. So test your markers first, then choose with a doctor.
