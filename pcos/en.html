Hormone imbalance and PCOS

How it works, which markers are often off, and why you test first.

About 1 in 8 women has PCOS. Since 2026 it is officially called PMOS: polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome. The new name shows better what it is: a hormonal and metabolic condition. Only a doctor can tell whether you have it. This page helps you understand what is going on and which markers you can have tested.

When is it PCOS?

Your ovaries are two small organs next to your womb. They hold egg cells, each in a follicle: a small sac of fluid. Normally one follicle grows each cycle until it bursts and releases an egg. That is ovulation. In PCOS many follicles often stay small and don't burst. On an ultrasound they look like little sacs, but they aren't cysts, even though the old name says so. According to the international guideline, you have PCOS if you have 2 of these 3 features, after other causes are ruled out:

  1. Irregular or missing periods. This is often a sign that ovulation isn't happening.
  2. Too many androgens: hormones such as testosterone, often called 'male' but made by women too. You can see it as acne or hirsutism (a lot of hair growth in places such as the chin, chest or belly), or in your blood.
  3. Many small follicles on ultrasound, or a high AMH (adults only). AMH is a hormone made by those small follicles: the more follicles, the higher your AMH.

Other causes your doctor rules out first include your thyroid, high prolactin and your adrenal glands. Those markers are further down the list.

How it works

Your hormones work like a chain of signals. The pituitary, a gland below your brain, makes two hormones that steer your ovaries: FSH makes follicles grow, and LH prompts your ovaries to make hormones, including testosterone. Insulin plays a part too. It's a hormone from your pancreas that helps sugar move from your blood into your cells. In insulin resistance your cells respond less well to insulin, so your pancreas makes more of it. In PCOS these two reinforce each other:

  1. Your brain sends the LH signal more often, while FSH lags behind.
  2. High LH and high insulin push your ovaries to make more testosterone.
  3. High insulin makes your liver produce less SHBG, the protein that binds testosterone in your blood. More free, active testosterone is then left.
  4. More testosterone makes your brain less sensitive to progesterone, a hormone that normally slows the LH signal. So LH stays high and the cycle keeps itself going.

Insulin resistance is common in PCOS, even if you're lean. In a study using the most precise measurement, a clamp test, 75% of lean women with PCOS had insulin resistance, and 95% of women with PCOS who were overweight.

Which markers are often off

The typical picture in PCOS. Not everyone has every change, and one marker means little without the rest. The list has four kinds of markers:

  1. Androgens. For testosterone you look at the total and at the free part. The FAI is your testosterone divided by your SHBG. LC-MS/MS is an accurate lab method to measure it. DHEA-S comes mainly from your adrenal glands.
  2. Your cycle. LH, FSH and AMH show how your ovaries are being steered. You only make progesterone after ovulation, so a low level around day 21 means it didn't happen.
  3. Your metabolism. Triglycerides and HDL are fats in your blood, glucose is your blood sugar. In an OGTT you drink sugar water and measure how your blood sugar responds. HOMA-IR is a calculation from your fasting insulin and glucose.
  4. Ruling out. TSH (your thyroid), prolactin and 17-OH-progesterone (your adrenal glands) show whether something else is causing your symptoms.

Supplements: test first

Many women with PCOS try supplements such as myo-inositol, berberine or chasteberry (vitex). Myo-inositol is a substance your body makes itself and that is also found in food. Berberine is a bitter substance from plants such as barberry. Chasteberry is an extract from the berries of a shrub. Supplements aren't harmless:

Berberine

Blocks liver enzymes that clear medicines, so medicines can act more strongly. Not in pregnancy or breastfeeding.

Chasteberry (vitex)

Can affect prolactin and so hide a high level, which is exactly what you want to rule out. Disrupted an IVF cycle in one case report.

Myo-inositol

Few side effects, but the evidence is limited and uncertain.

The international guideline sees little evidence: inositol 'could be considered', with limited benefit, and for women trying to conceive the guideline calls it experimental.

Never start without data. The wrong supplement can make your symptoms worse: through side effects, an interaction with your medicines, or because it doesn't fit what's going on for you. So test your markers first, then choose with a doctor.
