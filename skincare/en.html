My skincare routine, in the right order

What I do every morning and evening, with the studies behind it.

I still have acne, and it comes back every cycle. This routine is how I look after my skin, every morning and every evening. Here's exactly what I do, in what order and why, with the studies behind it.

What almost nobody knows

Five numbers about skincare, and what to do with them today.

24%

Daily sunscreen meant 24% less skin ageing

In Australia, 903 people under 55 were randomly assigned to use sunscreen every day, or only when they chose to. After 4.5 years, skin ageing was 24% lower in the daily group, measured by the pattern of fine lines. That group showed no measurable increase in ageing at all. I put it on every morning, as my last step.

Study of 903 people

½ teaspoon

That's how much sunscreen your face, neck and ears need

SPF is the protection factor against burning. It's tested in the lab with 2 milligrams of sunscreen per square centimetre of skin. For your face, neck and ears, that's over half a teaspoon. Most people apply less than half that. With half the amount, your protection can drop by as much as two-thirds.

British Association of Dermatologists

0.3%

The most retinol your face cream may contain

Retinol is a form of vitamin A used in cosmetics. Since 1 November 2025, a new product in the EU may contain no more than 0.3% retinol, a body lotion 0.05%. From 1 May 2027 that applies to everything on the shelves. Some people already get a lot of vitamin A from food and supplements, so the pack must now also say it contains vitamin A. So since November 2025, a 1% retinol serum can no longer be newly placed on the EU market.

EU Regulation 2024/996

0

'Skin cycling' has never been tested

Skin cycling is a social media trend, created by an American dermatologist. The plan has four evenings. On the first you exfoliate, on the second you use a retinoid, and the last two are for rest. A retinoid is a substance derived from vitamin A. No published study has tested this fixed plan. The two ideas behind it do hold up: build up a retinoid slowly, and don't exfoliate every night. You'll find both in my week below.

Dermatology Times, June 2026

70%

On dry skin, hyaluronic acid can leave your skin drier

Hyaluronic acid is a substance your skin makes itself. It holds water, like a sponge. It doesn't bring any water of its own. It can only pull water from the air at a humidity of around 70%. In drier air, it draws water up from deeper layers of your skin, where it can evaporate. I put it on damp skin, and finish with a moisturiser.

My skin, and my acne

A good day, and when the acne comes.

My skin on a good day

This is my skin on a good day. That's how it is for most of my cycle. Just after I ovulate, around day 17 to 22, the acne comes up. Ovulation is the moment your ovary releases an egg. The acne stays until my period, then fades again.

I had my blood, my hormones and my gut tested, and I still don't know the cause. My routine doesn't make the acne go away. It's how I look after my skin, in the weeks it's there too. What I've found out about acne is in my acne guide.

      Dermatologists on skin cycling

      Build up slowly

      Your skin needs time to get used to a retinoid. That goes better if you build up the number of evenings gradually. If your skin gets red or flaky, skip an evening.

      1. Weeks 1 and 2: tretinoin on 2 evenings (Monday and Thursday). The rest are recovery evenings.
      2. Weeks 3 and 4: tretinoin on 3 evenings (Monday, Wednesday, Friday), and azelaic acid every other morning.
      3. From month 2: as in the week below, with one exfoliation evening on Saturday.

      Part 3

      My morning

      Six steps, the same every day.

      1. A gentle cleanser

      Every morning I start with a gentle cleanser. It removes oil and what's left from the night. That oil, called sebum, is a layer your skin makes itself to stay supple and protected.

      1. A cleanser contains surfactants, washing ingredients that attach to both oil and water, so oil can dissolve in water.
      2. That's how you rinse away oil, traces of sunscreen and make-up, and dead skin cells.
      3. Your skin is naturally slightly acidic: its pH is between about 4 and 6. pH tells you how acidic something is: 7 is neutral, lower is acidic, higher is alkaline.
      4. Ordinary soap is alkaline, with a pH of 9 to 10, and can leave your skin tight and dry. A gentle cleanser is neutral or slightly acidic and irritates less.

      Dermatologists recommend a gentle cleanser, then gently patting your skin dry. I pat dry too, every morning and evening.

      2. Toner

      Then a toner. That's a watery liquid you apply to your skin after cleansing. There are many kinds: some mainly contain water and humectants, others alcohol or acids.

      1. Humectants, such as glycerin, draw in water and hold it in the top layer of your skin.
      2. Toners are often said to shrink pores.
      3. A toner with alcohol or acids can actually dry out or irritate your skin. So choose a gentle one.

      To be honest, there's no evidence a toner adds much. I still use one, and let it sink in before the vitamin C goes on.

      3. Vitamin C, under my sunscreen

      Next, vitamin C. It's an antioxidant: a substance that captures free radicals. Free radicals are unstable particles that can damage other substances in your skin.

      1. UV light and air pollution create free radicals in your skin.
      2. An antioxidant such as vitamin C captures them. Your skin does this itself too, with the vitamin C it already contains, mostly in the top layer.
      3. That's why vitamin C goes under your sunscreen in the morning, as an extra. It doesn't replace your sunscreen.

      In a study, 5% vitamin C cream gave visibly smoother skin than placebo after 6 months. How much vitamin C on your skin adds to the vitamin C from your food isn't well understood yet.

      4. Azelaic acid, for a more even tone

      Azelaic acid is an acid used in serums for a more even tone. It makes redness and spots less visible. Those red and brown marks have different causes.

      1. A spot often leaves a red or brown mark behind.
      2. Brown marks are places where your skin has made extra pigment, its brown colouring. The sun causes this too.
      3. Redness shows when the tiny blood vessels just under your skin stand out, for example when your skin is irritated.

      In a small study, a serum with 10% azelaic acid (together with retinal, a form of vitamin A) gave 18% less redness than the same formula without it. Cosmetic serums usually contain 10%. Higher strengths are prescription-only.

      How I add it. From week 3 of building up, every other morning at first. If it stings together with vitamin C, I alternate them by morning. Never on the same evening as tretinoin.

      Use and safety

      5. Moisturiser

      Then my moisturiser. It keeps your skin hydrated and supports your skin barrier. Your skin barrier is the outer layer of your skin: flat skin cells with fats between them, like bricks and mortar. That layer keeps moisture in and irritants out.

      1. Water evaporates through your skin all day. When your skin is dry, the barrier often works less well and your skin feels tight.
      2. Humectants, such as glycerin and hyaluronic acid, draw water into the top layer.
      3. Fats and oils fill the gaps between your skin cells, so your skin feels smoother and softer.
      4. Occlusives, such as petroleum jelly, leave a thin layer on your skin, so less water evaporates.

      Moisturisers differ a lot. According to a review of studies, some ingredients, such as certain emulsifiers, can even weaken your skin barrier.

      6. Sunscreen, always last

      Sunscreen goes on last. It contains filters that absorb or reflect UV light. UV is the part of sunlight you can't see but that does reach your skin. Two kinds reach your skin: UVB and UVA. The SPF (sun protection factor) mainly tells you how well a sunscreen protects against UVB.

      1. UVB mostly lands in the top layer of your skin. It causes sunburn: your skin turns red.
      2. UVA goes deeper into your skin. Sunlight holds about 20 times more UVA than UVB, and it passes through glass.
      3. There, UV switches on enzymes that break down collagen, the protein that keeps your skin firm. That damage builds up over years, which is how wrinkles and pigment spots form.
      4. So choose a sunscreen that protects against both: UVA and UVB.

      Sunscreen is the step with the strongest evidence - see the 24% at the top. It matters even more when your skin is more sensitive to the sun, as it is with acids or a retinoid. Use over half a teaspoon for your face, neck and ears.

      Part 4

      My evening

      Three kinds of evening, which I rotate.

      Every evening I cleanse twice: first with a cleansing oil, then with my gentle cleanser. What comes next depends on the evening.

      My tretinoin evening

      On these evenings I use tretinoin. It's a retinoid: a substance derived from vitamin A. It makes the top layer of your skin produce new cells faster, and old cells let go of each other more easily. So that layer renews itself faster. I only apply it to completely dry skin. Damp skin gives more irritation, so hyaluronic acid doesn't go on right before it on this evening. If it stings, a thin layer of moisturiser can go underneath first.

      1. Cleansing oil
      2. Gentle cleanser, pat dry
      3. Wait until your skin is fully dry
      4. Tretinoin, pea-sized amount
      5. Moisturiser

      Only with a doctor. Tretinoin is prescription-only and only for use after a doctor's assessment. Not during pregnancy or when trying to conceive. In the first weeks your skin can get dry, red or flaky. That usually settles within about 4 weeks. Use SPF 30+ every day, because your skin becomes more sensitive to the sun.

      Once a week: exfoliating

      On Saturday I exfoliate. Exfoliating means loosening the top layer of dead skin cells, so your skin feels smoother. An acid toner does this with an acid, for example glycolic acid, an AHA (alpha hydroxy acid). These acids loosen the bonds between the dead cells, so they come away more easily.

      1. Cleansing oil
      2. Gentle cleanser
      3. Acid toner (exfoliant)
      4. Hyaluronic acid on damp skin
      5. Niacinamide
      6. Moisturiser

      Acids make your skin more sensitive to the sun: in a study, 10% glycolic acid led to more UV damage after 4 weeks, gone within a week of stopping. This may be because the top layer of your skin blocks some UV itself, and acids loosen that layer.

      Careful. Never on the same evening as tretinoin, and not in the first month.

      Part 5

      Where do you start?

      You don't need to copy my routine.

      The basics

      Using too many products at once can irritate your skin, especially more than one anti-ageing product. That's the advice of the American Academy of Dermatology. These three steps are in my routine every day, whatever the evening.

      1. A gentle cleanser, then gently pat your skin dry.
      2. A moisturiser.
      3. In the morning, sunscreen last: SPF 30 or higher, over half a teaspoon for face, neck and ears.

      Then add one product at a time, so you can see how your skin responds. Unsure about your skin, or about a prescription medicine? Talk to a doctor.
