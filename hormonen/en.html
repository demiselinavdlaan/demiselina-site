Hormones

What your hormones do each month, which phase you're in now, what you can have measured, and what supplements do and don't do.

For a long time I thought it was my hormones. My symptoms came back every month, always in the same part of my cycle. Years and a lot of tests later, I know much more about how your hormones work, what you can measure and on which day. That's what I share here, with the studies behind it.

What almost nobody knows

The textbook says: 28 days, ovulation on day 14. Real cycles rarely look like that.

13%

Only 13% of cycles last exactly 28 days

Your cycle runs from the first day of your period to the first day of the next. Across more than 612,000 cycles, the average was 29.3 days. The difference sat mainly in the first half, before ovulation: the moment your ovary releases an egg.

Study of 612,613 cycles, funded by a cycle app

1 in 12

Even in healthy women, not every cycle has ovulation

In 259 healthy women with regular cycles, 42 of 509 cycles had no ovulation. You still bleed, but progesterone doesn't rise. Progesterone is the hormone your body only makes in large amounts after ovulation.

Study of 259 women

21-74

Your LH peak can be more than 3 times someone else's

LH (luteinising hormone) comes from your pituitary, a small gland under your brain, and sets off ovulation. In 20 healthy women who had blood taken every day, the peak ranged from 21 to 74 IU/L (units per litre). So someone else's result says little about yours.

Study of 20 women, tested daily

0.3-0.7 °C

After ovulation your temperature is higher

Progesterone works like a small heater. In the second half of your cycle your core temperature, the temperature inside your body, is 0.3 to 0.7 °C higher than in the first half. It shows best during sleep or right after waking. It tells you afterwards that you ovulated.

My testosterone: too high in May, not in September

Women make testosterone too, in small amounts. My lab calls 0.29 to 1.67 nmol/L normal. In May 2026 mine was 2.04, in September 0.74. So one test is a snapshot.

Part 1

I thought it was my hormones

What was going on for me, and everything I had tested.

It came back every cycle

Take my skin: from just after ovulation until my period I get acne, and the rest of the month my skin is fine. In May 2026 my blood seemed to point at my hormones. My testosterone was too high and my SHBG was on the low side. SHBG is a protein that holds on to testosterone in your blood. Only the part that floats free, the free testosterone, can do anything. The less SHBG, the more free testosterone.

By September both were back in range: testosterone 0.74, SHBG 55.6. My acne stayed. So one high value doesn't establish anything - not PCOS either.

Everything I had tested

I kept searching for years. In total I spent more than €10,000 on tests and clinics, and nobody could help me.

  • Hospital visits
  • Blood tests and DNA tests
  • A DUTCH test, which measures hormones in urine
  • An OAT test, which measures organic acids in urine: by-products of your metabolism
  • An HTMA, which measures minerals in your hair
  • Two stool tests of my gut
  • Clinics, including in the US
  • Lots of supplements, on advice and on my own. Myo-inositol made me light-headed

Two clinics told me my liver was 'congested'. My liver values said otherwise: GGT 12 and ALT 26, both normal. In June I felt weak and got dizzy when I stood up, and in September everything went black once and I had to sleep for two hours. What was going on: I was eating and drinking too little. Since I've been eating more, doing strength training and drinking more water with a little salt, I have a lot more energy. A gut test also showed increased permeability of my gut wall in my case (leaky gut). What's really behind my symptoms, I still don't know - it goes up and down. What I do now: I track which cycle day my acne starts, I have my blood re-tested, and my ring measures my sleep and heart rate every night.

    Four hormones run your cycle

    Hormones are messengers that travel in your blood. FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone) and LH come from your pituitary. Oestradiol and progesterone come from your ovaries. Every month they pass the baton to each other, from one period to the next.

    1. During your period your pituitary makes a little more FSH. That makes a few follicles grow in your ovaries: small sacs that each hold an egg.
    2. One follicle grows fastest and makes more and more oestradiol. Oestradiol thickens your womb lining, the layer on the inside of your womb. This is the follicular phase.
    3. Once oestradiol is high enough, your pituitary makes a lot of LH in a short time: the LH peak. About 16 hours after that peak the follicle bursts open and releases the egg. That's ovulation.
    4. The empty follicle turns into the corpus luteum (Latin for 'yellow body'). It makes a lot of progesterone, which gets your lining ready for a pregnancy. This is the luteal phase, the second half of your cycle.
    5. If you don't get pregnant, the corpus luteum stops. Progesterone and oestradiol fall, your lining sheds and your period starts. The round begins again.

    After ovulation the balance tips

    Oestradiol and progesterone work as a team. Oestradiol builds up your womb lining. Progesterone then slows that growth and gets the lining ready to receive a fertilised egg. In the chart above each line is scaled to its own peak, so they look equally large. Only when you convert them to the same unit do you see how strongly the balance shifts.

    1. In the first half your growing follicle mainly makes oestradiol. Progesterone stays low.
    2. After ovulation the corpus luteum makes a lot of progesterone, plus a little oestradiol.
    3. So the balance swings strongly towards progesterone. Your lining then switches from growing to getting ready.

    Oestradiol works in very small amounts. So even in the first half there's more progesterone in the same unit: 1.4 to 4 times as much. In the second half it's 40 to over 70 times. There's no single 'right' ratio, so always read your values together with your cycle day and symptoms.

    What if you don't ovulate?

    A cycle without ovulation is called an anovulatory cycle. You can still bleed, but the second half goes differently.

    1. Without ovulation no corpus luteum forms, so progesterone doesn't rise.
    2. Oestradiol then keeps your womb lining growing, without the brake of progesterone. Doctors call this 'unopposed oestrogen'. Online it's often called oestrogen dominance.
    3. A lining like that is unstable and sheds at unpredictable times. That often leads to irregular periods.

    Heavy training, eating too little

    Half the cycles of exercising women had no ovulation or a short second half, against 4% in inactive women.

    Perimenopause

    In women aged 43 to 53, the share of cycles with ovulation fell from 81% to 65% over 3 years.

    PCOS, thyroid and prolactin

    Known causes of cycles without ovulation.

    It happens now and then in healthy women too. In the situations above it happens more often.

    Not sure? A progesterone test in the second half shows whether you ovulated. The calculator above gives you the day. One test can mislead, so repeat it if in doubt.

    How your body clears hormones

    A hormone that has done its job has to be cleared by your body, or it keeps working. This happens every day, mainly through your liver, your bile and your gut. Bile is a fluid your liver makes and sends to your gut.

    1. Your liver attaches a tag to the hormone. That switches it off and makes it dissolve in water.
    2. Part of it leaves your body through your kidneys and urine. Another part travels with your bile to your gut.
    3. In your gut some bacteria can remove the tag again. Your body then takes part of the oestrogen back into your blood. This group of gut bacteria is called the estrobolome.
    4. The rest leaves your body in your stool.

    In humans the evidence for this is still thin. In a study of 62 women, extra wheat bran (the fibre-rich outer layer of the wheat grain) lowered oestrogen; oat and corn bran did nothing. In a larger study of 250 women, a lot of fibre was linked to lower hormone levels and more cycles without ovulation.

    Why your cycle can change from month to month

    Your cycle length differs between women, and often from month to month. That almost always comes from the first half.

    1. The first half, the follicular phase, lasts as long as your follicle needs to mature. That can differ from cycle to cycle.
    2. The second half, the luteal phase, lasts as long as the corpus luteum works. That's much more fixed.
    3. So a long or short cycle usually comes from a later or earlier ovulation.

    In the study of over 612,000 cycles, the first half lasted 16.9 days on average, with big differences. The phase after ovulation was fairly fixed at about 12 days. Normal is 21 to 35 days between periods, with 2 to 8 days of bleeding.

    What is normal

    Do you sleep worse or eat more before your period?

    Your hormones act on your whole body, not only your womb. What you notice of that in a given phase differs from woman to woman.

    • Sleep: in healthy women, sleep didn't change across the cycle. Their heart rate and temperature did move with it.

    Should you train by your cycle?

    With cycle syncing you adjust your training and eating to the phase of your cycle. The idea comes from the hormones.

    1. In the first half oestradiol rises. You'd feel stronger then and could train harder.
    2. In the second half progesterone and your temperature are higher. You'd then need to train more gently and eat differently.

    The evidence for it is weak. Across 78 studies, performance in the first days of the cycle was at most very slightly lower, and the researchers couldn't give general advice. Another review found no effect of phase on strength or muscle growth. We found no studies on eating by phase. So you can train on how you feel that day.

    What can you measure?

    What your wearable shows about your cycle, and which blood test to take on which day.

    What your wearable shows about your cycle

    A wearable is a ring, watch or band that measures your body day and night, such as Oura or WHOOP. Among other things it measures your skin temperature, your resting heart rate (your heart rate when you lie still) and your HRV. HRV stands for heart rate variability: how much the time between two heartbeats varies. When your nervous system is in rest mode, that variation is usually larger.

    1. After ovulation progesterone nudges your temperature up. Your wearable picks this up on your skin at night.
    2. Your heart also responds to your hormones shifting: your resting heart rate and HRV move with them.
    3. That pattern comes back every cycle. Once you've tracked a few cycles, you learn to recognise it.

    Your skin temperature is about 0.3 °C higher after ovulation. Your resting heart rate swings by 2.7 beats on average, peaking just before your period. Your HRV is lower in the second half. So compare your data with the same phase of an earlier cycle.

    Careful. A wearable doesn't reliably predict ovulation, can't identify a condition and isn't contraception.

    Which blood test, on which day

    A blood test is a snapshot: it shows how much of a substance is in your blood at that moment. Because your hormones move through your cycle, the day matters. The list has a few new names. TSH is the hormone that drives your thyroid. Prolactin switches on milk production; too much of it can block ovulation. Haemoglobin and ferritin are about your iron; more on that under heavy bleeding.

    1. On day 2 to 6 oestradiol and progesterone are still low and steady. That's when you best see your baseline FSH, LH and oestradiol.
    2. Progesterone only rises after ovulation and peaks about a week later. A test then shows whether you ovulated.
    3. For TSH and prolactin your cycle day matters little.

    FSH, LH and oestradiol

    On day 2 to 6 of your cycle.

    Progesterone

    About 7 days before your next period. Repeat it if it's low.

    TSH and prolactin

    Any day. Especially with irregular or missing periods.

    Testosterone and SHBG

    In the morning on day 4 to 10. Mainly with extra hair growth or an irregular cycle.

    Haemoglobin and ferritin

    With heavy bleeding or tiredness.

    One test says little: progesterone can swing a lot even within hours, because the corpus luteum releases it in waves. Always note your cycle day.

    Careful. On the pill? Then these days don't apply: the pill stops ovulation, raises SHBG and lowers your free testosterone.

    Part 4

    Is it your hormones?

    The most common problems, what's behind them and when to see your GP.

    PMS and PMDD

    PMS stands for premenstrual syndrome: symptoms in the 1 to 2 weeks before your period that disappear once it starts. Think irritability, low mood, sore breasts and bloating. PMDD (premenstrual dysphoric disorder) is the severe form, where mood is at the centre. Dysphoric means low, tense or irritable.

    1. After ovulation progesterone and oestradiol rise. Just before your period they fall quickly.
    2. In women with PMS these hormone levels are usually perfectly normal.
    3. Their brain does react more strongly to that same normal shift.

    PMDD is confirmed in 3.2% of women. In one study, symptoms in women with PMS disappeared when a medicine temporarily switched off their ovaries, and came back with oestradiol or progesterone. In women without PMS the same treatment didn't change their mood. That's why a diary over 2 to 3 cycles tells you more here than a blood test.

    See your GP if daily life suffers, if symptoms continue after your period, or with dark thoughts.

    When a condition follows your cycle

    Symptoms that flare around your period aren't always PMS. Often it's something you already have that gets worse in a certain phase.

    1. Your hormone levels are usually normal: there's no hormone imbalance.
    2. But oestrogen and progesterone act on much more than your womb, including your immune system.
    3. If you already have a condition such as asthma, migraine or eczema, the ordinary monthly shift can make it worse at set points in your cycle.

    Asthma

    17% of women with asthma notice it worsening around their period.

    Migraine

    22% of women with migraine get an attack with at least half of their periods.

    Eczema

    47% had monthly worsening, mostly in the week before their period.

    Acne

    44% noticed flares before their period.

    Low mood

    64% of women with depression noticed it worsening before their period.

    Your immune system changes in measurable ways. Defences in the cervix and vagina drop in the second half of your cycle, and your inflammation marker CRP (a protein your liver makes during inflammation) peaks during your period. In your blood, there are actually more white blood cells, your immune cells, in the second half.

    Tracking helps. Track how you feel every day for 2 to 3 cycles. If the week after your period is symptom-free, that fits PMS. If symptoms are there all month and only worse before your period, an existing condition is getting worse.

    Painful periods and endometriosis

    Period pain is cramping pain in your lower belly, just before or during your period. Endometriosis is something else: tissue similar to the womb lining grows outside your womb, for example on your ovaries or the lining of your belly.

    1. As your lining sheds, it releases prostaglandins: substances that make your womb contract.
    2. With a lot of prostaglandins your womb squeezes more often and harder, and gets less blood. You feel that as cramps.
    3. In endometriosis the tissue outside your womb also responds to your cycle hormones. It can cause inflammation and pain, also outside your period.

    Over 7 in 10 young women have period pain. About 1 in 10 has endometriosis. In the Netherlands it takes 7 years on average before it's recognised as endometriosis. No blood test helps: it comes down to your story, a physical examination and an ultrasound scan.

    See your GP if painkillers or the pill don't help, with pain outside your period, or pain during sex or on the toilet.

    Heavy bleeding and iron deficiency

    Heavy bleeding means your period is so heavy that it affects daily life: for example, you need to change very often or you leak through. Two blood tests show what that does to your iron. Haemoglobin is the protein in your red blood cells that carries oxygen, and it needs iron to do that. Ferritin shows how much iron you have in store.

    1. Every time you bleed you lose iron, because iron sits in your haemoglobin.
    2. Your body tops it up from your store. That runs low first: your ferritin falls.
    3. Only once your store is empty does your haemoglobin fall too. That's called anaemia.
    4. So a normal haemoglobin doesn't mean you have enough iron.

    Over 1 in 4 women has heavy bleeding, and almost half never saw a doctor about it. About 4 in 10 women have iron deficiency without anaemia. So have both haemoglobin and ferritin tested. Labs do use different cut-offs.

    Ferritin and cut-offs

    Irregular or missing periods

    Your cycle is called irregular if the time between periods keeps changing a lot, or falls outside 21 to 35 days. If your period stays away for months, that's called amenorrhoea.

    1. Your period comes because the corpus luteum stops after ovulation. If ovulation comes late or not at all, your period comes late, irregularly or not at all.
    2. Ovulation depends on a steady signal from your brain: short pulses of LH. Too little energy or too much prolactin can dampen that signal.
    3. In PCOS (polycystic ovary syndrome) your ovaries make more androgens, hormones often called 'male'. Follicles then often don't mature fully, so ovulation doesn't happen.

    In women referred for this, PCOS was by far the most common cause (82%), followed by high prolactin (7%). Under-eating with lots of training also plays a role: it occurs in almost half of female athletes. In a study of 29 women, LH pulses came less often after 5 days, but only when they ate well below what their training needed. A thyroid problem mainly disturbs the cycle when it's severe. Read more about PCOS on my PCOS page.

    See your GP if you haven't had a period for months, if you want to conceive, or with a stress fracture. A pregnancy test always comes first.

    Low progesterone

    'Low progesterone' usually means a short or weak second half of your cycle. Doctors call this luteal phase deficiency: the phase after ovulation lasts 10 days or less, or the corpus luteum makes too little progesterone.

    1. After ovulation the corpus luteum makes progesterone. That keeps your womb lining in place.
    2. If the corpus luteum stops too early, progesterone falls sooner and your period starts sooner. Your second half is then short.
    3. A low result doesn't always mean this is happening. You may have tested on a different day, in a dip between two waves of progesterone, or not ovulated that month.

    A short second half of the cycle occurs in about 9% of ovulatory cycles, but recurs in only 3.4% of women. According to a 2026 American guideline, it hasn't been shown to cause infertility or recurrent miscarriage, and there's disagreement on how to identify it reliably.

    Perimenopause

    Perimenopause is the years before your last period, usually from your forties. That last period itself is called the menopause.

    1. Your ovaries have fewer and fewer follicles left.
    2. Your pituitary makes more FSH to push the remaining follicles into action.
    3. You ovulate less often, and oestradiol sometimes shoots up and then drops again. That makes your cycle less regular.
    4. These swings can bring hot flushes, night sweats, poorer sleep and low mood.

    Hormone tests help little here: FSH, LH and oestradiol fluctuate too much to confirm it. So your GP mainly looks at your age, your cycle and your symptoms.

    Part 5

    What do supplements really do?

    What research says, and why you test first.

    What looks like a hormone problem can have a completely different cause. A supplement for the wrong cause doesn't help, and can make symptoms worse or blur your test results.

    What's sold for cycle symptoms

    A supplement is a pill, powder or drink with nutrients or plant extracts. Supplements are promoted for almost every problem above. Whether that's justified depends on how good the studies are.

    1. A good study compares two groups: one gets the supplement, the other a dummy pill (placebo), and nobody knows who gets which.
    2. If participants know what they're getting, or the study is poorly set up in another way, the result often looks better than it is. That's called a high risk of bias.
    3. Many vitamins and minerals have a safe upper limit: the amount per day you can take for years without harm. Above it, the chance of side effects goes up.

    Magnesium

    Small studies, too little evidence. Study doses are often above the safe limit.

    Vitamin B6

    Low-quality studies. Too much B6 can damage nerves, and study doses are up to 5 times the Dutch maximum.

    Calcium

    One large study. Blocks iron uptake if you take them together.

    Chasteberry (vitex)

    The most studied, but many studies have a high risk of bias. Can mask high prolactin. Not in pregnancy, breastfeeding or IVF.

    Omega-3 and ginger

    On average favourable studies for period pain, with a high risk of bias. Delaying a check for endometriosis is the biggest risk.

    Myo-inositol

    Limited and uncertain evidence in PCOS. See my PCOS page.

    The evidence for the supplements above is usually small or low quality, and study doses are often above the safe limit.

    What's sold as 'hormone detox'

    'Detox' means removing waste from your body. Lots of supplements and cleanses are sold to 'flush out' hormones, while your body already does that work every day.

    1. Your liver switches hormones off and makes them dissolve in water.
    2. Your kidneys remove part of them in your urine, your gut the rest in your stool.
    3. Whether your liver works well shows in liver values in your blood, such as ALT and GGT. These are enzymes from your liver that rise when your liver gets damaged.

    Detox programmes

    No randomised trials of commercial detox programmes have been done in humans.

    Liver flush

    The 'stones' passed after such a flush were soap, formed in the gut.

    Fibre

    The best studied, but through ordinary food. More isn't always better.

    Research on extra help with that work is thin.

    Only for a measured deficiency

    Some supplements only make sense if your level is actually low. A deficiency means your blood level is below the lab's cut-off. Without a test you don't know.

    1. If you're low in something, topping up can refill your store.
    2. If you have enough, extra usually does nothing more.
    3. Some, such as iron and vitamin D, build up in your body. Too much can then do harm.

    Iron

    Only useful with low ferritin. Too much iron is harmful and can hide the cause of blood loss.

    Vitamin D

    Low-quality studies, often with doses above the safe limit. Get your level tested first.

    Never start without data. Test your markers first and talk to a doctor, especially if you take medication or the pill, want to conceive or are doing IVF.
