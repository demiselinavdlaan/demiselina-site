Hormones

Your cycle, the most common problems, what you can measure, and what supplements do and don't do.

I thought I had a hormone problem myself - my symptoms kept coming back with my cycle. In the end it turned out to be something else; my story is at the bottom. Your hormones move in a pattern every month, but almost nobody has a textbook cycle. On this page you'll see how your hormones move, which problems are most common, what you can measure, and why you never start supplements without data.

Part 1

Your cycle

How your hormones move

Four hormones drive your cycle: FSH and LH from your brain, oestradiol and progesterone from your ovaries. Tap a phase, or slide across the chart to look day by day.

The balance between progesterone and oestradiol

In the chart each line is scaled to its own peak, so oestradiol and progesterone look equally large. Convert the values to the same unit and you see how strongly the balance shifts after ovulation: in the first half there's 1 to 4 times as much progesterone as oestradiol, in the second half 40 to 70 times. This prepares the womb lining. There's no single 'right' ratio, so always read your values together with your cycle day and symptoms.

What if that shift doesn't happen?

Without ovulation, progesterone doesn't rise. Oestradiol then acts unopposed on the womb lining, often leading to irregular periods. Doctors call this 'unopposed oestrogen'; online it's often called oestrogen dominance. It's more common than you'd think: in about 1 in 12 cycles in healthy women, and more often in these situations:

Heavy training, eating too little

Half the cycles of exercising women had no ovulation or a short second half, against 4% in inactive women.

Perimenopause

In women aged 43 to 53, the share of cycles with ovulation fell from 81% to 65% over 3 years.

PCOS, thyroid and prolactin

Known causes of cycles without ovulation.

How to check. A progesterone test in the second half of your cycle shows whether you ovulated. One test can mislead, so repeat it if in doubt.

On the term oestrogen dominance

How your body clears hormones

Your body clears hormones every day, mainly through your liver, your bile and your gut. Your gut bacteria play a part: they can switch some oestrogen back on and send it back to your blood. In humans the evidence is still thin. In a study of 62 women, extra wheat bran lowered oestrogen; oat and corn bran did nothing. In a larger study of 250 women, a lot of fibre was linked to lower hormone levels and more cycles without ovulation. So more isn't always better.

No cycle is average

In over 600,000 cycles, only 13% lasted exactly 28 days. The average was 29.3 days, and mainly the first half varies: the phase after ovulation is fairly fixed at about 12 days. Normal is 21 to 35 days between periods, with 2 to 8 days of bleeding.

What is normal

What you notice, and what's a myth

After ovulation your temperature rises by 0.3 to 0.7 °C. In healthy women, sleep didn't change across the cycle, and most studies found no fixed link between mood and a phase. In the second half women eat a little more on average, but studies measure this in different ways.

  • Myth: everyone has a 28-day cycle with ovulation on day 14.
  • Myth: everyone is low or irritable before their period.
  • Myth: you burn far more calories before your period.

What about cycle syncing?

Training and eating by phase is popular, but the evidence is weak. Across 78 studies, performance in the first days of the cycle was at most very slightly lower, and the researchers couldn't give general advice. We found no studies on eating by phase. So train on how you feel, not on a calendar.

Part 2

Measuring

What a wearable shows

A wearable picks up your cycle in your data. Your skin temperature is about 0.3 °C higher after ovulation. Your resting heart rate swings by 2.7 beats on average, peaking just before your period. Your HRV is lower in the second half. So compare your data with the same phase of an earlier cycle.

Careful. A wearable doesn't reliably predict ovulation, doesn't diagnose anything and isn't contraception.

Two of these studies were co-run by the manufacturer (Oura, WHOOP)

Which blood tests, on which day

Hormones fluctuate, so timing matters. One test says little: progesterone can even swing a lot within hours. Always note your cycle day.

FSH, LH and oestradiol

On day 2 to 6 of your cycle.

Progesterone

About 7 days before your next period. Repeat it if it's low.

TSH and prolactin

Any day. Especially with irregular or missing periods.

Testosterone and SHBG

With acne, extra hair growth or an irregular cycle.

Haemoglobin and ferritin

With heavy bleeding or tiredness.

Careful. On the pill? Then these days don't apply: the pill stops ovulation, raises SHBG and lowers your free testosterone.

Part 3

The most common problems

PMS and PMDD

Symptoms in the 1 to 2 weeks before your period that disappear once it starts, such as irritability, low mood, sore breasts and bloating. PMDD is the severe, mood-led form: confirmed in 3.2% of women. A diary over 2 to 3 cycles tells you more here than a blood test.

See your GP if daily life suffers, if symptoms continue after your period, or with dark thoughts.

The prevalence of premenstrual dysphoric disorder: Systematic review and meta-analysis.

Reilly TJ, Patel S, Unachukwu IC, Knox CL, Wilson CA et al.

J Affect Disord 2024 · PMID 38199397

Thuisarts: premenstrual syndrome

Patient information

Looks hormonal, but isn't always

Symptoms that flare around your period aren't always PMS. Often it's something you already have that gets worse in the days before your period. Your hormone levels are usually normal: the ordinary monthly shift changes a lot in your body, including parts of your immune system. Defences in the cervix and vagina drop in the second half of your cycle, and your inflammation marker (CRP) peaks during your period. In your blood, there are actually more white blood cells in the second half.

Asthma

17% of women with asthma notice it worsening around their period.

Migraine

22% of women with migraine get an attack with at least half of their periods.

Eczema

47% had monthly worsening, mostly in the week before their period.

Acne

44% noticed flares before their period.

Low mood

64% of women with depression noticed it worsening before their period.

How to tell the difference. Track how you feel every day for 2 to 3 cycles. If the week after your period is symptom-free, that fits PMS. If symptoms are there all month and only worse before your period, an existing condition is getting worse.

Painful periods and endometriosis

Over 7 in 10 young women have period pain. About 1 in 10 has endometriosis: tissue similar to the womb lining, outside the womb. In the Netherlands, diagnosis takes 7 years on average. No blood test helps: it comes down to your story, an examination and an ultrasound.

See your GP if painkillers or the pill don't help, with pain outside your period, or pain during sex or on the toilet.

Heavy bleeding and iron deficiency

Over 1 in 4 women has heavy bleeding, and almost half never saw a doctor about it. This drains your iron stores: about 4 in 10 women have iron deficiency without anaemia. Have haemoglobin and ferritin tested. Labs do use different cut-offs.

Irregular or missing periods

In women referred for this, PCOS was by far the most common cause (82%), followed by high prolactin (7%). Under-eating with lots of training also plays a role: it occurs in almost half of female athletes. A thyroid problem mainly disturbs the cycle when it's severe. Read more about PCOS on my PCOS page.

See your GP if you haven't had a period for months, if you want to conceive, or with a stress fracture. A pregnancy test always comes first.

Which tests when

Low progesterone

A short second half of the cycle occurs in about 9% of ovulatory cycles, but recurs in only 3.4% of women. According to a 2026 American guideline, it hasn't been shown to cause infertility or miscarriage, and the diagnosis is disputed.

Perimenopause

The years before your last period, usually from your forties. Your cycle changes and you may get hot flushes, night sweats, poorer sleep and low mood. Hormone tests help little here: FSH, LH and oestradiol fluctuate too much to diagnose it.

Part 4

My story

I thought it was my hormones

My symptoms kept coming back with my cycle, acne being the clearest. So naturally I thought of my hormones. After testing, my SHBG turned out slightly low - not by much, but it seemed to fit.

Everything I tried

Before I found the cause, I had everything tested. About €10,000 in total - and nobody could help me.

  • Hospital visits, to rule everything out
  • Blood tests and DNA tests
  • A DUTCH test (hormones in urine) and an OAT test (organic acids in urine)
  • An HTMA (minerals in hair)
  • Two GI tests (stool)
  • Several clinics, also in the US

What it turned out to be

My acne didn't come from my hormones - eggs turned out to be a trigger for me. On top of that, I wasn't digesting my food well. I wasn't getting enough fluid, so my body made too little stomach acid. That switched on my immune system, and the GI tests showed a leaky gut. Because of that, my body also struggled to clear hormones. That's why fibre matters so much to me: it helps carry hormones out in your stool, so your body doesn't take them back up. Enough fibre, not as much as possible - see the part on clearance above.

My story, not advice. Your cause may be completely different. Get tested first and talk to a doctor.

Part 5

Supplements

My story shows why I never just start supplements: what looks like a hormone problem can have a completely different cause. A supplement for the wrong cause doesn't help, and can make symptoms worse or blur your test results.

What's sold for cycle symptoms

Supplements are promoted for almost every problem above. The evidence is usually small or low quality, and study doses are often above the safe limit.

Magnesium

Small studies, too little evidence. Study doses are often above the safe limit.

Vitamin B6

Low-quality studies. Too much B6 can damage nerves, and study doses are up to 5 times the Dutch maximum.

Calcium

One large study. Blocks iron uptake if you take them together.

Chasteberry (vitex)

The most studied, but many studies have a high risk of bias. Can mask high prolactin. Not in pregnancy, breastfeeding or IVF.

Omega-3 and ginger

On average favourable studies for period pain, with a high risk of bias. Delaying a check for endometriosis is the biggest risk.

Myo-inositol

Limited and uncertain evidence in PCOS. See my PCOS page.

What's sold as 'hormone detox'

Online you'll read a lot about 'flushing out' hormones with supplements or cleanses. Your liver, bile and gut already do that work every day, and research on extra help is thin.

Detox programmes

No randomised trials of commercial detox programmes have been done in humans.

Liver flush

The 'stones' passed after such a flush turned out to be soap, formed in the gut.

Fibre

The best studied, but through ordinary food. More isn't always better.

Only for a measured deficiency

Some supplements only make sense if your level is actually low. Without a test you don't know, and too much can do harm.

Iron

Only useful with low ferritin. Too much iron is harmful and can hide the cause of blood loss.

Vitamin D

Low-quality studies, often with doses above the safe limit. Get your level tested first.

Never start without data. Test your markers first and talk to a doctor, especially if you take medication or the pill, want to conceive or are doing IVF.
