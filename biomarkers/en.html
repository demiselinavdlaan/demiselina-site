The blood markers I have tested

By group: what each marker measures, why it matters, and the study behind it.

These are the markers I have tested at Cellular, grouped by type. I retest a few times a year, so I can see what changes. For each marker you'll find what it measures, why it matters, and the study behind it.

Hormones

Sex hormones, cortisol, prolactin and aldosterone.

Testosterone, free testosterone and FAI

A hormone is a messenger: your body makes it in one place, and through your blood it sends a signal to cells elsewhere. Testosterone is one of the androgens, hormones often called 'male'. Women make them too, in small amounts, in the ovaries and the adrenal glands (two small glands on top of your kidneys). Testosterone plays a part in your muscles, your bones and your sex drive. This is how it works:

  1. Most of your testosterone is bound to proteins in your blood, mainly SHBG. While it's bound, it does nothing.
  2. A small part floats freely. That free testosterone can enter your cells and send a signal.
  3. The FAI (free androgen index) is a calculation: your testosterone divided by your SHBG, times 100. The higher the FAI, the more testosterone is free, relatively speaking.

The international guideline on PCOS, a hormone condition in which the ovaries make more androgens, uses free testosterone and the FAI to assess an excess of male hormones. In 595 healthy women, testosterone fell mostly at a younger age, and the menopause itself caused no extra drop.

Reference range: testosterone 0.48-1.85 nmol/L · FAI below 4 (women under 50; varies by lab) · GP: only with a reason

SHBG

SHBG (sex hormone binding globulin) is a protein made by your liver. It binds sex hormones in your blood, such as testosterone and oestrogen. Think of it as a sponge that holds on to hormones:

  1. Hormone bound to SHBG can't reach your cells. Only the free part is active.
  2. If your SHBG is low, more testosterone stays free. Testosterone then has more effect, even when your total testosterone is normal.
  3. If your SHBG is high, more is bound and less is free.

In postmenopausal women, a low SHBG was linked to a much higher risk of type 2 diabetes. The pill raises SHBG, which makes hormone results hard to read while you take it.

Reference range: women 19.8-155 · men 13.5-71.4 nmol/L (varies by lab) · GP: only with a reason

DHEA-S

DHEA is a hormone from your adrenal glands. In your blood it is mostly present as DHEA-S, a more stable form that labs can measure well. Your body uses it as a raw material for other hormones:

  1. Your adrenal glands make DHEA-S every day and release it into your blood.
  2. Tissues around your body turn it into testosterone or oestrogen, right where it's needed.
  3. Because DHEA-S comes mainly from your adrenal glands, the level shows how much androgen your adrenal glands make.

DHEA-S falls with age, and the PCOS guideline calls it a less precise extra test when testosterone is normal. Across 23 studies in 1,188 postmenopausal women, extra DHEA did not clearly improve libido (sex drive).

Reference range: women aged 30-39 1.6-6.2 µmol/L; falls with age (varies by lab) · GP: not standard

Cortisol (morning)

Cortisol is your main stress hormone, made in your adrenal glands. It frees up energy, such as sugar in your blood, and helps your body cope with stress and inflammation. It follows a daily rhythm:

  1. A small gland under your brain, the pituitary, sends the signal hormone ACTH to your adrenal glands. In response, they make cortisol.
  2. Cortisol rises most just after you wake up. Then it falls through the day, and it's lowest at night.
  3. When there's enough cortisol, your brain turns the signal down. That keeps the amount in balance.

Because cortisol peaks in the morning, doctors use a morning level as a first check that your adrenal glands make enough of it. A review of 58 studies found no evidence for 'adrenal fatigue' caused by stress.

Reference range: before 10 am 101-536 nmol/L (varies by lab) · GP: only with a reason

Oestradiol and progesterone

Oestradiol is your main oestrogen, a female sex hormone from your ovaries. It makes the lining of your womb grow and also matters for your bones. Progesterone is made mainly after ovulation. It gets the lining ready for a possible pregnancy. This is how they work together in your cycle:

  1. In the first half of your cycle, a follicle grows in your ovary: a small sac holding an egg. It makes more and more oestradiol.
  2. Around mid-cycle the egg is released: ovulation. What's left of the follicle, the corpus luteum, starts making progesterone.
  3. If you don't get pregnant, both hormones fall in the days before your period. The lining sheds and your period starts.

Because both swing so widely, oestradiol is measured on day 2 to 5 (day 1 is the first day of your period) and progesterone about 7 days before your next period, around day 21 of a 28-day cycle. In 107 women, a single progesterone level of about 16 nmol/L or higher was a reliable sign that ovulation had taken place.

Reference range: oestradiol follicular phase 77-921 pmol/L · progesterone luteal phase 3.8-51 nmol/L (varies by lab) · GP: only with a reason

Prolactin

Prolactin is a hormone from your pituitary, the small gland under your brain that steers many other hormones. After giving birth, prolactin drives the production of breast milk. This is how it's controlled:

  1. Normally your brain keeps prolactin low, using the messenger dopamine.
  2. During pregnancy and breastfeeding, prolactin rises sharply, so your breasts make milk.
  3. High prolactin holds back the hormones that trigger ovulation.

In women, a high level can go together with irregular or missed periods. According to the guideline, about 4 in 10 high results are due to macroprolactin, a large form that is less active. Stress during the blood draw can also raise the level.

Reference range: women 110-560 mU/L (varies by lab) · GP: only with a reason

Diagnosis and treatment of hyperprolactinemia: an Endocrine Society clinical practice guideline.

Aldosterone

Aldosterone is a hormone from your adrenal glands. It controls how much salt (sodium) and fluid your body holds on to, and with that your blood pressure. This is how it keeps your blood pressure steady:

  1. When your blood pressure drops, or your body is low on salt, your kidneys release a substance called renin.
  2. Renin sets off a chain reaction that makes your adrenal glands produce aldosterone.
  3. Aldosterone makes your kidneys hold on to salt and water. That brings your blood pressure back up.
  4. If your adrenal glands make too much aldosterone on their own, your body turns renin down. That's why a single value says little, and doctors read aldosterone together with renin.

Among 1,672 people with high blood pressure from GP practices, 6 in 100 made too much aldosterone. The 2025 guideline advises testing everyone with high blood pressure for this.

Reference range: 56-660 pmol/L · read as the aldosterone-renin ratio (varies by lab) · GP: not standard

Lipids

The fats in your blood: cholesterol and triglycerides.

LDL and non-HDL cholesterol

Cholesterol is a fatty substance your cells need, for example as a building block for hormones. Fat doesn't dissolve in blood, so cholesterol travels in small packages of fat and protein. LDL is the package that carries cholesterol from your liver to the rest of your body. Non-HDL is all cholesterol except HDL: LDL plus the other packages that can get into your artery walls. This is how it builds up:

  1. When there's a lot of LDL in your blood, more particles push through the inner lining of your arteries and get stuck there.
  2. Immune cells come to clear it up, but fill up with fat themselves and stay in the wall.
  3. Over the years this grows into a plaque: a thickening in the artery wall that narrows the vessel.

In more than 184,000 women, the 30-year risk of heart and vascular disease rose from 8% with a non-HDL below 2.6 mmol/L to 34% at 5.7 or higher.

Reference range: LDL below 3.0 · non-HDL below 3.8 · total cholesterol below 5.0 mmol/L (varies by lab) · Total/HDL ratio: below 5 (some labs below 4) · GP: only with a reason

Application of non-HDL cholesterol for population-based cardiovascular risk stratification: results from the Multinational Cardiovascular Risk Consortium.

Brunner FJ, Waldeyer C, Ojeda F, Salomaa V, Kee F et al.

Lancet 2019 · PMID 31810609 · PMCID PMC6913519

HDL cholesterol

HDL is another of those packages of fat and protein. It works as a clean-up service for cholesterol:

  1. HDL picks up spare cholesterol from your tissues and artery walls.
  2. It carries that cholesterol back to your liver.
  3. Your liver removes it through bile, a fluid your liver releases into your gut.

In more than 116,000 Danes, both a low and an extremely high HDL were linked to higher mortality. In women, mortality was lowest around 2.4 mmol/L and higher from 3.5 upwards.

Reference range: women above 1.2 · men above 1.0 mmol/L (varies by lab) · GP: only with a reason

Extreme high high-density lipoprotein cholesterol is paradoxically associated with high mortality in men and women: two prospective cohort studies.

Madsen CM, Varbo A, Nordestgaard BG

Eur Heart J 2017 · PMID 28419274

Triglycerides

Triglycerides are the form in which your body carries and stores fat. They're fuel: your muscles and other cells get energy from them.

  1. After a meal your gut absorbs fat and sends it into your blood as triglycerides. That's why the level rises after eating.
  2. Your liver also makes triglycerides itself, for example from sugar and alcohol your body doesn't need straight away.
  3. What your cells don't burn straight away is stored in your fat tissue.

In 26,509 healthy American women, triglycerides measured 2 to 4 hours after eating were most strongly linked to later heart and vascular disease. Fasting values, measured after a night without food, added little on top of other risk factors.

Reference range: below 1.7 mmol/L (women and men; varies by lab) · GP: only with a reason

Fasting compared with nonfasting triglycerides and risk of cardiovascular events in women.

Bansal S, Buring JE, Rifai N, Mora S, Sacks FM et al.

JAMA 2007 · PMID 17635891

Metabolic

Blood sugar, insulin, kidneys and salt balance.

Fasting glucose

Glucose is the sugar in your blood, the main fuel for your cells and your brain. A fasting glucose is your blood sugar after a night without food. This is how your body keeps it steady:

  1. After a meal your gut breaks carbohydrates down into glucose, which passes into your blood.
  2. Your pancreas, an organ behind your stomach, then releases the hormone insulin. This lets your cells take up the glucose.
  3. At night your liver slowly releases glucose from its store. That keeps your blood sugar steady while you're not eating.

In data from almost 700,000 people, a fasting glucose between 3.9 and 5.6 mmol/L was not linked to heart disease. Above that, the risk rose slightly. Diabetes roughly doubled the risk of heart disease, and the link was stronger in women than in men.

Reference range: below 6.1 mmol/L · 6.1-6.9 raised · 7.0 or higher is the diabetes threshold (NHG) · GP: only with a reason

Diabetes mellitus, fasting blood glucose concentration, and risk of vascular disease: a collaborative meta-analysis of 102 prospective studies.

Emerging Risk Factors Collaboration, Sarwar N, Gao P, Seshasai SR, Gobin R et al.

Lancet 2010 · PMID 20609967 · PMCID PMC2904878

Insulin and HOMA-IR

Insulin is the hormone that moves sugar from your blood into your cells. It works like a key that opens your cells to glucose. In insulin resistance, your cells respond less well to that key. This is how it goes:

  1. When your cells respond less well to insulin, more glucose stays in your blood.
  2. Your pancreas then makes extra insulin to make up for it. That way your blood sugar can stay normal for a long time while your insulin is high.
  3. When it can no longer keep up, your blood sugar rises too.
  4. HOMA-IR is a calculation from your fasting glucose and insulin. It estimates how well your cells respond to insulin.

The 2023 international PCOS guideline calls the usual insulin tests inaccurate and advises against them in routine care, so a single result says little.

Reference range: fasting insulin below 17 mU/L (varies by lab) · HOMA-IR: no fixed Dutch cut-off · GP: not standard

Creatinine

Your kidneys are two filters in your lower back. They take waste out of your blood and turn it into urine. Creatinine is one of those waste products. Your muscles make it every day, in a fairly steady amount.

  1. Creatinine passes from your muscles into your blood. Your kidneys filter it out and you pass it in your urine.
  2. If your kidneys work less well, more creatinine stays in your blood.
  3. The lab uses your creatinine, age and sex to calculate the eGFR: an estimate of how much blood your kidneys clean per minute.
  4. A lot of muscle gives a higher creatinine, even when your kidneys are healthy.

In data from more than 105,000 people, an eGFR below 60 was linked to higher mortality. Between 75 and 105 there was no difference.

Reference range: women 45-84 · men 59-104 µmol/L · an eGFR below 60 counts as reduced (varies by lab) · GP: only with a reason

Lancet 2010 · PMID 20483451 · PMCID PMC3993088

Sodium and albumin

Sodium is the main salt in your blood. It decides where the water in your body goes. Albumin is the most common protein in your blood. Your liver makes it.

  1. Water moves towards where there's most salt. That's how sodium keeps fluid in your blood and around your cells.
  2. If you drink much more than you lose through urine and sweat, your blood gets diluted and your sodium falls.
  3. Albumin keeps fluid inside your blood vessels and carries substances such as hormones, calcium and medicines.
  4. During inflammation or illness, your liver makes less albumin.

Among 488 Boston Marathon runners, 13% had a low sodium at the finish, mostly those who gained weight during the race, a sign of drinking too much. A low albumin mainly reflects inflammation or illness, and says little about how much protein you eat.

Reference range: sodium 135-145 mmol/L · albumin 35-52 g/L (varies by lab) · GP: only with a reason

Hyponatremia among runners in the Boston Marathon.

N Engl J Med 2005 · PMID 15829535

Nutr Clin Pract 2021 · PMID 33125793

Thyroid

The hormones that set your metabolism.

TSH, free T4 and free T3

Your thyroid is a butterfly-shaped gland in your neck. It makes hormones that set the pace of your metabolism: how much energy your cells use, and for example your heart rate and body heat. It works like a thermostat:

  1. Your pituitary makes TSH. That's the signal telling your thyroid to make hormone.
  2. Your thyroid mainly makes T4, a storage form. Your tissues turn T4 into T3, the active form that makes your cells work faster.
  3. When there's enough T4 and T3, your pituitary makes less TSH. If your thyroid is underactive, TSH goes up. That's why TSH is usually the first test.
  4. Free T4 and free T3 are the part not bound to proteins in your blood, so they can act, just like with testosterone.

A clearly underactive thyroid is a well-known cause of fatigue. For a mild deviation, treatment didn't ease symptoms across 21 studies.

Careful. Do you take biotin (vitamin B7), for example in a hair, skin or nail supplement? It can make thyroid and other hormone results falsely high or falsely low. Tell your doctor or the lab before your blood test.

Reference range: TSH 0.27-4.2 mU/L · free T4 and free T3 vary by lab · GP: only with a reason, usually TSH only

Inflammation

How much inflammation is going on in your body.

hs-CRP

Inflammation is how your immune system responds to damage or infection. CRP (C-reactive protein) is a protein your liver makes when there's inflammation somewhere in your body. hs-CRP (high-sensitivity CRP) also measures very small amounts, so mild inflammation shows up.

  1. During inflammation, immune cells release signalling substances.
  2. Those signals make your liver produce CRP. With an infection, CRP can rise sharply within a day or two.
  3. Once the inflammation is over, CRP falls again within a few days. A level that stays slightly raised fits with mild inflammation that lingers.

Among 4,847 British civil servants without fatigue, those with a CRP of 1 mg/L or higher had 28% higher odds of becoming fatigued over the next 3 years.

Reference range: CRP below 5-10 mg/L (varies by lab) · sensitive cut-offs 1 and 3 mg/L · GP: regular CRP, only with a reason

Association of C-reactive protein and interleukin-6 with new-onset fatigue in the Whitehall II prospective cohort study.

Cho HJ, Kivimäki M, Bower JE, Irwin MR

Psychol Med 2013 · PMID 23151405 · PMCID PMC3819455

Blood and iron

Your red blood cells and your iron stores.

Haemoglobin and full blood count

Haemoglobin (Hb) is the protein in your red blood cells that carries oxygen. It contains iron, which is where the oxygen attaches. The full blood count also counts your blood cells and measures what they look like.

  1. In your lungs haemoglobin picks up oxygen. Your blood carries it to your muscles, brain and other organs.
  2. Your bone marrow makes new red blood cells every day. For that it needs iron, B12 and folate, among other things.
  3. With iron deficiency your red blood cells become smaller and paler. MCV measures their size, MCH and MCHC how much haemoglobin they hold.
  4. White blood cells are part of your immune system. Platelets help your blood clot when you have a cut.

Anaemia means too little haemoglobin. Iron-deficiency anaemia is common in women who menstruate and is a well-known cause of fatigue.

Reference range: Hb women 7.5-10.0 · men 8.5-11.0 mmol/L (varies by lab) · GP: Hb standard, full blood count only with a reason

Iron-deficiency anemia.

Camaschella C

N Engl J Med 2015 · PMID 25946282

Ferritin, iron and transferrin

You need iron to make haemoglobin and to make energy in your cells. Ferritin is a protein that stores iron, mainly in your liver: your iron store. Transferrin is the protein that carries iron through your blood. The transferrin saturation shows what share of it is carrying iron.

  1. Iron from your food is absorbed in your gut. What you don't need straight away goes into storage.
  2. A little ferritin ends up in your blood. The bigger your store, the higher your ferritin.
  3. When you lose iron, for example through your period, your store empties first. Only later does your haemoglobin fall.
  4. Inflammation raises ferritin, regardless of your store. A normal ferritin can then hide a low store.

In 198 women with fatigue and a ferritin below 50 µg/L, fatigue eased more with iron than with placebo (a dummy pill), even without anaemia.

Reference range: ferritin women 20-150 · men 25-250 µg/L (varies by lab) · Iron women 9-30 µmol/L · transferrin 1.8-3.8 g/L · transferrin saturation below 45% · GP: only with a reason, usually if Hb is low

Vaucher P, Druais PL, Waldvogel S, Favrat B

CMAJ 2012 · PMID 22777991 · PMCID PMC3414597

Liver

Two enzymes that show how your liver is doing.

ALT and GGT

Your liver is the largest organ inside your body. It processes nutrients, makes proteins and clears substances such as alcohol and medicines. ALT and GGT are enzymes: proteins that run chemical reactions in your cells.

  1. ALT sits mainly in your liver cells, GGT in your liver and bile ducts (the tubes that drain bile). A small amount always ends up in your blood.
  2. When liver cells are damaged or under strain, for example from fat in the liver, more ALT leaks into your blood.
  3. GGT rises with alcohol, some medicines and poor bile flow, among other things.

That's why doctors use them as a sign of strain on your liver. Across 19 studies with more than 9 million people, a higher GGT was linked to higher mortality. For ALT, the link differed by region.

Reference range: ALT up to 34 · GGT up to 35 U/L (women; men ALT up to 45 · GGT up to 40; varies by lab) · GP: only with a reason

Liver enzymes and risk of all-cause mortality in general populations: a systematic review and meta-analysis.

Kunutsor SK, Apekey TA, Seddoh D, Walley J

Int J Epidemiol 2014 · PMID 24585856

Nutrients

Vitamins you get from food and supplements.

Vitamin D

Your body makes vitamin D mostly in your skin, under the influence of sunlight. A small part comes from food, such as oily fish. It helps your body absorb calcium from food, for strong bones. It also plays a part in your muscles and immune system.

  1. Sunlight turns a precursor in your skin into vitamin D.
  2. Your liver turns it into 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the storage form. That's what the lab measures.
  3. Your kidneys turn that into the active form when your body needs it. The active form makes your gut absorb more calcium.

In 120 young adults with a level below 50 nmol/L, fatigue eased more after a single dose of vitamin D than after placebo. Experts don't agree on the best target.

Reference range: 30 nmol/L or higher (Dutch Health Council) · many labs 50-250 nmol/L · GP: not standard

Effect of vitamin D3 on self-perceived fatigue: A double-blind randomized placebo-controlled trial.

Nowak A, Boesch L, Andres E, Battegay E, Hornemann T et al.

Medicine (Baltimore) 2016 · PMID 28033244 · PMCID PMC5207540

Vitamin B12 and MMA

Vitamin B12 is found mainly in animal foods, such as meat, fish, eggs and dairy. You need it for your nerves and to make red blood cells. MMA (methylmalonic acid) is a substance your cells can only break down with the help of B12.

  1. To absorb B12 from food, your stomach makes a special protein (intrinsic factor). Together they are absorbed in your gut.
  2. Your blood carries B12, though your cells can only use part of it. So a B12 level on its own doesn't tell the whole story.
  3. If your cells are truly short of B12, they can't break down MMA. MMA then rises in your blood.

In people without a clear deficiency, extra B12 didn't help memory or mood across 16 studies.

Reference range: B12 145-569 pmol/L · grey zone 148-260 · MMA below 0.45 µmol/L (varies by lab) · GP: only with a reason

Vitamin B2 and B6

Vitamins B2 (riboflavin) and B6 are B vitamins found in foods such as dairy, meat, fish and wholegrains. They act as helpers: enzymes need them to do their job.

  1. B2 helps your cells get energy from food. It's also needed to turn B6 into its active form.
  2. Active B6 helps you process protein and make haemoglobin and signalling substances in your brain.

B2 and B6 contribute to normal energy metabolism. In 123 young British women with low B2 status, haemoglobin rose as their B2 status improved. In a large American study, women of childbearing age had lower B6 levels than men, and most women on the pill had a low B6.

Careful. Too much B6 from supplements can cause nerve symptoms, such as tingling or numbness in your hands and feet. B6 is in many multivitamins and sports products, so add them all up. Discuss a high B6 result with your doctor.

Reference range: B2 200-375 nmol/L · B6 35-110 nmol/L (varies by lab) · GP: not standard

Vitamin A

Vitamin A is needed for your eyes, skin and immune system. You get it ready-made from animal foods such as liver and dairy, or as beta-carotene from vegetables such as carrots, which your body turns into vitamin A itself.

  1. In your eyes, vitamin A is needed to turn light into a signal to your brain, especially in dim light.
  2. Your liver stores most of your vitamin A and releases it into your blood, bound to a carrier protein.
  3. That way your liver keeps the blood level steady for a long time, even as your store shrinks or grows.

Inflammation can also lower the result, so the level only partly reflects your store. In 22,748 pregnant women, a lot of vitamin A from supplements in the early weeks was linked to more birth defects.

Careful. Pregnant or trying to conceive? The Netherlands Nutrition Centre advises no liver, only a little liver products such as liver sausage and pâté, and no supplements with vitamin A. If you take a supplement, choose one made for pregnancy.

Reference range: 1.0-4.2 µmol/L (varies by lab) · GP: not standard

Biomarkers of Nutrition for Development (BOND)-Vitamin A Review.
