The blood markers I have tested

What they measure, the study behind each one, and my own results.

My symptoms came back every cycle, so for a long time I thought it was hormonal. For years I had everything tested. I had hospital appointments and went to clinics, in the US too. I had my blood, my hormones, my gut and even my DNA tested. More than €10,000 in total, and nobody could help me. Two clinics told me my liver was 'clogged', while the liver values in my blood were fine: GGT 12 and ALT 26. That's when I started reading my results myself. Below are the markers I have tested now, with the study behind each one, and for a few my own result.

What almost nobody knows

Five things that change how you look at a blood result.

1 in 20

Healthy people fall outside the range too

The reference range on your result is the range the middle 95% of a group of healthy people fall within. So about 1 in 20 healthy results falls outside it by definition. The more markers you have tested, the more likely one sits just outside. Have that one retested first, the same way.

Review of reference ranges

9 of 23

Your hair vitamin can throw off your thyroid test

Biotin is a B vitamin found in many hair, skin and nail supplements. Many lab tests use biotin to catch the substance they measure. In 6 healthy adults who took 10 mg a day for a week, a common dose, 9 of 23 of these tests gave a wrong result. They included tests of your thyroid, the gland in your neck that sets the pace of your metabolism. Taking biotin? Mention it before your blood is taken.

Study of 6 healthy adults

< 50

Your iron store can be low while your Hb is fine

Ferritin is your iron store. Haemoglobin (Hb) is the protein that carries oxygen in your blood, and it's usually what your GP checks first. When you lose iron, as we do every month with our period, your store empties first and your Hb only drops later. In 198 women with fatigue, a ferritin under 50 µg/L and a normal Hb, fatigue fell by 48% with iron and by 29% with a dummy pill (placebo). So ask for ferritin separately.

Study of 198 women

3 in 4

For most fatigue, the GP finds no cause

Of the people who see their GP about fatigue, about a quarter get an explanation. For 8% it's physical, for 16% psychological or social, like stress or worries. For about 3 in 4, the cause stays unknown. Tired for more than six months? Then the authors name wider blood tests and a look at your sleep as next steps.

Review in the Dutch Journal of Medicine

2.04 → 0.74

My testosterone: too high in May, normal in September

We women make testosterone too, in smaller amounts than men. In May 2026 mine was 2.04 nmol/L, above my lab's limit (1.67). In September it was 0.74. My SHBG, the protein that holds testosterone in your blood, went from 39.2 to 55.6 in that time. So two results can be far apart. Have an abnormal result retested before you draw conclusions.

Part 1

How do you get a result you can use?

What to sort out before your blood is taken.

How to prepare for your blood test

How and when you have blood taken changes your result. So do it the same way every time, and you can compare two results fairly.

  1. Go in the morning, before 10. Cortisol, your stress hormone, peaks just after you wake up and falls through the day. Zinc is lower in the afternoon too.
  2. Come fasted: nothing to eat from the evening before; water is fine. Fasting glucose and insulin need it, and zinc drops after a meal too.
  3. Taking biotin, say in a hair, skin or nail supplement? Mention it, and ask whether to stop first.
  4. Ill, or just over a cold? Put your test off. During an infection CRP, a protein your liver makes when there's inflammation, can rise up to 1,000-fold, and your ferritin goes up too.
  5. Having your hormones tested? Note your cycle day (day 1 is the first day of your period). Oestradiol and progesterone change a lot from week to week.
  6. Always use the same lab. Labs measure differently, and your own values usually sit in a narrow part of the range. So a small shift can already mean something for you.
  7. Keep every result, with the lab's range next to it. Not sure whether a difference counts? Ask the lab for the reference change value: the smallest change that's bigger than the normal swing.

Part 2

Is it your hormones?

Testosterone, SHBG, DHEA-S, oestradiol, progesterone and prolactin.

Testosterone, free testosterone and FAI

A hormone is a messenger. Your body makes it in one place, and through your blood it sends a signal to cells elsewhere. Testosterone is one of the androgens, the hormones often called 'male'. We women make them too, in small amounts, in our ovaries and adrenal glands - two small glands on top of your kidneys. Testosterone plays a part in your muscles, your bones and your sex drive.

  1. Most testosterone in your blood is bound to proteins, mainly SHBG. While it's bound, it does nothing.
  2. A small part floats freely. That free testosterone can enter your cells and send a signal there.
  3. The FAI (free androgen index) is a calculation: testosterone divided by SHBG, times 100. The higher the FAI, the more testosterone is free in proportion.

My FAI was 5.2 in May 2026, just under my lab's upper limit, and 1.3 in September. The 2023 international guideline uses free testosterone and the FAI to judge whether you have too many androgens. In 595 healthy women, testosterone fell mostly at a young age, and the menopause itself added no extra drop.

Reference range: testosterone 0.48-1.85 nmol/L · FAI below 4 (women under 50; varies by lab) · GP: only with a reason

SHBG

Think of SHBG as a sponge in your blood. It's a protein from your liver that holds on to sex hormones, like testosterone and oestrogen. SHBG stands for sex hormone binding globulin.

  1. Whatever is bound to SHBG can't reach your cells. Only the free part does anything.
  2. If your SHBG is low, more testosterone stays free. You then feel more of its effect, even if your total testosterone is normal.
  3. If your SHBG is high, more is bound and less is free.

My SHBG was 39.2 nmol/L in May 2026: within range, on the low side. In women after the menopause, a low SHBG was linked to a much higher risk of type 2 diabetes. The pill raises SHBG, which makes your hormone results hard to read while you're on it.

DHEA-S

Your adrenal glands make DHEA every day, a hormone your body uses as a raw material. Your tissues convert it on the spot into testosterone or oestrogen. In your blood it's mostly found as DHEA-S, a more stable form the lab can measure well. Because DHEA-S comes almost only from your adrenal glands, it shows how many androgens they make.

My DHEA-S was 8.7 µmol/L in May 2026, just under my lab's upper limit (8.8). DHEA-S falls with age, and the 2023 guideline calls it an extra test that is less precise than testosterone. In 23 studies of 1,188 women after the menopause, extra DHEA didn't clearly improve sex drive.

Reference range: women aged 30-39 1.6-6.2 µmol/L, higher when younger (my lab: 2.6-8.8); falls with age (varies by lab) · GP: not standard

Oestradiol and progesterone

Oestradiol is our main oestrogen, a female sex hormone from your ovaries. It makes the lining of your womb grow and matters for your bones too. Progesterone is made mainly after you ovulate. It gets that lining ready for a possible pregnancy.

  1. In the first half of your cycle, a follicle with an egg grows in your ovary. It makes more and more oestradiol.
  2. After ovulation, when the egg is released, what's left of the follicle makes progesterone.
  3. If you don't get pregnant, both drop before your period.

Because they swing so much, you measure oestradiol on day 2 to 5 and progesterone about 7 days before your next period, around day 21 in a 28-day cycle. In 107 women, a single progesterone result from about 16 nmol/L was a reliable sign that ovulation had happened.

Reference range: oestradiol follicular phase 77-921 pmol/L · progesterone luteal phase 3.8-51 nmol/L (varies by lab) · GP: only with a reason

Prolactin

Prolactin comes from your pituitary, the small gland under your brain that steers many other hormones. After giving birth, it makes breast milk. Normally your brain keeps it low with the messenger dopamine. A lot of prolactin holds back the hormones that start ovulation.

A high level can go with irregular or missing periods. According to the guideline, some high results are macroprolactin, a large form that is less active. Stress during the blood draw can raise the level too.

Reference range: women 110-560 mU/L (varies by lab) · GP: only with a reason

Part 3

Why are you tired?

Iron, thyroid, vitamin D, inflammation and cortisol.

For a long time I felt weak. In June I got dizzy when I stood up, and in September everything went black once and I had to sleep for two hours. My iron and my thyroid were fine in May. I was eating and drinking too little, though. More food, strength training and more water with salt gave me more energy. It still goes up and down.

Haemoglobin and blood count

Every red blood cell is full of haemoglobin (Hb), the protein that carries oxygen from your lungs to your muscles, brain and organs. It contains iron, and that's where the oxygen attaches. The blood count also counts your red and white blood cells and your platelets, and looks at what they're like.

  1. Your bone marrow makes new red blood cells every day, using iron, B12 and folic acid, among other things.
  2. With iron deficiency they get smaller and paler. MCV measures their size, MCH and MCHC how much haemoglobin they hold.

Anaemia means too little haemoglobin. Anaemia from iron deficiency is common in women who have periods, and fatigue is a well-known complaint with it.

Reference range: Hb women 7.5-10.0 · men 8.5-11.0 mmol/L (varies by lab) · GP: Hb standard, full blood count only with a reason

Iron-deficiency anemia.

Camaschella C

Ferritin, iron and transferrin

You need iron to make haemoglobin and to make energy in your cells. Ferritin is the protein that stores iron, mainly in your liver: your iron store. Transferrin carries iron through your blood, and transferrin saturation shows how much of it is carrying iron.

  1. Whatever you don't need straight away goes into storage. The bigger your store, the higher your ferritin.
  2. When you lose iron, as we do every month with our period, your store empties first. Only later does your haemoglobin drop.
  3. Inflammation raises ferritin, separately from your store. A normal ferritin can then hide a low store.

My ferritin was 100 µg/L in May 2026, with a low CRP on the same day. So my store was fine. In 198 women with fatigue, a ferritin under 50 and no anaemia, fatigue fell more with iron than with placebo.

Reference range: ferritin women 20-150 · men 25-250 µg/L (varies by lab) · Iron women 9-30 µmol/L · transferrin 1.8-3.8 g/L · transferrin saturation below 45% · GP: only with a reason, usually if Hb is low

TSH, free T4 and free T3

In your neck sits a butterfly-shaped gland: your thyroid. It sets the pace of your metabolism, so how much energy your cells use, and things like your heart rate and body heat. It works like a thermostat.

  1. Your pituitary makes TSH, the signal telling your thyroid to make hormone.
  2. Your thyroid mainly makes T4, a storage form. Your tissues turn T4 into T3, the active form. Free T4 and free T3 are the part floating loose in your blood that can do its work.
  3. When there's enough T4 and T3, your pituitary makes less TSH. If your thyroid is too slow, TSH rises. That's why TSH is usually the first test.

My TSH was 1.97 in May 2026, in the middle of the range. Fatigue is a well-known complaint with a clearly underactive thyroid. When the thyroid was only mildly off, treatment didn't help symptoms in 21 studies.

Careful. Do you take biotin (vitamin B7), for example in a hair, skin or nail supplement? It can make thyroid and other hormone results falsely high or falsely low. Tell your doctor or the lab before your blood is taken.

Vitamin D

You make vitamin D mostly yourself, in your skin, when the sun shines on it. A small part comes from food, like oily fish. It helps your body absorb calcium for strong bones, and plays a part in your muscles and your immune system.

  1. Your liver turns it into 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the storage form. That's what the lab measures.
  2. Your kidneys make the active form from it when your body needs it.

In 120 young adults with a level under 50 nmol/L, fatigue improved more after a single dose of vitamin D than after placebo. Experts don't agree on the best target level.

Reference range: 30 nmol/L or higher (Dutch Health Council) · many labs 50-250 nmol/L · GP: not standard

hs-CRP

When your immune system responds to damage or an infection, that's called inflammation. Your liver then makes CRP (C-reactive protein), a protein. With an infection CRP rises sharply within a day or two, then falls again. The hs-CRP (high-sensitivity CRP) test also picks up very small amounts. If it stays slightly raised without an infection, that fits with low-grade inflammation that lingers.

Among 4,847 British civil servants who weren't fatigued, people with a CRP of 1 mg/L or higher had 28% higher odds of becoming fatigued over the next 3 years.

Reference range: CRP below 5-10 mg/L (varies by lab) · sensitive cut-offs 1 and 3 mg/L · GP: regular CRP, only with a reason

Cortisol (morning)

Cortisol, your main stress hormone, follows a fixed daily rhythm. Your adrenal glands make it. It releases energy, like sugar into your blood, and helps your body deal with stress and inflammation.

  1. Your pituitary sends the messenger hormone ACTH to your adrenal glands. In response, they make cortisol.
  2. Just after you wake up, cortisol rises most. Then it falls through the day, until it's lowest at night.

That's why doctors use a morning level as the first check of whether your adrenal glands make enough cortisol. 'Adrenal fatigue' is often given as an explanation for tiredness, but a review of 58 studies found no evidence for it.

Part 4

How is your metabolism doing?

Your blood sugar, your insulin and the fats in your blood.

Fasting glucose

Your cells and your brain run mostly on glucose, the sugar in your blood. Your fasting glucose is your blood sugar after a night without food.

  1. After you eat, your pancreas, an organ behind your stomach, makes the hormone insulin. That makes your cells take up the glucose.
  2. At night your liver slowly releases glucose from its store, so your blood sugar stays steady.

In data from almost 700,000 people, a fasting glucose between 3.9 and 5.6 mmol/L was not linked to heart disease. Above that, the risk rose slightly. Diabetes about doubled the risk of heart disease, and in women that link was stronger than in men.

Reference range: below 6.1 mmol/L · 6.1-6.9 raised · 7.0 or higher is the diabetes threshold (NHG) · GP: only with a reason

Diabetes mellitus, fasting blood glucose concentration, and risk of vascular disease: a collaborative meta-analysis of 102 prospective studies.

Emerging Risk Factors Collaboration, Sarwar N, Gao P, Seshasai SR, Gobin R et al.

Lancet 2010 · PMID 20609967 · PMCID PMC2904878

Insulin and HOMA-IR

Think of insulin as a key. The hormone opens your cells, so the sugar in your blood can get in. With insulin resistance, your cells respond less well to that key.

  1. Your pancreas then makes extra insulin. So your blood sugar can stay normal for a long time while your insulin is high, until your blood sugar rises too.
  2. HOMA-IR is a calculation using your fasting glucose and insulin. It estimates how well your cells respond to insulin.

The 2023 international PCOS guideline calls the usual insulin tests inaccurate and advises against them in routine care. So one result says little.

Reference range: fasting insulin below 17 mU/L (varies by lab) · HOMA-IR: no fixed Dutch cut-off · GP: not standard

LDL and non-HDL cholesterol

Your cells need cholesterol, for example as a building block for hormones. Fat doesn't dissolve in blood, so cholesterol travels in little packages of fat and protein. LDL carries cholesterol from your liver to the rest of your body. Non-HDL is all cholesterol except HDL: LDL plus the other packages that can get into your artery wall.

  1. When there's a lot of LDL in your blood, more particles get stuck in the wall of your blood vessels. Immune cells clear them up, fill up with fat themselves and stay there.
  2. Over the years this builds a plaque: a thickening that narrows the blood vessel.

In more than 184,000 women, the 30-year risk of heart and vascular disease rose from 8% with a non-HDL below 2.6 mmol/L to 34% at 5.7 or higher.

Reference range: LDL below 3.0 · non-HDL below 3.8 · total cholesterol below 5.0 mmol/L (varies by lab) · Total/HDL ratio: below 5 (some labs below 4) · GP: only with a reason

Brunner FJ, Waldeyer C, Ojeda F, Salomaa V, Kee F et al.

Lancet 2019 · PMID 31810609 · PMCID PMC6913519

HDL cholesterol

HDL is the clean-up crew. It picks up spare cholesterol from your tissues and artery wall and takes it back to your liver. Your liver gets rid of it through bile, a fluid it releases into your gut.

In more than 116,000 Danes, both a low and an extremely high HDL were linked to higher mortality. In women, mortality was lowest around 2.4 mmol/L and higher from 3.5.

Reference range: women above 1.2 · men above 1.0 mmol/L (varies by lab) · GP: only with a reason

Eur Heart J 2017 · PMID 28419274

Triglycerides

Triglycerides are the form in which your body carries and stores fat, as fuel for your muscles and other cells. After a meal your gut sends fat into your blood as triglycerides, so the level rises after eating. Your liver makes them too, partly from sugar and alcohol your body doesn't need right away.

In 26,509 healthy American women, triglycerides measured 2 to 4 hours after eating were most strongly linked to later heart and vascular disease. Fasting levels added little on top of the other risk factors.

Part 5

Are your liver and kidneys doing their job?

Your liver, your kidneys, and the balance of salt and fluid.

ALT and GGT: my 'clogged' liver

Two clinics told me my liver was 'clogged'. Your liver is the largest organ inside your body. It processes nutrients, makes proteins and clears substances such as alcohol and medicines. Whether it's under strain shows partly in two enzymes in your blood: ALT and GGT. Enzymes are proteins that make reactions in your cells happen.

  1. ALT sits mainly in your liver cells, GGT in your liver and bile ducts, the tubes that drain bile. A little always ends up in your blood.
  2. When liver cells are damaged or overloaded, for example by fat in the liver, more ALT leaks into your blood.
  3. GGT rises with alcohol, some medicines and a sluggish flow of bile, among other things.

In May 2026 my GGT was 12 and my ALT 26 U/L, both well under my lab's limits (40 and 35). I don't drink alcohol. In 19 studies with more than 9 million people, a higher GGT was linked to higher mortality. For ALT, that link differed by region.

Reference range: ALT up to 34 · GGT up to 35 U/L (women; men ALT up to 45 · GGT up to 40; varies by lab) · GP: only with a reason

Liver enzymes and risk of all-cause mortality in general populations: a systematic review and meta-analysis.

Kunutsor SK, Apekey TA, Seddoh D, Walley J

Creatinine

Your kidneys are two filters in your lower back. They take waste out of your blood and turn it into urine. Creatinine is one of those waste products: your muscles make it every day. If your kidneys work less well, more of it stays in your blood.

  1. The lab converts your creatinine, age and sex into the eGFR: an estimate of how much blood your kidneys clean per minute.
  2. A lot of muscle gives a higher creatinine, even with healthy kidneys. If you do a lot of strength training, keep that in mind.

In data from more than 105,000 people, an eGFR below 60 was linked to higher mortality. Between 75 and 105 there was no difference.

Reference range: women 45-84 · men 59-104 µmol/L · an eGFR below 60 counts as reduced (varies by lab) · GP: only with a reason

Sodium and albumin

For a long time I drank too little. Now I drink more water, with salt, and since I've been drinking more I have a lot more energy. Sodium is the main salt in your blood. It decides where the water in your body goes, because water moves to where there's most salt. Albumin is the most common protein in your blood. Your liver makes it. It holds fluid in your blood vessels and carries substances such as hormones, calcium and medicines.

Drinking too much is possible too. If you drink far more than you pee and sweat out, your blood gets diluted and your sodium drops. Of 488 Boston Marathon runners, 13% had a low sodium afterwards, mostly those who gained weight during the race, a sign of drinking too much. A low albumin mostly says something about inflammation or illness, and little about how much protein you eat.

Reference range: sodium 135-145 mmol/L · albumin 35-52 g/L (varies by lab) · GP: only with a reason

Aldosterone

Aldosterone is a hormone from your adrenal glands. It controls how much salt and fluid your body holds on to, and with that your blood pressure.

  1. When your blood pressure drops, or you're low on salt, your kidneys release renin. In response your adrenal glands make aldosterone, which makes your kidneys hold on to salt and water.
  2. If your adrenal glands make too much aldosterone on their own, your body actually holds renin back. That's why doctors judge aldosterone together with renin.

Among 1,672 people with high blood pressure in general practice, 6 in 100 made too much aldosterone. The 2025 guideline advises testing everyone with high blood pressure for this.

Part 6

Are you getting enough?

B12, B2, B6, vitamin A and zinc.

Vitamin B12 and MMA

B12 is found mostly in animal foods: meat, fish, eggs and dairy. You need it for your nerves and to make red blood cells. MMA (methylmalonic acid) is a substance your cells can only break down with the help of B12.

  1. To absorb B12 from your food, your stomach makes a special protein: intrinsic factor.
  2. Your blood carries B12, but your cells can only really use part of it. So a B12 level on its own doesn't tell the whole story.
  3. If your cells really lack B12, they can't break MMA down, and MMA rises in your blood.

In people without a clear deficiency, extra B12 didn't help memory or mood in 16 studies.

Reference range: B12 145-569 pmol/L · grey zone 148-260 · MMA below 0.45 µmol/L (varies by lab) · GP: only with a reason

Vitamins B2 and B6

Vitamin B2 (riboflavin) and B6 are found in dairy, meat, fish and wholegrains, among other foods. Enzymes need them to do their work. B2 helps your cells get energy from your food and makes B6 active. B6 helps make haemoglobin, among other things.

In 123 young British women with low B2 status, haemoglobin rose as their B2 status improved. In a large American study, women of childbearing age had lower B6 levels than men, and most women on the pill had a low B6.

Careful. Too much B6 from supplements can cause nerve symptoms, like tingling or numbness in your hands and feet. B6 is in many multivitamins and sports products, so add everything up. Discuss a high B6 level with your doctor.

Reference range: B2 200-375 nmol/L · B6 35-110 nmol/L (varies by lab) · GP: not standard

Vitamin A

You need vitamin A for your eyes, your skin and your immune system. You get it ready-made from animal foods such as liver and dairy, or as beta-carotene from vegetables like carrots, which your body converts itself. In your eyes it's needed to turn light into a signal to your brain, especially in the dark.

Your liver stores most of your vitamin A and keeps the blood level steady for a long time, even as your store shrinks or grows. Inflammation can also lower the result, so the level only partly follows your store. In 22,748 pregnant women, a lot of vitamin A from supplements in the first weeks was linked to more birth defects.

Careful. Pregnant or trying to get pregnant? The Netherlands Nutrition Centre then advises no liver, only small amounts of liver products like liver sausage and pâté, and no supplements with vitamin A. If you use a supplement, choose one made for pregnancy.

Reference range: 1.0-4.2 µmol/L (varies by lab) · GP: not standard

Zinc

Zinc is a mineral your body needs for a great many processes, including your immune system and your skin. You get it mostly from meat, fish, shellfish, cheese, wholegrains and pulses.

  1. Only about 1% of all the zinc in your body is in your blood.
  2. Your zinc level drops after eating, over the course of the day and with inflammation. Some medicines and hormones, like the pill, affect it too.
  3. That's why the lower limit depends on when you're tested. For women it's 10.7 µmol/L for a fasting morning test, and about 9.0 in the afternoon.

My zinc was 11 µmol/L in December 2025, just above my lab's lower limit of 10.7. That close to the limit? Get retested, fasting and in the morning. Experts find the blood level useful, but it mainly responds to a real shortfall or a supplement, and less to the zinc in your food.

Reference range: women lower limit 10.7 µmol/L for a fasting morning test (varies by lab) · GP: not standard

Biomarkers of Nutrition for Development (BOND)-Zinc Review.

King JC, Brown KH, Gibson RS, Krebs NF, Lowe NM et al.

J Nutr 2015 · PMID 26962190 · PMCID PMC4807640

I have my blood tested a few times a year. In between, my ring measures my sleep, my resting heart rate and my HRV almost every night - how much the time between my heartbeats varies. Those are biomarkers too: measurable values from your body. You can start with them today, without a blood test. Always discuss an abnormal result with your GP.
