Acne: where it comes from

Hormones, insulin, diet, gut, products and more - and what the evidence says for each cause.

Online you read about a new cause of acne every week: your hormones, your liver, your gut. Some of it holds up, some doesn't. On this page I explain step by step how a spot forms, what terms like insulin resistance, androgens and SHBG mean, and what's really known about each cause.

Part 1

How acne forms

Four steps, and where the causes come in.

How a spot forms

Your skin has thousands of hair follicles, tiny tubes that a hair grows out of. Each follicle has an oil gland attached. It makes sebum: an oily layer that keeps your skin supple and protected. Acne starts in that follicle, in four steps:

  1. More sebum. The oil gland makes more sebum than usual, often because of hormones.
  2. Clogged pore. Skin cells inside the follicle don't shed properly. They stick together with the sebum into a plug. If it stays closed, you see a small white bump (a whitehead). If it opens to the air, it turns dark (a blackhead).
  3. Bacteria. The skin bacterium C. acnes grows well in that closed, oily plug. This bacterium normally lives on your skin.
  4. Inflammation. Your immune system reacts to the plug and the bacteria. That gives a red bump, a spot with pus, or with deep inflammation a painful lump.

Genes play a big part: in a study of 1,557 female twin pairs, acne was 81% heritable. The diagram below shows at which step each cause comes in.

Part 2

Hormones and insulin

What's true about high testosterone, your cycle, PCOS and insulin.

Androgens and testosterone

Androgens are hormones often called 'male', but women make them too, in the ovaries and the adrenal glands. Testosterone is the best known. This is how they act on your skin:

  1. Testosterone travels through your blood to your skin. Most of it is bound to a protein, SHBG. Only the loose, free testosterone can act.
  2. In the oil gland an enzyme (5-alpha-reductase) turns testosterone into DHT, a much stronger form.
  3. DHT makes the oil gland grow and produce more sebum. That's step 1 of acne.

Most people with acne have normal hormone levels in their blood. According to the Dutch GP guideline, that skin enzyme often works harder in acne, so your skin reacts more strongly, not necessarily your blood. In small groups at specialist clinics, half to just over 60% of women did have at least one raised value, often free testosterone or DHEA-S (an androgen from the adrenal gland), with lower SHBG.

Your cycle

44% of women notice their acne flares in the week before their period. In that week oestrogen and progesterone fall. How exactly that shift leads to spots isn't well understood yet. Acne is also common in adult women: in their twenties about half had it, in their thirties over a third. My hormones page shows how your hormones move over a month.

PCOS

PCOS is a hormone condition in which the ovaries make more androgens and the cycle is often irregular. More androgens means more sebum, so acne is more common in PCOS: in 43% of women with PCOS, against 21% without. The other way round, 17 to 37% of women with acne had PCOS in small studies. Acne on its own, without for example excess hair or an irregular cycle, is a weak sign of raised hormones. My PCOS page explains how it works.

Contraception

Hormonal contraception changes your own hormones, and with them your skin. This is how:

  1. The combined pill contains oestrogen and a progestin (a synthetic progesterone). The oestrogen makes your liver produce more SHBG, which holds on to more testosterone, and the pill quiets your ovaries. Less free testosterone means less sebum.
  2. Progestin-only methods, such as the implant, the injection and the hormonal IUD, lack that oestrogen. Some progestins also resemble androgens a little.
  3. When you stop the pill, your SHBG falls again and your ovaries make their own androgens again.

In 31 trials every combined pill reduced acne compared with placebo. With a hormonal IUD, 15% of women got acne. Acne after stopping the pill has never been properly studied, but given the steps above it makes sense that your skin can change then.

Insulin and insulin resistance

Insulin is a hormone from your pancreas. After eating your blood sugar rises, and insulin works like a key: it opens your cells so the sugar can get in and your blood sugar falls again. In insulin resistance your cells respond less well to that key. Your pancreas then makes more and more insulin to get the same result. Your blood sugar can stay normal for a long time, while your insulin is too high. This is how that can reach your skin:

  1. High insulin prompts the ovaries to make more testosterone.
  2. High insulin makes your liver produce less SHBG. More free testosterone is then left.
  3. Insulin also raises IGF-1, a growth hormone that makes oil-gland cells produce more fat.
  4. Together that gives more sebum: step 1 of acne.

People with acne more often have insulin resistance: in a review of 13 studies their HOMA-IR (a measure of insulin resistance) was higher, and in men 22% had insulin resistance against 11% without acne. That's a link, not proof it's the cause. A diet low in fast carbohydrates helped in two small studies, but in the larger one participants also lost weight, and according to the Dutch GP guideline no diet is convincingly proven.

And the aromatase route?

Aromatase is an enzyme that turns androgens into oestrogens. Online you'll read that acne comes from too much or too little aromatase, or from 'oestrogen dominance': too much oestrogen relative to progesterone. No research in humans shows that. What is known points the other way. Women who lack aromatase are left with more androgens and actually get acne. And oestrogen lowers sebum: in a pill, the drop in sebum came mainly from the oestrogen.

Part 3

Diet, gut and liver

What's really known, and what mostly lives online.

Milk and sugar

Milk contains proteins that raise your insulin and IGF-1: in a study in boys, casein raised IGF-1 by 15%, and whey raised insulin by 21%. Researchers think milk can raise sebum that way, through the route above. In a review of 14 studies with 78,529 people, the odds of acne were 25% higher with dairy, and 32% with skimmed milk. These aren't experiments, and the link weakened after taking other factors into account. Yoghurt and cheese showed no link. Lots of fat and sugar was also linked to acne, and in a small study milk chocolate gave more spots within 48 hours than sweets with the same amount of sugar.

Too much B12

You need vitamin B12 for your nerves and your blood. But the acne bacterium C. acnes uses B12 too. This is how too much B12 can cause spots:

  1. Normally the bacterium makes B12 itself.
  2. If you take in a lot of B12, for example through injections, more of it reaches your skin. The bacterium then stops making B12.
  3. Instead the bacterium makes more porphyrins, substances that irritate your skin and fuel inflammation.

In one study, 1 in 10 healthy people got acne within a week of taking B12. In a series of 5 women, the spots came within a week to 5 months of B12, and cleared 3 to 6 weeks after stopping. It happens in a small number of people. If you take B12, have your level measured first.

Protein shakes

Whey is the milk protein found in many protein shakes. There are reports of young people whose acne came with whey and cleared when they stopped. But in a 6-month study of 49 men, 30 grams of whey a day didn't make their acne worse. So the evidence is mixed.

Your gut

Your gut wall is only one cell layer thick. Those cells are held together by tiny junctions. They let nutrients through but keep bacteria and their waste out. This is how a leaky gut can reach your skin:

  1. Zonulin is a protein that controls how open those junctions are. More zonulin means they open further: a more permeable, 'leaky' gut.
  2. Pieces of bacteria can then get into your blood.
  3. Your immune system reacts to that, also outside your gut.
  4. That extra inflammation can add to step 4 of acne.

There are signs of this indirect route. In acne, I-FABP was higher - a marker of damage to the gut wall - and the higher it was, the more severe the acne. As early as 1983, blood from people with severe acne reacted more strongly to bacterial toxins. People with acne also have different gut bacteria in small studies, and probiotics gave mixed results. These are small studies showing a link, not yet proof of cause. With zonulin, watch the test: the widely used tests don't measure zonulin itself but other proteins.

Liver and detox

Your liver clears hormones: it attaches a tag so your body can remove them through bile and urine. Online this is called the 'detox phases', and you'll read that a slow liver causes acne. No research in humans links your liver or those phases to acne. DIM and calcium-D-glucarate are in many acne supplements, but have never been studied in acne. Your liver values only matter in acne if you take certain medicines. How your body clears hormones is explained on my hormones page.

Part 4

Skin and products

What clogs your skin, and what to leave alone.

Pore-clogging products

'Comedogenic' means a product can clog pores and cause blackheads: step 2 of acne. Heavy creams and oils can do that. In the 1970s, mild acne in a third of adult women was linked to cosmetics. Products have changed since. Lists of 'comedogenic' ingredients are unreliable, because a finished product with such an ingredient often doesn't clog, and 'non-comedogenic' isn't a protected term. Hair pomade gave 70% of regular users blackheads on the forehead.

Friction and picking

Pressure and friction irritate the follicle and push the plug deeper. This is called acne mechanica, and it comes from things like a face mask, helmet or phone. People who wore a mask for more than 4 hours a day had skin problems almost twice as often. Picking does the same: it makes the inflammation worse and leaves scars more often.

Washing

Acne doesn't come from dirty skin: the plug sits under your skin, inside the follicle. Scrubbing actually irritates your skin. Wash your face gently twice a day. In a small study, washing once a day gave more inflamed spots, and more than twice didn't help further.

What you can do yourself

And the sun?

Many people think sun dries out acne. It doesn't. UV changes sebum so that it clogs more. And after a spot a dark mark often remains, because your skin makes extra pigment. Sun makes those marks darker. Sunscreen helps prevent them.

Part 5

What else plays a part

Medicines, stress, smoking and more.

Other causes

These are the causes most often overlooked:

Medicines

Among them corticosteroids, testosterone and anabolic steroids, lithium and some epilepsy drugs. 38% of Dutch anabolic steroid users got acne.

Steroid and lightening creams

Creams with corticosteroids, including some skin-lightening creams, can cause acne.

Stress

During exams acne worsened with more stress, in a small study. It's a link; Thuisarts says stress isn't a cause.

Sleep

Only small studies, showing a link and no proof of cause.

Smoking

Mixed: in a review of 17 studies the link wasn't significant, a little stronger in adults.

Part 6

Measuring

When to have hormones checked, and which values.

Which blood tests, and when

Have hormones checked mainly if you have more than acne alone: an irregular cycle, lots of hair growth, hair loss, acne that suddenly starts in adulthood, or acne that comes back quickly after treatment. If you're on the pill, androgen values are only reliable 3 months after stopping. These values then make sense:

Total and free testosterone

Ideally measured with the most accurate method (LC-MS/MS).

SHBG

The protein that binds testosterone. Low SHBG means more free testosterone.

DHEA-S and androstenedione

If testosterone is normal.

TSH, prolactin, 17-OH-progesterone and FSH

To rule out other causes.

Glucose or HbA1c

For your blood sugar. Fasting insulin and HOMA-IR are advised against in the guideline, because they're inaccurate.

Vitamin B12

If you take or receive B12.

Part 7

Treatment

What the GP and dermatologist do.

What treatment looks like

The GP chooses treatment based on how severe your acne is. Each treatment works on a different step:

  1. Mild: a skin treatment, such as benzoyl peroxide, which curbs bacteria, or a retinoid, which opens clogged pores.
  2. Moderate: plus a skin antibiotic, against bacteria and inflammation.
  3. Severe: an antibiotic course of up to 3 months.
  4. After that isotretinoin, through the GP or the dermatologist. It shrinks the oil glands.

The pill is only given if you also want contraception. Spironolactone isn't licensed for acne in the Netherlands. Apart from benzoyl peroxide, all these are prescription treatments: discuss them with your GP.

Never start without data. Don't take supplements for acne without measuring: B12 can actually make acne worse, and DIM has never been studied in acne.
