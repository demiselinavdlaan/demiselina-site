Acne: where it comes from

My own acne, my results, and what the studies say about each cause.

Every cycle, just after I ovulate, my acne comes up. It stays until my period. The rest of the month my skin is good. I had my blood, my hormones and my gut tested, and I still don't know the cause. What I do know now is how acne forms, and what's actually known about each cause. I'm sharing that here, with my own results and a check for your acne.

What almost nobody knows

Every number comes from a study, except the last one. That's my own blood.

81%

Acne is largely in your genes

In a study of 1,557 female twin pairs, 81% of the differences in acne came down to genes. That means the tendency you get from your parents. It helps explain why one person gets acne from something another person never notices.

Study of 1,557 twin pairs

44%

For almost half, acne flares just before their period

Of 400 women with acne, 44% noticed it flares just before their period. That's when oestrogen and progesterone fall, two hormones from your ovaries. So keep track of which day of your cycle your acne comes up.

Survey of 400 women

1 in 10

Vitamin B12 can trigger acne

In one study, 1 in 10 healthy people got acne within a week of extra B12. In 5 women, the acne cleared 3 to 6 weeks after they stopped B12. Taking B12 or getting injections? Have your level measured.

Small studies

32%

Skimmed milk is more strongly linked to acne than whole milk

In a review of 14 studies with 78,529 people, the odds of acne were 32% higher with skimmed milk, and 25% with dairy in general. Yoghurt and cheese showed no link. These aren't experiments, so whether milk itself causes acne, we don't know yet.

Part 1

My acne, every single cycle

What I had tested, what came out, and what I still don't know.

It starts just after I ovulate

My acne follows my cycle. Your cycle runs from the first day of your period to the day before the next one. Since July 2026 I've kept a diary of which day my acne comes up.

  1. Until I ovulate, my skin is good. Ovulation is the moment your ovary releases an egg.
  2. Just after I ovulate, around day 17 to 22, the acne comes up. Mostly along my jawline and on my chin, and it's worst in the evening.
  3. It stays until my period, then fades again.

Nobody could help me

Because my acne follows my cycle so closely, for a long time I thought it was hormonal. And my acne wasn't the only thing that came back every cycle. For years I had everything tested: my blood, my DNA, my hormones, my gut. I went to hospital and to clinics, as far as the United States. In total I spent more than €10,000 on it. Nobody could help me.

My testosterone was high, and then it wasn't

In May 2026 something showed up in my blood: my testosterone was too high. Testosterone is a hormone women make too, just less than men. It makes your oil glands produce more sebum, the oily layer on your skin. SHBG is a protein that holds on to testosterone in your blood. The lower your SHBG, the more testosterone is free to act.

  1. May 2026: testosterone 2.04 nmol/L, above the limit of 1.67. My SHBG was 39.2: within range, but on the low side.
  2. September 2026: testosterone 0.74, back within range. My SHBG had risen to 55.6.
  3. My acne still came back every cycle.

That fits what the Dutch GP guideline says. Most people with acne have normal hormone levels in their blood, because the skin converts testosterone faster itself. So a normal result doesn't rule hormones out, and a high result doesn't explain everything.

I had my gut tested too

In February 2026 I did the GI-MAP, a stool test from the United States. It measures how your digestion works and how your gut responds.

  1. Elastase, an enzyme your pancreas uses to break down food, was 186. The limit is 200, so I was just under it. In almost half of people whose low result was repeated, the second test was normal.
  2. Secretory IgA, a measure of the immune defence in your gut wall, was high: 4,766. No guideline uses this value to establish anything.
  3. No study links these two values to acne.

Where I am now

I still have acne, and I don't know the cause. What I do know comes from my diary and my results.

  • Almost every cycle it starts just after I ovulate. In October 2026 it came up again, from the evening of day 16.
  • I have my blood measured again. That's how I saw in September that my testosterone was back to normal, while my acne stayed.
  • My skin routine is on my skincare page.
  • What I learn along the way, I add to this page.

Part 3

What happens in your skin?

Four steps, and where each cause comes in.

From oil to a spot

Your skin has thousands of hair follicles, tiny tubes that a hair grows out of. Each follicle has an oil gland attached. It makes sebum, an oily layer that keeps your skin supple and protected. Acne starts in that follicle.

  1. More sebum. The oil gland makes more sebum than usual, often because of hormones.
  2. Clogged pore. Skin cells inside the follicle don't shed properly. They stick together with the sebum into a plug. If it stays closed, you see a small white bump (a whitehead). If it opens to the air, it turns dark (a blackhead).
  3. Bacteria. The skin bacterium C. acnes grows well in that closed, oily plug. This bacterium normally lives on your skin.
  4. Inflammation. Your immune system reacts to the plug and the bacteria. That gives a red bump, a spot with pus, or with deep inflammation a painful lump.

For me it's a mix of step 2 and step 4: clogged pores and inflamed bumps. The diagram above shows at which step each cause comes in. Whether you get acne is largely down to your genes: in the twin study at the top, acne was 81% heritable.

Part 4

Is it your hormones?

Testosterone, your cycle, PCOS and the pill.

Women make testosterone too

Androgens are hormones often called 'male'. Women make them too, in the ovaries and the adrenal glands. Testosterone is the best known.

  1. Testosterone travels through your blood to your skin. Most of it is bound to a protein, SHBG. Only the loose, free testosterone can act.
  2. In the oil gland an enzyme (5-alpha-reductase) turns testosterone into DHT, a much stronger form.
  3. DHT makes the oil gland grow and produce more sebum. That's step 1 of acne.

Most people with acne have normal hormone levels in their blood. According to the Dutch GP guideline, that skin enzyme often works harder in acne: your skin reacts more strongly, even when your blood is normal. In small groups at specialist clinics, half to just over 60% of women did have at least one raised value, often free testosterone or DHEA-S (an androgen from the adrenal gland), with lower SHBG. My acne stayed too when my testosterone was back to normal.

Why it flares before your period

Of 400 women with acne, 44% noticed it flares just before their period. That's when oestrogen and progesterone fall. Nobody knows exactly how that drop leads to acne yet. Mine starts just after I ovulate, well before those last days. Acne is also common in adult women: in a survey, about half of women in their twenties had it, and over a third in their thirties. Could it be PCOS?

PCOS is a hormone condition in which the ovaries make more androgens and the cycle is often irregular. More androgens means more sebum, so acne is more common in PCOS: in 43% of women with PCOS, against 21% without. The other way round, 17 to 37% of women with acne had PCOS in small studies. If you only have acne, without for example excess hair or an irregular cycle, that's only a weak sign of raised hormones. My PCOS page explains how it works.

The pill, the coil and your skin

Hormonal contraception changes your own hormones, and often your skin with them.

  1. The combined pill contains oestrogen and a progestogen (a synthetic progesterone). The oestrogen makes your liver produce more SHBG, which holds on to more testosterone, and the pill quiets your ovaries. Less free testosterone means less sebum.
  2. Progestogen-only methods, such as the implant, the injection and the hormonal coil, lack that oestrogen. Some progestogens also resemble androgens a little.
  3. When you stop the pill, your SHBG falls again and your ovaries make their own androgens again.

In 31 trials every combined pill reduced acne compared with placebo. With a hormonal coil, 15% of women got acne. Acne after stopping the pill has never been properly studied, but given the steps above it makes sense that your skin can change then. If you want to change your contraception, discuss it with your GP.

Does the oestrogen dominance story hold up?

'Oestrogen dominance' means too much oestrogen relative to progesterone. It's often named as a cause of acne, as is too much or too little aromatase, an enzyme that turns androgens into oestrogens. No research in humans shows that. What is known points the other way. Women with a rare condition in which they make no aromatase are left with more androgens and actually get acne. And oestrogen lowers sebum: with one pill, the drop in sebum came mainly from the oestrogen.

Part 5

Is it what you eat or take?

Insulin, milk, protein shakes and vitamin B12.

What insulin does to your skin

Insulin is a hormone from your pancreas. After eating your blood sugar rises, and insulin works like a key: it opens your cells so the sugar can get in and your blood sugar falls again. In insulin resistance your cells respond less well to that key. Your pancreas then makes more and more insulin to get the same result. So your blood sugar can stay normal for a long time, while your insulin is too high.

  1. High insulin prompts the ovaries to make more testosterone.
  2. High insulin makes your liver produce less SHBG. More free testosterone is then left.
  3. Insulin also raises IGF-1, a growth hormone that makes oil-gland cells produce more fat.
  4. Together that gives more sebum: step 1 of acne.

People with acne more often have insulin resistance: in a review of 13 studies their HOMA-IR (a measure of insulin resistance) was higher, and in men 22% had insulin resistance against 11% without acne. That's a link, not proof it's the cause. A diet low in fast carbohydrates helped in two small studies, but in the larger one participants also lost weight, and according to the Dutch GP guideline no diet is convincingly proven.

Should you stop drinking milk?

Milk contains proteins that raise your insulin and IGF-1. In a study in boys, casein raised IGF-1 by 15%, and whey raised insulin by 21%. Researchers think milk can raise your sebum that way, through the route above.

In a review of 14 studies with 78,529 people, the odds of acne were 25% higher with dairy, and 32% with skimmed milk. These aren't experiments, and the link weakened after taking other factors into account. Yoghurt and cheese showed no link. Lots of fat and sugar was also linked to acne. In a small study, milk chocolate gave more spots within 48 hours than sweets with the same amount of sugar. Want to know if it plays a part for you? Track what you drink next to how your skin looks.

Can B12 cause acne?

You need vitamin B12 for your nerves and your blood. The acne bacterium C. acnes uses B12 too.

  1. Normally the bacterium makes B12 itself.
  2. If you take in a lot of B12, for example through injections, more of it reaches your skin. The bacterium then stops making B12.
  3. Instead the bacterium makes more porphyrins, substances that irritate your skin and fuel inflammation.

In one study, 1 in 10 healthy people got acne within a week of extra B12. In a series of 5 women, the acne came within a week to 5 months of starting B12, and cleared 3 to 6 weeks after stopping. It happens in a small number of people. If you take B12, have your level measured.

Part 6

Is it your products?

Creams, friction, washing and the sun.

Which products clog pores

'Comedogenic' means a product can clog pores and cause blackheads: step 2 of acne. Heavy creams and oils can do that. In the 1970s, mild acne in a third of adult women was linked to cosmetics. Products have changed since. Lists of 'comedogenic' ingredients are unreliable, because a finished product with such an ingredient often doesn't clog. And 'non-comedogenic' isn't a protected term. Hair pomade gave 70% of regular users blackheads on the forehead.

Your phone, your mask and picking

Pressure and friction irritate the follicle and push the plug deeper. This is called acne mechanica, and it comes from things like a face mask, a helmet or your phone against your cheek. People who wore a mask for more than 4 hours a day had skin problems almost twice as often. Picking does the same: it makes the inflammation worse and leaves scars more often.

How often should you wash your face?

Twice a day, gently. In a small study, washing once a day gave more inflamed spots, and more than twice didn't help further. Acne doesn't come from dirty skin: the plug sits under your skin, inside the follicle. Scrubbing actually irritates your skin. My own routine is on my skincare page.

Does the sun help?

No. UV changes sebum so that it clogs pores more. And after a spot a dark mark often stays behind, because your skin makes extra pigment. Sun makes those marks darker, and sunscreen helps prevent them.

Part 7

What else could it be?

Your gut, your liver, medicines and stress.

Does your gut play a part?

Your gut wall is only one cell layer thick. Those cells are held together by tiny junctions. They let nutrients through and keep bacteria and their waste out.

  1. Zonulin is a protein that controls how open those junctions are. More zonulin means they open further: a more permeable, 'leaky' gut.
  2. Pieces of bacteria can then get into your blood.
  3. Your immune system reacts to that, also outside your gut.
  4. That extra inflammation can add to step 4 of acne.

There are signs of this indirect route. In acne, I-FABP was higher - a marker of damage to the gut wall - and the higher it was, the more severe the acne. As early as 1983, blood from people with severe acne reacted more strongly to bacterial toxins. People with acne also have different gut bacteria in small studies, and probiotics gave mixed results. These are small studies showing a link, not yet proof of cause. With zonulin, watch the test: the widely used tests don't measure zonulin itself but other proteins.

Is it a 'clogged liver'?

Two clinics told me I had a 'clogged liver'. My liver values in my blood were normal: GGT 12 and ALT 26, two values your GP often measures.

Your liver clears hormones: it attaches a tag so your body can remove them through bile and urine. This is often called the 'detox phases', and you'll read that a slow liver causes acne. No research in humans links your liver or those phases to acne. DIM and calcium-D-glucarate are in many acne supplements, but have never been studied in acne. Your liver values only matter in acne if you take certain medicines. My hormones page explains how your body clears hormones.

Medicines, stress and smoking

Sometimes the cause is something you take or put on your skin. Stress, sleep and smoking are only weakly linked to acne in studies.

Medicines

Among them corticosteroids, testosterone and anabolic steroids, lithium and some epilepsy drugs. 38% of Dutch anabolic steroid users got acne.

Steroid and lightening creams

Creams with corticosteroids, including some skin-lightening creams, can cause acne.

Stress

In a small study, acne got worse during exams as stress went up. That's a link. Thuisarts doesn't count stress as a cause.

Sleep

Only small studies, showing a link and no proof of cause.

Smoking

Mixed: in a review of 17 studies there was no clear link, a little stronger in adults.

What you can do yourself

Part 8

What can you have measured?

When to have your hormones checked, and which values.

Which blood tests, and when

Have your hormones checked mainly if you have more than acne alone: an irregular cycle, lots of hair growth, hair loss, acne that suddenly starts in adulthood, or acne that comes back quickly after treatment. If you're on the pill, androgen values are only reliable 3 months after stopping. In May 2026 I had testosterone, free testosterone, SHBG and DHEA-S measured, and in September testosterone and SHBG again.

Total and free testosterone

Ideally measured with the most accurate method (LC-MS/MS).

SHBG

The protein that binds testosterone. Low SHBG means more free testosterone.

DHEA-S and androstenedione

If testosterone is normal.

TSH, prolactin, 17-OH-progesterone and FSH

To rule out other causes.

Glucose or HbA1c

For your blood sugar. Fasting insulin and HOMA-IR are advised against in the guideline, because they're inaccurate.

Vitamin B12

If you take or receive B12.

Zinc

In a review of studies, people with acne had lower zinc levels in their blood on average. That's a link. Whether a low level causes acne isn't proven. My zinc was 11 µmol/L (December 2025), just above the limit of 10.7.

Always discuss an abnormal result with your GP.

Part 9

What if it doesn't go away?

What your GP and dermatologist can do.

From mild to severe

Your GP chooses treatment based on how severe your acne is. Each treatment works on a different step of acne.

  1. Mild: a skin treatment, such as benzoyl peroxide, which curbs bacteria, or a retinoid, which opens clogged pores.
  2. Moderate: plus a skin antibiotic, against bacteria and inflammation.
  3. Severe: an antibiotic course of up to 3 months.
  4. After that isotretinoin, through the GP or the dermatologist. It shrinks the oil glands.

The pill is only given if you also want contraception. Spironolactone isn't licensed for acne in the Netherlands. Apart from benzoyl peroxide, all these are prescription treatments: discuss them with your GP.

Never start without data. Don't take supplements for acne without measuring: B12 can actually make acne worse, and DIM has never been studied in acne.

Treatment by the dermatologist

Acne almost never has one cause. Start with your pattern: on which day of your cycle does it come up? Put that next to your blood results and your products, and change one thing at a time. Always discuss an abnormal result with your GP.
