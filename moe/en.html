Waking up tired

Why you wake up tired, which stress you don't feel, and what you can measure.

In June 2026 I felt weak, and I got dizzy when I stood up. In September everything went black once, and I had to sleep for two hours. I thought I was drinking enough. I wasn't: I was eating and drinking too little. Since I started eating more and drinking more, with a little salt, I've had a lot more energy. It still goes up and down.

What almost nobody knows

50%

In the first half hour after waking, your cortisol rises by half

Cortisol is a hormone made by your adrenal glands, two small glands on top of your kidneys. It helps you wake up and keeps your blood sugar steady. In the first 30 to 45 minutes after waking it rises extra: the morning peak. In 509 adults, cortisol rose by about 50% on average in the first half hour. Even in healthy people, about 1 in 4 showed no clear rise. So one measurement tells you little.

4 studies with 509 adults

58

Your adrenals don't run out from stress

Adrenal fatigue is the idea that long-term stress drains your adrenal glands, like a battery. A review of 58 studies found no evidence for it, and the Endocrine Society, an international society of hormone doctors, says it doesn't exist. Your adrenals have no store that can run empty: they make cortisol the moment your brain asks for it. With long-term stress it's mainly that signalling that changes.

Review of 58 studies

1,200 kcal

Eating too little raises your cortisol, even if you don't feel stressed

A kilocalorie (kcal) is the measure of the energy in food. In a study of 121 women, some ate 1,200 kcal a day for 3 weeks. Eating less raised the total amount of cortisol they made across the day. They mainly felt more stressed when they had to count their calories. So your body reads too little fuel as stress, even when you don't notice anything.

Study of 121 women

1.4%

A small fluid deficit already makes you tired and foggy

25 young women lost an average of 1.4% of their body weight in fluid through exercise, sometimes with a water pill. If you weigh 60 kilos, that's about 0.8 litres. They felt more tired, had less energy, found it harder to concentrate and got more headaches. Their thinking tasks mostly didn't get worse.

Study of 25 women, partly by a bottled-water company

12.7%

That's how much my cortisol rose after waking

In May 2025 I did a DUTCH test, a test you do at home with urine and saliva. My cortisol rose only 12.7% after waking, where the lab calls 50 to 160% normal. That says less than it seems. In one study, 26% of people took at least one sample at the wrong time, and their cortisol barely rose after waking. A reliable picture takes 2 to 6 days, and I measured one. The DUTCH test has also only been studied by its own maker.

My mornings

What I had tested, and what I do differently now.

Nobody could help me

I struggled to get out of bed, fell asleep easily and had brain fog: a cloudy head where it's hard to focus. I'd had all sorts of things tested for years, for more than €10,000: my blood, my DNA, my gut and my hormones. I went to hospital and to clinics, including in the United States. Nobody could help me. Two clinics said my liver was 'congested', while two liver markers in my blood, GGT and ALT, were simply normal.

DUTCH is a test you do at home: the lab measures your hormones in dried urine, and your cortisol across the day in saliva. My morning peak was almost gone. That's one day, a late first sample makes the peak look smaller, and the test has only been studied by its own maker.

I was eating too little and drinking too little. To your body, eating too little is stress, even when you don't feel stressed. Now I eat more, do strength training and drink more water, with a little salt. Since I've been drinking more, I have a lot more energy. It still goes up and down.

Into the sun in the morning

I move straight after getting up. In a small study, young adults who were often tired got more energy from 6 weeks of light or moderate exercise.

Liquorice root

In the morning I take a liquorice root supplement. Liquorice root contains glycyrrhizin, a substance that makes cortisol work for longer. There's no controlled research on fatigue. I track my sleep with an Oura ring. From 24 July to 24 October 2026, over 83 nights, I slept 7 hours 45 minutes on average. Even so, in September everything went black once.

Careful. Extra salt isn't right for everyone. GPs don't advise extra salt for ordinary low blood pressure, and certainly not with high blood pressure. Liquorice root raises your blood pressure too. Have your blood pressure measured first and talk to a doctor.

Tired, but you don't feel stressed?

Your stress system also reacts to too little food, fluid, sleep and recovery.

Which stress don't you feel?

To your body, stress is anything that throws you off balance. That's pressure in your head, and also too little food, too little sleep or hard training. Your stress system, the teamwork between your brain and your adrenal glands, responds with cortisol, even when you feel fine yourself.

Think of the past two weeks and choose no, sometimes or often for each question. Each answer counts towards one or two areas.

  1. You skip breakfast or lunch because you're not hungry or have no time.

  2. Only in the afternoon do you notice you've barely drunk anything.

  3. When you stand up quickly, you get dizzy or everything goes black for a moment.

  4. On days you drink little, you get a headache or a cloudy head.

  5. You sleep less than 7 hours a night.

  6. You go to bed at a different time every night.

  7. You only get outside late in the morning, or hardly at all.

  8. You train on days when you've eaten little.

  9. Your period has become irregular or stopped since you started training more or eating less.

  10. You often train so hard that you can barely talk.

Five kinds of stress you don't notice

Your brain keeps track of whether you're getting enough fuel, sleep and rest. It reads a shortage as stress, even when you feel fine.

  1. If you eat too little, or burn far more through training than you eat, your brain reads that as a shortage.
  2. It then signals your adrenal glands, just as it does with tension. You don't have to notice anything yourself.
  3. Cortisol frees up energy: it makes your liver produce sugar from your stores, so your brain and muscles keep enough fuel.
  4. RED-S stands for relative energy deficiency in sport: you eat too little for what you burn through training. Your body then saves on other tasks, such as your cycle and your recovery.
  5. With illness or inflammation your immune system makes signalling substances that also switch on your stress system. Cortisol then dampens the inflammation again.

In 121 women on 1,200 kcal a day, total cortisol rose within 3 weeks. They mainly felt more stressed from counting calories.

Where does brain fog come from?

Brain fog is a catch-all for a cloudy head: you're forgetful and find it hard to focus. It isn't a medical term. It often comes with waking up tired, and hangs on the same shortages.

  1. Your brain has almost no stores of its own. It only works well with a steady supply of blood, oxygen and fuel.
  2. When you stand up or stand for a long time, blood sinks to your legs. Your body then has to work harder to keep enough blood in your head. When you're short of fluid, there's less blood in circulation, and that works less well.
  3. Ferritin is your iron store. You need iron for haemoglobin, the protein in your red blood cells that carries oxygen. Your brain also uses iron itself, for example to make signalling substances.

In a review of 70 studies, short-term sleep loss mostly hit attention and reaction speed. A small fluid deficit made young women less able to concentrate. In women aged 18 to 35, rising ferritin went with clearly better scores on thinking tasks. And young people with POTS, a condition where your heart beats too fast when you stand, themselves named fatigue, too little sleep, standing for long and dehydration as the main triggers for their brain fog.

Part 3

Why do you wake up tired?

What cortisol should do, and what changes when stress goes on for a long time.

What cortisol does across the day

Cortisol is a hormone: a substance that carries a message through your body in your blood. Your adrenal glands make it. Cortisol helps you wake up, keeps your blood sugar steady and dampens inflammation. It should be high in the morning and low in the evening.

  1. Your brain gives the go-ahead. It sends a signal to your pituitary, a small gland just below your brain.
  2. Your pituitary sends a message through your blood to your adrenal glands, which then make cortisol. Together this is your stress system.
  3. Cortisol then slows your brain and pituitary down again. This is called feedback. It works like a thermostat: once there's enough cortisol, the signal gets weaker.
  4. A clock in your brain sets how hard this system works: your daily rhythm. Towards morning it turns up, so you wake up with energy. Just after waking an extra wave comes on top. That's the morning peak.
  5. After that cortisol falls all day, until it's lowest around midnight. That lets you wind down and sleep in the evening.

When stress goes on for months

Short-term stress is useful: your stress system helps you deal with it. With stress that lasts for weeks or months, things change.

  1. With stress your brain gives your adrenal glands an extra signal. You make more cortisol, which gives you energy and focus.
  2. Once the stress is over, feedback brings cortisol back down to your normal rhythm.
  3. If the stress goes on, the system stays switched on for longer. Over time it adapts, and your cortisol can actually fall.
  4. Your curve then gets flatter: less difference between a high morning and a low evening.

With stress that lasts, cortisol rises first. The longer the stress lasts, the lower your morning value and the flatter your daily curve. That's what a review of 107 studies found, though the studies varied a lot. In a review of 80 studies, a flatter curve went with poorer health, including fatigue; whether that's cause or effect isn't known yet. In a large English study, mostly of older men, people with the lowest morning cortisol were one and a half times as likely to become tired later.

Your adrenals don't run out

Adrenal fatigue is the idea that long-term stress makes your adrenal glands run out, like a battery. They would then make too little cortisol, and that would make you tired.

  1. Your adrenal glands don't have a store of cortisol that can run empty. They make it when your brain asks for it.
  2. With long-term stress it's mainly the control that changes: how strongly your brain signals your adrenal glands, and how they respond to feedback.
  3. That can change your daily curve while your adrenal glands still work normally. Truly too little cortisol is a rare disease, adrenal insufficiency. You'll find more on it further down.

According to the Endocrine Society, an international society of hormone doctors, adrenal fatigue doesn't exist. A systematic review of 58 studies found no evidence for it either. If you're given that label anyway, ask which test showed it.

What sets your clock

Your daily rhythm is your internal clock: a rhythm of about 24 hours that sits in your brain. It sets when you feel awake and sleepy, and when your cortisol rises and falls.

  1. On its own your clock doesn't run at exactly 24 hours. So it has to reset itself a little every day.
  2. Light is the strongest signal for that. Light that reaches your eyes in the morning tells your clock the day has started.
  3. Regular times for sleeping, eating and moving also help your clock stay on time.

Your cortisol rhythm follows your day. When 10 people slept and ate 12 hours out of step, their cortisol rhythm fully reversed. In 8 men who stayed awake all night, bright light at 5 am gave an immediate rise in cortisol of more than 50%; bright light in the afternoon did nothing. In over 400,000 people, each extra hour outdoors went with getting up more easily and feeling tired less often. And young adults who were often tired got more energy from 6 weeks of light or moderate exercise. No study shows that a flat curve 'recovers' this way.

Part 4

What can you measure?

What a cortisol test, your wearable and your blood do and don't show, and when to see your GP.

What a cortisol test does and doesn't tell you

You can measure cortisol in blood, saliva or urine. In your blood most cortisol is bound to a protein that carries it. Only the loose, free part really does anything, and that's the part you also measure in saliva and urine.

  1. You take a sample at set times: on waking, half an hour and an hour later, and through the rest of the day.
  2. The lab measures the cortisol in each sample. Together they give your curve: how high your morning peak is and how fast cortisol falls after it.
  3. The morning peak is short. If you take the first sample too late, your peak looks smaller than it is.
  4. Your curve also differs from day to day, depending on how you slept and what's going on that day.

One morning blood test says little about your daily curve. It mainly helps rule out a real adrenal condition, and even then an extra test is needed. Saliva across the day shows the curve, but only if you sample exactly on time: 26% didn't, and in them the morning peak almost vanished from the measurement. One day says little; a reliable picture takes 2 to 6 days. The DUTCH urine test has only been studied by its own maker. If you're on the pill, your blood cortisol is higher because of a protein that binds cortisol.

What your wearable shows

A wearable is a ring, watch or band you wear day and night. It measures your heart rate, and gets two values from it. It doesn't measure cortisol. Your resting heart rate is how many times your heart beats per minute when you're fully at rest, such as in your sleep. HRV (heart rate variability) is how much the time between two heartbeats varies.

  1. A small light on the underside shines into your skin. With every heartbeat more blood flows through your small blood vessels, and a sensor sees that in the light. That's how it counts your heart rate.
  2. Your heart doesn't beat like a metronome. At rest the time between beats varies more, and your HRV is higher. With stress or illness your HRV is often lower and your resting heart rate higher.
  3. It estimates your sleep from how still you lie and how your heart rate changes. It doesn't measure your brain, as a sleep lab does.

Look wider than cortisol

Waking up tired often has another cause. A few standard blood tests let you look wider.

  1. Haemoglobin carries oxygen in your blood. You make it with iron from your store (ferritin). When that store runs out, you make less haemoglobin. That's called anaemia: your muscles and brain get less oxygen.
  2. TSH is a signal from your pituitary that switches on your thyroid. Your thyroid sets the pace of your metabolism: how fast your body uses energy. If it works too slowly, your pituitary sends more TSH, and you often feel tired.
  3. In coeliac disease your immune system reacts to gluten, a protein in wheat, rye and barley. That damages the lining of your small intestine, so you absorb less iron and fewer vitamins. The blood test measures antibodies (IgA) your immune system makes in the process. Total IgA is measured alongside, because if you naturally make little IgA, the test can come out normal when it isn't.
  4. HbA1c shows your average blood sugar over the past 2 to 3 months. CRP is a protein your liver makes when there's inflammation; ESR measures inflammation in a different way.

What you can do yourself, and when to see your GP

When should you see your GP?

In adrenal insufficiency your body really makes too little cortisol. It's a real disease, and not the same as the idea of adrenal fatigue.

  1. In the best-known form, Addison's disease, your own immune system attacks your adrenal glands. They get damaged and can no longer make enough cortisol, however hard your brain signals.
  2. Your adrenal glands then often also make too little aldosterone, a hormone that holds on to salt and fluid in your body. You lose salt and fluid, your blood pressure drops and you get dizzy when you stand up.
  3. It can also follow long-term use of prednisone or similar medicines. These act like cortisol and, through feedback, slow down your own production. When you stop, it doesn't always come back straight away. So never stop them suddenly on your own.

The form that starts in the adrenal glands themselves is rare: about 100 to 140 per million people. It's often recognised late. Symptoms are fatigue, weight loss, dizziness on standing, low blood pressure, poor appetite and stomach pain. If you have these together, see your GP. An ordinary blood test isn't enough: a special test is needed. In it you get an injection of a man-made version of the pituitary's signal, and the lab measures whether your adrenal glands make enough cortisol in response.

Part 5

What does your body need first?

First enough food and fluid, and topping up what you're really short of. Only then supplements.

Building blocks first

By building blocks we mean what your body needs every day to work well: enough food, carbohydrates and fluid, and vitamins and minerals such as iron, vitamin D and B12. Your body reacts to shortages with stress. So top up the building blocks first, and measure what you're missing.

  1. Food is your fuel. Your liver stores carbohydrates as a small sugar reserve, for between meals and overnight.
  2. When that reserve runs low, cortisol helps make new sugar, partly from protein. If you keep eating too little, that system has to step in more often.
  3. Fluid keeps your blood volume up, so your blood pressure stays steady, even when you stand up.
  4. Your body uses vitamins and minerals as parts: iron to carry oxygen, B12 for your blood and nerves, and vitamin D for your bones and muscles, among other things. Taking extra only makes sense if you're really short.

Eating enough

Strict dieting raised cortisol: in 121 women on 1,200 kcal a day within 3 weeks.

Enough carbohydrates

On a very low-carb diet, urine cortisol was higher, 71 vs 50 µg a day, in a small study.

Drinking enough

A small fluid deficit made young women more tired and gave more headaches.

And supplements?

A supplement is a pill, powder or drink containing a nutrient or a plant extract. Once you know what you're missing, you can look further.

  1. Magnesium is mostly in your bones and inside your cells. Only a very small part is in your blood. That's why a blood value says little about your stores.
  2. You need vitamin B6 for your nerves, but too much actually damages them. You feel that as tingling or numbness in your hands and feet.
  3. Liquorice root contains glycyrrhizin, the substance that makes liquorice sweet. In your kidneys an enzyme normally turns cortisol into cortisone, a form that does almost nothing. Glycyrrhizin blocks that enzyme.
  4. Cortisol then keeps working for longer, and in your kidneys it does the same as aldosterone: you hold on to salt and fluid and lose potassium. That raises your blood pressure.

Magnesium

Your blood magnesium says little. Research on magnesium and stress is of low quality.

Vitamin B6

Too much damages nerves. In the Netherlands a supplement may contain at most 21 mg a day.

Liquorice root

Prolongs the action of cortisol in your body, which is why it's recommended online 'for your adrenals'. There's no controlled research on fatigue or low blood pressure. It does raise blood pressure and lower potassium, already at 100 mg glycyrrhizin a day. Not with water pills, blood pressure medicines, prednisone or laxatives, not in pregnancy, and not without a doctor. In DGL the active substance has been removed.

Never start without data. A supplement for the wrong cause doesn't help, and can muddy your symptoms or your test results. Test your markers first and talk to a doctor, especially if you take medication or the pill, or want to conceive.
