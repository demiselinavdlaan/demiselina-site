Waking up tired

What cortisol does, where brain fog comes from, which stress you don't feel, and what you can measure.

Every morning I struggled to get going, felt weak, got dizzy when I stood up and had brain fog. I was eating and drinking too little - stress I didn't feel, but my body did. On this page you'll see how cortisol should move over the day, which kinds of stress you don't feel, and what you can measure.

Part 1

Cortisol across the day

How it should run, and what changes after long-term stress.

How cortisol should run

Cortisol helps you wake up, keeps your blood sugar steady and dampens inflammation. It should be high in the morning and low in the evening. Tap a part of the day.

First high, then low

With stress that lasts, cortisol rises first. The longer the stress lasts, the lower your morning value and the flatter your daily curve. That's what a review of 107 studies found, though the differences were small and the studies varied a lot. A flatter curve went with more fatigue in 80 studies; whether that's cause or effect isn't known yet. In a large English study, mostly of older men, people with the lowest morning cortisol were one and a half times as likely to become tired later.

Your adrenals don't run out

Online this is often called adrenal fatigue. According to the Endocrine Society that condition doesn't exist, and a systematic review of 58 studies found no evidence for it. Your adrenals don't run out from stress. What changes is how strongly your brain signals them and how they respond to feedback.

Part 2

Stress you don't feel

Your stress system also reacts to shortages, not just to pressure.

More than pressure in your head

You can feel fine while your body is under stress. These shortages raise cortisol, even when you don't feel stressed:

Eating too little

In 121 women on 1,200 kcal a day, total cortisol rose within 3 weeks, mostly in the evening. They didn't feel more stressed.

Too little energy for lots of training

Female athletes without periods had higher daytime cortisol. It's part of RED-S, where your cycle and recovery are thrown off too.

Too little sleep

After one short night, evening cortisol was 37 to 45% higher. After six nights of 4 hours, it fell six times more slowly in the evening.

Hard training

30 minutes at 80% of your maximum gave 83% more cortisol. At 40% there was no clear effect.

Illness or inflammation

Your stress system also reacts to inflammation in your body.

Low calorie dieting increases cortisol.

And brain fog?

Brain fog isn't a medical term but a catch-all: forgetful, a cloudy head and trouble focusing. It often comes with waking up tired, and hangs on the same shortages. In a review of 70 studies, short-term sleep loss mostly hit attention and reaction speed. A small fluid deficit made young women less able to concentrate. And in women aged 18 to 35, rising ferritin went with clearly better scores on thinking tasks. Young people with brain fog themselves named fatigue, too little sleep, standing for long and dehydration as triggers most often.

How it went for me

I felt weak and got dizzy when I stood up, even though I thought I was drinking enough. I struggled to get out of bed, fell asleep easily and had brain fog. Once everything went black and I had to sleep for two hours. My DUTCH test showed my morning peak was almost missing: 13%, where 50 to 160% is normal. Two clinics looked at my liver, but my blood results showed it was doing fine. When I put my data side by side, it was simpler: I was eating too little and drinking too little. Eating more, strength training and drinking more, with a little salt, made the difference. Since I've been drinking more, I have a lot more energy. I'm not there yet, it goes up and down. But I now know what I'm looking at.

My story, not advice. Extra salt isn't right for everyone. For ordinary low blood pressure GPs advise against extra salt, and certainly not with high blood pressure. Get tested first and talk to a doctor.

What helps your rhythm

Your cortisol rhythm follows your day. When people slept and ate 12 hours out of step, their rhythm reversed. Bright light early in the morning gave a bigger morning peak. Being outdoors helps too: in 400,000 people, each extra hour outside went with getting up more easily and feeling tired less often. And regular exercise, even light, gave more energy. I go outside and move straight after getting up myself. No study shows that a flat curve 'recovers' this way.

Part 3

Measuring

What a cortisol test does and doesn't tell you, and what else you can measure.

What a cortisol test does and doesn't tell you

One morning blood test says little about your daily curve. It mainly helps rule out a real adrenal condition, and even then an extra test is needed. Saliva across the day shows the curve, but only if you sample exactly on time: 26% didn't, and then the morning peak disappeared from the measurement. One day says little; a reliable picture takes 2 to 6 days. The DUTCH urine test has only been studied by its own maker. If you're on the pill, your blood cortisol is higher because of a protein that binds cortisol.

Look wider than cortisol

Waking up tired often has another cause. At the GP only about 8% got a clear physical explanation, and low mood was found most often. These blood tests let you look wider:

Your thyroid.

Part 4

When to see your GP

A real adrenal condition is rare, but it does exist.

Adrenal insufficiency

In adrenal insufficiency your body really makes too little cortisol. It's rare, about 100 to 140 per million people, and often recognised late. Symptoms are fatigue, weight loss, dizziness on standing, low blood pressure, poor appetite and stomach pain. If you have these together, see your GP. An ordinary blood test isn't enough: a special test is needed.

Part 5

Building blocks first

Top up what you're short of first. Only then look at supplements.

The building blocks

Your body reacts to shortages with stress. So top up the building blocks first, and measure what you're missing:

Eating enough

Strict dieting raised cortisol: in 121 women on 1,200 kcal a day within 3 weeks.

Enough carbohydrates

On a very low-carb diet, urine cortisol was higher, 71 vs 50 µg a day, in a small study.

Drinking enough

A small fluid deficit made young women more tired and gave more headaches.

Iron

Iron only helped fatigue with ferritin of 50 or lower. Too much iron is harmful.

Vitamin D

Topping up only reduced fatigue when deficient. Fatigue alone is no reason to test.

Vitamin B12

Only useful if you're deficient.

Only then: supplements

Once you know what you're missing, you can look further. This is where a few well-known supplements stand:

Magnesium

Your blood magnesium says little. Research on magnesium and stress is of low quality.

Vitamin B6

Too much damages nerves. In the Netherlands a supplement may contain at most 21 mg a day.

Liquorice root

Prolongs the action of cortisol in your body, which is why it's recommended online 'for your adrenals'. There's no controlled research on fatigue or low blood pressure. It does raise blood pressure and lower potassium, already at 100 mg glycyrrhizin a day. Not with water pills, blood pressure medicines, prednisone or laxatives, not in pregnancy, and not without a doctor. In DGL the active substance has been removed.

Never start without data. A supplement for the wrong cause doesn't help, and can muddy your symptoms or your test results. Test your markers first and talk to a doctor, especially if you take medication or the pill, or want to conceive.
